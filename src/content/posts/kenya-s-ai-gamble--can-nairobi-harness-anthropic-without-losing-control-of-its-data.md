NAIROBI'S AI GAMBLE: WILL ANTHROPIC REALLY LET US CONTROL THE DATA, OR ARE WE JUST SIGNING UP FOR MORE FOREIGN TECH SHENANIGANS?

Kenya thinks it's playing smart by diving headfirst into Artificial Intelligence, signing a shiny new deal with US company Anthropic. The government's all chirpy about AI in health, education, and public services. But let's be real, the real test isn't the ink on the paper signed in New York, it's whether we'll end up owning the systems or just being the grateful recipients of someone else's digital leftovers. History, especially here in Nairobi, has a nasty habit of repeating itself when it comes to grand promises and actual control.

The government's already barking about data governance being a non-negotiable red line, insisting that Kenyan law will reign supreme. Good. Because if there's one thing we've learned from watching our own infrastructure crumble faster than a poorly built kiosk during the rainy season, it's that external promises rarely translate to local resilience. [Nairobi Braces for Deluge, Infrastructure Fails Again As Officials Dither](/posts/nairobi-braces-for-deluge--infrastructure-fails-again-as-officials-dither), remember that? This AI data issue feels uncomfortably familiar.

They're also talking a big game about wanting a say in how these AI systems are developed and evaluated, pushing for African capacity and systems that actually *get* Kenya - our languages, our priorities, our messy social context. That's a noble thought, but let's not get ahead of ourselves. Building local expertise to scrutinize and adapt foreign tech is a monumental task. It's easy to sign declarations, much harder to build the brains and the infrastructure to back them up.

The real question is whether this "cooperation framework" is a genuine partnership or just another way for foreign tech giants to gain a foothold, collecting our data under the guise of collaboration. We're moving from attracting global tech to needing domestic capacity to *control* it. That's a jump, not a step. And judging by past experiences, the leap might be bigger than we think.

So, while the Principal Secretary and Ambassador are busy with their "technology diplomacy strategy," let's see what actually comes out of this. Will we have researchers who can genuinely challenge Anthropic's findings? Will public services actually improve in a way that benefits *us*, or will we just be paying more for sophisticated foreign software? The ultimate measure isn't the number of partnerships, but whether Kenya emerges with actual power over its digital future, not just a contract with a fancy name.
