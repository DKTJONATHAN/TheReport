Ruto at 4: The Debt Question: What Did Kenya Borrow and Where Did the Money Really Go?

This is not good. The numbers are out, and Kenya's public debt has ballooned to Sh13 trillion under Ruto's four years. Servicing these loans is now one of the biggest drains on government revenue. So, what's the catch? We borrowed a mountain of cash, but the real question is what we got for it, and if any of it actually makes us richer, or just richer in debt.

It's a classic Kenyan story, isn't it? We borrowed Sh4.25 trillion in just four years. The Treasury tells us it's not *just* new borrowing, but also exchange rates and all sorts of financial wizardry. But let's be honest, most of it is just new debt. They're spending billions on development, sure - roads, water, housing - the usual suspects. But then they admit that a good chunk of it, and this is the kicker, is going to pay for the government's day-to-day running. Remember when Ruto promised we wouldn't borrow for recurrent expenditure? Yeah, that went out the window faster than a handshake.

The latest figures show we're increasingly borrowing just to keep the lights on. In 2023/24, over half of the borrowing went to recurrent costs. In 2024/25, it was Sh250.4 billion for the same thing. The Public Finance Management Act is apparently just a suggestion. They talk about "fiscal consolidation" as if it's some magic wand, but the reality is, our tax money is increasingly going to pay off old debts. It makes you wonder if our leaders are more concerned with fancy rides and fancy meals than the actual future, much like we saw with DP Kindiki's demands. [DP Kindiki Demands Sh450m More for Fancy Rides and Fancy Meals, While the Rest of Us Scrape By](/posts/dp-kindiki-demands-sh450m-more-for-fancy-rides-and-fancy-meals--while-the-rest-of-us-scrape-by).

And the price of all this borrowing? It's not just the Sh1.72 trillion we paid just in debt servicing in 2024/25. Kenya is still seen as a risky borrower by international markets, meaning we pay more to borrow. Even a slight dip in the shilling, as the Treasury CS warned, could send debt servicing costs soaring. They're trying to get creative, talking about debt-for-food swaps and other financial gymnastics, but the PS himself admits it: borrowing is not sustainable.

So, what's the verdict after four years? We've got a bigger debt pile, a heavier repayment bill, and a questionable return on investment. Some borrowing went to actual projects, yes, but a significant chunk went to plug holes in the budget, refinance old debts, and, alarmingly, fund day-to-day operations. The Treasury’s own report calls our debt "sustainable but at high risk of debt distress." That's a polite way of saying we're on thin ice.

The real test for the rest of Ruto's term isn't about *if* we can borrow more, but *if* that borrowing actually creates enough economic activity to pay itself back without crippling essential services. The question isn't just how much we owe, but whether the future taxpayers will ever see the benefit of this mountain of debt, or just be left with the bill.
