Ruto at 4: The Debt Question - What Did Kenya Borrow and Where Did All That Money Go?

Nairobi's debt mountain has hit Sh13 trillion under Ruto, and servicing it is eating government revenue alive. Don't expect a miracle, just more borrowing.

The Sh13 trillion debt is a stark reminder that promises of fiscal discipline often evaporate faster than morning mist in Nairobi.

The numbers don't lie, do they? Four years in, and President Ruto's administration has managed to balloon Kenya's public debt to a staggering Sh13.013 trillion. That’s an extra Sh4.25 trillion on our plates since he swaggered into office in September 2022. And before you get all excited about new roads and fancy housing, let's be clear: not every shilling borrowed is directly tied to a shiny project. A good chunk of this cash disappears into the black hole of general budget financing, and increasingly, into covering the government's day-to-day expenses, a move President Ruto himself once condemned as “simply wrong.” It seems promises, like economic forecasts, are made to be broken.

And the promises? Oh, they're a dime a dozen. Ruto swore off borrowing for recurrent expenditure, remember? His State of the Nation address in 2022 was practically a sermon on fiscal responsibility. Yet, here we are. The latest Treasury figures show that in 2023/24, more than half of the Sh766.4 billion borrowed went straight into recurrent spending. Then again in 2024/25, a cool Sh250.4 billion of the Sh854.5 billion borrowed was swallowed by the same gaping maw of recurrent bills. The Public Finance Management Act’s principle of borrowing for development? Apparently, it’s more of a suggestion than a rule these days. It's starting to feel like [DEBT FOR BREAKFAST: THE GOVERNMENT’S PLAN TO BORROW US INTO OBLIVION](/posts/debt-for-breakfast--the-government-s-plan-to-borrow-us-into-oblivion) is the real agenda.

Now, they'll tell you about "fiscal consolidation" and how the share of borrowing for development is supposedly rising. But let’s talk about the real pain: debt servicing. For the 2024/25 financial year alone, Kenya coughed up Sh1.72 trillion just to pay principal and interest. And the projections for 2025/26? A cool Sh1.9 trillion. That's money raised from your taxes, hard-earned shillings, being fed directly into the pockets of lenders, often to repay debts incurred by *previous* governments. This isn't rocket science; it's a debt trap, plain and simple, and it’s why international credit rating agencies keep us firmly in the "high-risk borrower" category, despite a recent minor upgrade from Moody's.

The constant threat of a weaker shilling only adds insult to injury. Treasury Cabinet Secretary John Mbadi himself warned that a depreciating shilling could push debt servicing costs from Sh1.5 trillion to an even higher figure. For the 2026/27 financial year, a whopping Sh1.5 trillion is already earmarked for debt repayment *before* any money is allocated to crucial sectors like health, education, or security. This isn't some abstract Treasury statistic anymore; it directly impacts the money available for everything that matters to us, the common mwananchi. The government might be exploring fancy financial instruments like debt-for-food swaps, but let's face it, they're just trying to plug holes in a sinking ship.

Ultimately, the real test for this administration isn't about whether Kenya can *continue* borrowing, but whether every new shilling borrowed can actually generate enough economic activity, jobs, and revenue to repay the loans without crippling the services we rely on. The Treasury’s own strategy document admits our debt is "sustainable but at high risk of debt distress." That’s polite talk for "we're in deep trouble." The question for future taxpayers isn't just about what we borrowed, but whether the supposed assets and opportunities created by that debt will ever be worth the crippling bill they'll inevitably have to pay.
