MP Lesuuda's Grand Road Redesign for Pedestrians and Cyclists: Another Empty Promise?

Nairobi's roads are a death trap, and MP Naisula Lesuuda is suddenly noticing it. After years of cyclists screaming blue murder, a bill has finally crawled to the public participation stage. It's meant to give "vulnerable" road users like cyclists and pedestrians more protection. But let's be real, in this city, "protection" usually means more rules that no one follows and infrastructure that crumbles before it's even finished. This is the kind of political theatre that keeps us all waiting for change that never quite arrives.

Lesuuda claims her epiphany came in 2019 after "listening to cyclists." Apparently, seeing two kids on a bicycle along a busy road was the catalyst. "Our children deserve roads that protect them, not roads they have to survive," she pontificated. Noble words, but as we've seen with countless projects before, good intentions often get lost in the Kenyan bureaucracy and corruption. We've heard these kinds of promises before, haven't we? It’s like watching the [Saudi Golf Experiment Hits the Debt Wall](/posts/the-saudi-golf-experiment-hits-the-debt-wall) - a lot of fanfare, a lot of talk, and then... crickets.

The Kenya Roads (Amendment) Bill 2024 proposes dedicated cycling lanes, pedestrian walkways, and crossings. Fancy. It even talks about catering for those using wheelchairs. It sounds great on paper, a utopian vision of safe, green transport. But the reality is, our roads are already a mess. Building new infrastructure is one thing; maintaining it, and ensuring drivers actually respect it, is another entirely. This is Nairobi, not some sanitized European city. Drivers here treat lane markings as suggestions and pedestrians as moving obstacles.

The Senate committee is now asking for public input, with submissions due by October 1st, 2026, and a hearing on the same day. So, if you’re one of those brave souls who cycles or walks to work, get your complaints in now. This is your chance to shout into the void. Whether these dedicated facilities actually become part of how our roads are planned and built, or if it all just becomes another forgotten piece of paper, remains to be seen. Lesuuda says the journey "started in 2019" and "there is still work to do." Well, tell us something we don't know.

This bill is just another step, a baby step at that, in a long, arduous journey. We've seen grand plans before, like the ambitious [Great Green Wall: How CBAM Decelerated Global Decarbonization](/posts/the-great-green-wall-of-bureaucracy-policy-failure), which promised so much and delivered so little. Until I see concrete, well-maintained lanes and a significant shift in driver behavior, I’ll remain skeptical. Until then, keep your head on a swivel.
