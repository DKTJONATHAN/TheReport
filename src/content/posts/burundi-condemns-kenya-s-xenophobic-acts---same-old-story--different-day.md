It's a familiar script playing out again in Nairobi, only this time, the victims are our East African neighbours. President Ruto's directive to chase out "undocumented foreign traders" has predictably spiraled into a messy crackdown, leaving Burundians, Ugandans, and Rwandans scrambling for safety and basic dignity. You'd think our leaders would learn, but apparently, political posturing trumps regional stability every single time. Hundreds queuing at embassies, their meager belongings guarded, is a stark image of our famed "East African Community."

Burundi's Foreign Minister Édouard Bizimana didn't mince words, rightly calling out Kenya's actions and the implied accusation of being a "failed state" for letting civilians do the police's dirty work. He's not wrong. When the government's message is "get out," it's not a leap for some citizens to take matters into their own hands, leading to the kind of violence and xenophobia we've seen before. It seems the peace Kenyans enjoy in Burundi is a one-way street when it comes to trade opportunities back home.

Then comes the inevitable damage control, a hollow apology from Principal Secretary Korir Sing'oei, claiming the message was "misunderstood." Misunderstood? Or perfectly understood by those looking for an excuse to harass and loot? It's always easier to blame misinterpretation than to admit a directive was poorly conceived or, worse, deliberately inflammatory. The sincerity of sorrow for "problems that have befallen you" rings particularly flat when it's the government's own actions that caused them.

Meanwhile, the East Africa Law Society and former politicians like Kabando wa Kabando are left to preach about the rule of law and the EAC Treaty, reminding us that Kenya isn't an island. We talk about empowering small businesses and closing the [SME Financing Gap](/posts/woccu-and-wfcu-partner-with-kenyan-saccos-to-help-close-the-sme-financing-gap) one day, and the next we're chasing our neighbors out of their stalls. The hypocrisy is truly breathtaking, especially when you consider how many Kenyans thrive on similar opportunities in those very same neighbouring countries, and beyond.

But who's really listening? When economic times are tough, it's always easier to point fingers at the "foreigner" than to address the systemic issues. This isn't about legalities; it's about scapegoating and a convenient distraction. The "East African Community" is just a fancy name until it's time to actually act like one. For now, it's just another cycle of condemnation, apologies, and the same old xenophobic acts.
