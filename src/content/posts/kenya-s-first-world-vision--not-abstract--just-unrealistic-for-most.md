So, State House had another one of those "historic" moments last Tuesday. President Ruto, apparently, received a report from Professor Nyong'o's team, a "Developing a New Vision for Kenya: Towards a First World Nation" masterpiece. The writer claims you could "sense a quiet but powerful shift." Yeah, right. More like the usual government theatre, a country taking a deep breath before diving back into the same old mess. A "long, demanding climb" sounds about as realistic as a politician keeping a promise.

Prof. Nyong'o, bless his seasoned planner heart, spoke with the gravitas of someone who's seen Kenya rise, stumble, and then trip over its own feet. And the President, listening with all seriousness, knows the next 30 years will "define Kenya's place in the world." Oh, the grand pronouncements! It's all about the everyday frustrations, they say. The mother in Kayole, the farmer in Mwea, the jobless graduate in Eldoret, the boda-boda guy in Kisumu. These are the people who keep the wheels turning while the big shots talk about "First World Nation" dreams. It’s a nice thought, but let's be honest, these are the same people who have been hoping for better for decades, and we're still here.

The report itself admits, "Kenya gets development right, but not long enough to sustain it." Zing! That one stings because it’s the gospel truth. We've had our moments of brilliance, sure. But then comes the political transitions, the shaky institutions, the endless governance gaps. It’s the Kenyan cycle of brief sparks followed by long, damp squibs. Last Tuesday's ceremony? Just another reminder that we're supposed to "break that cycle." As if. Look at the Sh5.2 Billion manual payroll scandal laid bare in counties - that’s the kind of "institutional fragility" we’re talking about, not exactly a sign of breaking cycles.

They’re throwing around examples like Singapore and South Korea, talking about affordable housing and social savings reforms. And yes, healthcare reform is trying to "strengthen human capital." But are these reforms perfect? Of course not. They cause debate, discomfort, and resistance. Because, in Kenya, anything that actually *works* tends to get complicated fast. Vision 2030 gave us roads and ports, but the report rightly warns these gains can be lost. We need strong laws and institutions, not just another report gathering dust.

The report’s "most compelling message" is about people, about uniting the nation. It can't just be a Nairobi story; it needs to be a Turkana, Kwale, Nyeri, and Kisii story. It needs to be felt everywhere. And the justice system? Apparently, it needs to become an "accelerator of development." Justice delayed is development denied, they say. Well, in Kenya, justice often seems to be on permanent sabbatical. While we're talking about speeding things up, maybe they can start by sorting out the endless backlog of cases that clog up the courts, just like [the endless arguments and chaos that plague public life](/posts/clout--chaos--and-concaves--why-the-carter-efe-vs-portable-fight-is-just-a-glorified-circus).

So, last Tuesday's ceremony was more than a handover. It was a reminder that nations "prosper when they choose discipline over drama, continuity over chaos and unity over fragmentation." A lovely sentiment. But let's not forget, Kenya has overcome challenges before. The question is, will it learn from them? If we maintain stability, strengthen institutions, invest in people, and keep our eyes on the horizon, then maybe, just maybe, becoming a "First World nation by 2060" is within reach. Or, more likely, it’s just another lofty goal to be discussed over expensive lunches.
