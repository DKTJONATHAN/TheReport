Ruto: Foreigners Welcome, But Don't Step On Kenyan Toes, Or Else.

President William Ruto, perhaps realizing his tough talk on foreign small traders was stirring up more trouble than it was worth, has done a swift about-face. Now, he's all about open arms, provided everyone plays by his government's "rules." Basically, it's a "come one, come all, but don't get any funny ideas" kind of welcome.

The crackdown on hawkers and petty traders from abroad, which he so enthusiastically ordered just days ago, seems to have been conveniently forgotten, or at least repackaged. Ruto now insists Kenya is open to investors and workers from across Africa and beyond, a stark contrast to his previous pronouncements about protecting Kenyan businesses. He's even gone so far as to preach about regional integration and fairness, as if some Kenyans haven't already shown their "DNA" for taking the law into their own hands, especially when it comes to competition.

His pronouncements in Bomet sound more like damage control than genuine policy shifts. The government is now busy with a "regularisation exercise," a fancy term for trying to clean up the mess created by the initial aggressive stance. This comes after whispers of harassment and genuine fears of xenophobia, which, let's be honest, is never too far beneath the surface in these parts, much like the darker side of Nairobi's "elite" life we've seen documented.

Ruto's attempt to differentiate between "legal" and "illegal" foreign business operations is a classic government move: acknowledge a problem, then pretend you had it under control all along. The message is clear: keep your head down, follow the paperwork, and don't cause a fuss. Otherwise, the "fair treatment" he promises might just evaporate, leaving you to face the consequences of our own unique brand of Kenyan hospitality.







President William Ruto, after a brief but noisy flirtation with a crackdown on foreign small-scale traders, has apparently remembered Kenya's "DNA" for being an "open society." Now, he's singing a different tune from Bomet, saying the country welcomes workers, investors, and visitors from Africa and beyond, provided they, you know, actually follow Kenyan laws. It’s the same old song, just with a slightly less aggressive intro.

His remarks days after he directed authorities to get tough on foreigners engaged in everything from hawking to petty trade now sound like a convenient backtrack. Ruto stressed that no Kenyan has the right to take the law into their own hands, a statement that rings a bit hollow when you consider the frequent displays of "Kenyan hospitality" towards outsiders, especially when business is involved. It seems the government is now more concerned about international optics and potential fallout than actually protecting local traders.

Ruto’s commitment to regional integration blocs like EAC, Comesa, and AfCFTA is being trotted out as the reason for this newfound openness. Citizens of these zones, he says, are free to invest and work here within the rules. The government will continue enforcing regulations, ensuring everyone, regardless of origin, is treated fairly and complies with employment, investment, and visitor rules. It’s a rather neat way of saying, "We'll kick you out if you don't have your papers in order, but we'd rather not admit we panicked."

This "clarification" follows understandable concern among foreign nationals, particularly East Africans, about harassment and the potential for xenophobic attacks. The government's assurance that documented foreign workers and traders are protected under Kenyan law feels a bit like closing the stable door after the horse has bolted, or at least after it’s already been chased around the paddock by overzealous officials. The message is simple: follow the official channels, or risk becoming the next target of the government’s shifting policies.
