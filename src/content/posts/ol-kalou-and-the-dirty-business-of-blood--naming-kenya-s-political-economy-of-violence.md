So, you thought Kenya’s democracy was about casting a ballot and going home? Think again. In Ol Kalou, 2026, folks decided they’d had enough of trusting the state to police its own elections. They became "vote guards," patting down uniformed officers and checking bags, because apparently, the only thing that matters is making sure no funny business gets into the polling station. Meanwhile, a few kilometres away, another crowd was using rocks to make a point about vehicles - because sometimes, you’ve got to stop the transport to stop the trouble. It’s a messy business, this "democracy."

Then the real show starts. Prados roll in, balaclavas go on, and suddenly, the air is thick with teargas. Some photojournalist gets roughed up and his camera snatched. Gunshots? Maybe, who cares when you’re running for your life? The official story? "Goonism." A convenient little word to sweep all the organized ugliness under the rug. It’s remarkable how easily we can pretend that a coordinated convoy, traceable to a sitting MP, is just a bunch of random hooligans. This is how power works, you see. It organizes violence, sure, but it also organizes the *language* we use to talk about it.

The Directorate of Criminal Investigations will launch a "probe," the Kenya Human Rights Commission will count unmarked vehicles, and journalists will do their usual song and dance, tracing plates and asking awkward questions. They’ll uncover the "convoy's anatomy," the unmarked vehicles, the politically connected figures, maybe even a presidential name dropped for good measure. It's all documented, all evidence. But will it change the narrative? Don't hold your breath. The Nation might name an MP, but Tuko will still scream "UDA MP’s Vehicle Linked to Goons." The Cabinet Secretary will vow to punish "goonism" while denying any parallel command structure. It’s a well-rehearsed play, this.

The problem isn't just that words are misused; it's that they become habits. "Goonism" explains nothing about who assembled them, who financed them, or who stood to benefit. It's a convenient fiction that allows us to avoid looking at the systemic rot. When a headline says "goons disrupted voting," the inquiry ends before it even begins. We’re looking at the stones and the teargas, not the architects of the chaos. This isn't just about linguistic accuracy; it's about a democratic obligation to follow the evidence, even when it leads to uncomfortable places, like a legislator's vehicle. We can't investigate structures we refuse to name.

This whole charade isn't new. It's a refined version of the same old tricks. Colonialists had their auxiliaries, KANU had its Youth Wing, and now we have these ad-hoc arrangements, like temporary supply chains for violence. They assemble, perform their task, and disappear before accountability can catch up. The economic conditions that leave young men desperate for work also make them available for whatever political economy of blood is on offer. It’s about logistics, money, and plausible deniability, not ideology.

The real question isn't "Who are these men?" but "Who assembled this network, who financed this operation, who expected a political return?" The reporting from Ol Kalou already points to organized coordination and political purpose. But our vocabulary, stuck in the past, struggles to keep up. We need language that can distinguish structured coercion from mere thuggery, especially when we consider the ongoing issues of governance and the scramble for resources that fuels these conflicts, as seen in discussions about [Africa 2026: Navigating the New Frontiers of Energy, Finance, and Strategic Sovereignty](/posts/africa-2026--navigating-the-new-frontiers-of-energy--finance--and-strategic-sovereignty). History isn't repeating; it's just getting more sophisticated in its brutality.
