Kenya's Influence: More Than Just Empty Peace Talks, Says French Envoy


The French envoy is trying to tell us Kenya is still a big deal, even when our peace talks go belly-up. He says it's our economy, our friends abroad, and our willingness to do the dirty jobs that really count. But let's be honest, when have our "mediation efforts" ever actually *solved* anything in this region? It's like trying to put out a bonfire with a teacup.


Suquet claims our economic muscle gives us clout beyond just handshakes with other governments. He’s talking about our businesses, our banks, and our ever-expanding trade deals. Apparently, these create "networks" that supposedly help us shape conversations. Sounds like a lot of fancy jargon for "we have money, so people listen." But when the Sudan conflict is still a dumpster fire and our attempts to talk sense into them have yielded zero, it’s hard to see that influence in action.


He admits there's been "little visible progress" in Sudan. Understatement of the year. But he wants us to appreciate Nairobi's "willingness to engage." Yes, we're willing to show up and have our pictures taken while the real problems fester. This whole mess, he says, is just a reflection of the wider regional rot. Apparently, other countries that used to be diplomatic big shots are too busy cleaning up their own messes to help us clean up ours. It's a race to the bottom, and we're all invited.


France seems to like us because we're a convenient pit stop for their businesses trying to get into East Africa. Transport links, legal services, a "relatively developed private sector" - all code for "it's easier to do business here than in most of your neighbours." And don't forget that defense agreement. Intelligence, maritime security, peacekeeping - sounds impressive, but how much of it actually translates into tangible results on the ground? It feels more like a formality for them, a way to keep a finger on the pulse.


Then there's the trade deal with Europe. Duty-free access for our goods, while they slowly chip away at our import duties. It’s a deal, sure, but the real benefit seems to be keeping us tethered to their market. It’s all about economics, partnerships, and strategic cooperation, but when you strip away the diplomatic fluff, it sounds a lot like we're just a useful pawn on the global chessboard, good for trade and taking on the thankless tasks nobody else wants. It reminds me of all the summits and talk about being a digital hub; [Kenya's Digital Hub Dreams: Another Summit, More Hot Air?](/posts/kenya-s-digital-hub-dreams--another-summit--more-hot-air) - a lot of noise, not much action.








The outgoing French Ambassador, Arnaud Suquet, is out here telling us Kenya's standing in Africa isn't just about whether our peace talks actually achieve anything. Apparently, we’ve got clout because of our economy, our international buddies, and our knack for taking on the tough global assignments. It's a nice sentiment, I suppose, but when you look at the actual results of some of these high-profile mediation attempts, it feels a bit like praising a chef for their willingness to cook while the kitchen is on fire.


Suquet’s got this theory that Kenya’s economic position gives us influence that stretches way past official government chats. He’s talking about our businesses, our financial institutions, and our growing commercial ties as the real power brokers. These, he argues, build networks that, over time, help us shape conversations and forge partnerships. He reckons we should be looking at this broader influence instead of just harping on whether our mediation efforts have brought about actual political settlements. It’s a neat distinction, but it doesn't change the fact that tangible peace hasn't materialized.


This distinction is especially relevant when you look at Kenya's involvement in the Sudan conflict. Our attempts to get people talking have hit roadblock after roadblock. Suquet admits there's been "little visible progress," but he still wants us to acknowledge Nairobi's "willingness to engage." Yes, we're willing to send people to meetings and make pronouncements, but the actual conflict is still raging. He blames the difficulties partly on the wider regional decline, where even countries that used to lead diplomatically are now wrestling with their own serious political and security nightmares. It’s a bit of a “look around, everyone’s struggling” situation.


Kenya's importance to France, according to Suquet, is also tied to its role as a major commercial and logistical hub on the continent. Paris sees Nairobi as a key entry point into East African markets, thanks to our transport infrastructure, financial and legal services, and a private sector that’s actually functioning. It’s why around 120 French companies are busy operating here. This commercial relationship has apparently blossomed into closer strategic cooperation, including a defense agreement last year that expands our collaboration into intelligence, maritime security, peacekeeping, training, and humanitarian operations.


Maritime cooperation, he emphasizes, is particularly significant given Kenya’s position on the Indian Ocean. Paris sees potential for closer collaboration on maritime security and information exchange. And let's not forget our trade arrangements with Europe. Under the Kenya-EU Economic Partnership Agreement, our goods get duty-free, quota-free access to the European market, while European imports get progressively lower duties over 25 years. It all sounds very interconnected and strategic, but one has to wonder how much of this truly benefits the average Kenyan on the ground, or if it’s just more sophisticated ways of maintaining existing power dynamics.
