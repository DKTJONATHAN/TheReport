After Oyo: Ambassadors Returning? Don't Hold Your Breath.

Nairobi is already celebrating a diplomatic win that hasn't even happened yet. The "return of ambassadors" from Sunday's summit in the Congo is just another political handshake, glossing over the deep distrust and unaddressed issues that will keep those diplomatic doors truly shut.

The whole "ambassador" thing is a joke, isn't it? Nairobi's man in Kinshasa, Peter Tum, is already posing with his shiny new bio, "Kenya's Ambassador to the DRC," even though the Congolese haven't bothered to give him the official nod. Under protocol, he's still just a hopeful nominee, a bit like someone saying they're married before the wedding. Kinshasa, bless their patient hearts, are in no rush. Their senior official basically told us, "We'll get to it, maybe, when we feel like it." It's called *agrément*, and they're holding it like a bargaining chip, knowing Nairobi's election clock is ticking faster than their own.

President Ruto is desperate for good news, for export growth, for protection of Kenyan money sunk into the DRC, and for that Mombasa-DRC trade corridor to actually start humming. He needs wins. Tshisekedi, on the other hand, is chilling. He's got no election looming, so he can afford to keep Nairobi's *agrément* on ice until they actually *do* something, not just talk about it. The Oyo summit was full of the usual diplomatic fluff - "mutual understanding," "good-neighbourliness" - which translates to "we haven't actually agreed on anything important yet." As one analyst put it, it's a "thaw, not a full reset." They'll bring ambassadors back, maybe dust off that old joint commission, and talk. Again.

The real problem is that Kenyan politicians, bless their naive hearts, just don't get the DRC's perspective. They're letting their own petty political fights spill over into foreign policy. Kinshasa's people are rightly skeptical, especially after Ruto's little stunt with Sudan last year. Accused of breaking promises there, and then suddenly, RSF guys are popping up in Nairobi like they own the place. It's no wonder the Congolese are looking at Ruto's Oyo pledges with the same suspicion they'd reserve for a politician promising tax cuts.

And then there are the statements. Nairobi's says all sunshine and roses about bilateral ties and trade. Kinshasa's? That's a whole other story. It's all about "state sovereignty" and "non-interference" - fancy diplomatic talk for "Nairobi, stop harbouring our rebels and people who badmouth us." Tshisekedi's readout even trumpeted the summit as part of a regional push against "Rwandan aggression." See? They know what's really going on. Ruto wants to calm Kenyan businesses; Tshisekedi wants to look tough at home. It’s a classic political manoeuvre, and frankly, it’s depressing how predictable it all is. The whole M23 issue, which is the heart of the matter, is conveniently left out of Nairobi's public statements. They're afraid of upsetting Rwanda, but in doing so, they're just cementing Kinshasa's distrust. It’s like trying to have a quiet word with your neighbour about their noisy dog while also trying to host a dinner party with them.

Even the mention of eradicating Ebola in the communiqué is telling. It's a safe, uncontroversial topic. It’s the diplomatic equivalent of saying "let's be nice" when you can't agree on anything else. Nairobi could have used health diplomacy, sending supplies directly, but no, they waited for a summit. It's a missed opportunity, a classic example of letting direct communication crumble. We saw it with the Cuban Missile Crisis, the silence between China and the US, and even our own recent thaw with Sudan. Talking to your rivals isn't a reward; it's basic survival. When embassies are empty, rumours replace facts, and a minor spat can turn into a costly trade war. Oyo got them in a room. Now, will they actually talk to each other when the cameras are gone? Don't bet your last shilling on it.







The promise of "return of ambassadors" from Sunday's four-party summit in Oyo, Republic of Congo, is already being spun as a diplomatic coup by Nairobi, but protocol is running far ahead of reality. In Kinshasa, Kenyan political appointee Peter Tum is already updating his X bio to "Kenya's Ambassador to the DRC," despite not having formal consent, or *agrément*, from the host state. Under diplomatic rules, he's just a nominee until officially accredited, a title that's currently more aspirational than actual.

President Tshisekedi's administration isn't exactly rushing to play along. When asked about granting accreditation, a senior Congolese official poured cold water on any immediate reset, stating it's a "gradual rapprochement" with no fixed schedule. Translation: Kinshasa isn't being hurried by Nairobi's domestic political calendar. Having stalled two previous Kenyan nominees, they know withholding *agrément* is their most potent leverage.

President Ruto urgently needs export growth and protection for Kenyan investments, especially with the 2027 election looming. He also needs momentum for the Mombasa-DRC trade corridor amidst the global scramble for critical minerals. Tshisekedi, however, faces no such immediate electoral pressure. Kinshasa will likely keep its *agrément* firmly on ice until Nairobi backs its words with tangible actions. This caution explains why dissecting the Oyo communiqué is far more critical than celebrating the handshake. The summit produced predictable boilerplate like "mutual understanding" and "African solidarity."

The fundamental issue between Nairobi and Kinshasa stems from a failure by Kenyan politicians, under President Ruto, to grasp the DRC's strategic perspective, exacerbated by domestic political rivalries bleeding into foreign policy. Statecraft requires acknowledging a neighbour's red lines, not necessarily agreeing with their worldview. Worse, this distrust has seeped into the Congolese public, where online commentators are urging caution towards Ruto's pledges. This skepticism is amplified by past events, like Sudan's military leadership accusing him last year of breaking a high-level promise, only for RSF figures to later resurface in Nairobi. We've seen how [Kenya's diplomatic blunders can have far reaching consequences](/posts/maraga--kenya-heads-for-state-failure-amid-kikuyu-clashes-and-southern-bypass-chaos).

The separate statements released by the Kenyan and Congolese presidencies after Oyo read like accounts of two different meetings. Ruto's stressed restoring ambassadorial ties and deepening trade, conspicuously omitting any mention of Rwanda. Tshisekedi's, however, led with solemn pledges on "state sovereignty, territorial integrity, [and] non-interference in internal affairs," a clear message to Nairobi to rein in Congolese opposition figures operating from Kenya. Kinshasa also framed the summit as part of regional momentum against "Rwandan aggression." Each leader left Oyo with a tailored narrative for their respective domestic audiences.

Even a public exchange on X between former US Special Envoy J. Peter Pham and Kenya's top foreign policy official, Korir Sing'oei, highlighted the raw, unresolved grievances. Pham warned that solidarity appeals would ring hollow if Nairobi continued allowing armed groups to use Kenya as a rear base, drawing a pointed comparison to hotels in Kinshasa. Sing'oei's furious rebuttal, dismissing critics as "manufacturers of innuendo," only underscored Kinshasa's skepticism by attacking the messenger instead of addressing the substance. The core contradiction at Oyo remains: while Rwanda's role in eastern Congo was central to discussions and Kinshasa's statement, Ruto refused to sign any joint communiqué naming Kigali, a calculation to avoid blowing up his ties with President Kagame. This public sidestepping of the Rwanda question, while Kinshasa spotlights it, leaves the deepest root of mutual suspicion untouched.
