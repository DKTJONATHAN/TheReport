Kenya's Fuel Security: A Mirage Amidst Global Oil Shocks

Kenyan officials are patting themselves on the back, claiming our fuel tanks are brimming with confidence despite the world's oil arteries getting a serious case of the jitters. Apparently, a good whack to Saudi Arabia's main oil pipeline is just a mild inconvenience for us, and our "strategic national reserves" and "existing supply contracts" are supposed to magically shield us from the impending global price hikes. Right. Let's all just hold our breath and hope for absolute calm, shall we?

Never mind that experts like Peter Ochieng are practically shouting from the rooftops that this "latest major shock" to the supply chain is going to translate directly into higher pump prices. He's talking about Brent crude at $107.4 a barrel and Murban at a cool $124. Add to that the rising costs of freight and insurance due to the ongoing Middle East drama, and the damaged infrastructure that will take months to fix - *if* the wars miraculously end tomorrow. But sure, the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority assures us everything is fine because we buy refined products from the Gulf oil majors. As if that's ever been a guarantee against price gouging.

The authorities are busy "monitoring unfolding events," which is government-speak for "we're hoping for the best and praying you don't riot." They've even frozen retail fuel prices for a month, a move that smells suspiciously like kicking the can down the road. It's a classic move, really. Keep the populace placid while the real storm gathers. It’s not unlike the machinations we’ve seen in the past, where deals are struck behind closed doors, a sort of [petroleum handshake](https://www.nation.co.ke/news/Golfs-Hollow-Moral-Victory-Ends-in-a-Petroleum-Handshake) that benefits a select few.

While independent analysts are warning of a surge, our regulator prefers to project "absolute calm." This is less about preventing panic buying and more about preventing mass outrage. They’re essentially telling us not to worry our pretty little heads about the volatile geopolitical landscape or the fact that our fuel security hinges on contracts with oil majors in a region perpetually on the brink. It’s the Kenyan way: hope for the best, prepare for the worst, and blame everyone else when it all goes south.
