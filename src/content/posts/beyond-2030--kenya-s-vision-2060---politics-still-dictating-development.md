So, they've got a new blueprint, Vision 2060 they're calling it. The idea is to make Kenya a Singapore by its 100th birthday. Sounds nice, eh? They say if this plan is tied to what ordinary Kenyans actually want and kept away from the election circus, we could see some real change in a generation. But let's be honest, when has anything in this country ever been that simple? It’s all about bold reforms, they claim, but the usual suspects - corruption, weak human capital, and a mountain of debt - are still the main characters in this Kenyan drama.

Professor Hiroyuki Hino, a consultant who's apparently been whispering sweet nothings into President Ruto's ear about this Vision 2060, points out that corruption is the real villain, gnawing away at our economy and our social fabric. He’s right, of course. At KICC, they’re talking about how stopping corruption could be "truly transformative." Maybe, if pigs could fly. The World Bank, bless their cotton socks, is throwing a US$750 million lifeline, but it comes with more conditions than a Nairobi matatu conductor. Disclosure of public officials’ personal interests, tighter rules on unsolicited PPP deals, and a Whistleblower Protection Act that’s more likely to get the whistleblower silenced than protected.

Then there's the debt. KSh13.0 trillion, for crying out loud. We're way above the IMF's sensible threshold, and the interest payments alone could probably fund half the projects in this new Vision. They’re trying to spin it, talking about credit rating upgrades and successful Eurobond maneuvers. It's like putting lipstick on a dying hyena. While they're busy patting themselves on the back for not defaulting *today*, the fundamental problem of how we got here, and how we'll get out, remains largely unaddressed. It’s the same old song and dance, with slightly different financial instruments.

And the politics? Oh, the glorious Kenyan politics. President Ruto’s talking about a "national conversation," but the opposition is already grumbling, and the public? We're largely indifferent. It’s hard to get excited when you’ve seen this movie before. The fear is that this grand Vision 2060 will just become another bargaining chip, another talking point for the 2027 election. We've seen leaders try to rally support through less than savory means, like those desperate grabs for numbers we discussed [Kindiki's 'Come Back Home' Plea to Munya, Linturi, Muturi: A Desperate Grab for Numbers?](/posts/kindiki-s--come-back-home--plea-to-munya--linturi--muturi--a-desperate-grab-for-numbers). This country thrives on political theatrics, and a long-term development plan is prime real estate for that.

The real question isn't whether we can *write* a pretty plan for 2060. It’s whether we, as a nation, have the political will, the institutional backbone, and the public trust to actually *stick to it*. Without that, this Vision 2060 is just another pretty document gathering dust in a government office, while the same old problems continue to dictate our reality. It's a nice idea, but in Nairobi, we've learned that nice ideas don't always survive first contact with reality, especially when that reality is a political playground.

Even the United Opposition can't seem to agree on a single candidate for 2027, months after promising to name one. Meanwhile, the ODM and UDA are busy with their usual political games, and there's even talk of factional disputes within the ODM following Raila Odinga's death. This is the landscape Vision 2060 has to navigate. It’s a landscape built on promises, broken promises, and the perpetual pursuit of power, not necessarily progress.
