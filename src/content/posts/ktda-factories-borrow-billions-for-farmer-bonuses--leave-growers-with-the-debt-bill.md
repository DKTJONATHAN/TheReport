So, the annual tea bonus, that sweet, sweet reward for back-breaking labour, turns out to be just another con. We're told it's for better leaf, higher prices, smart factory management. But the Tea Board of Kenya, bless their diligent souls, has blown the lid off this elaborate charade. Turns out, a chunk of that celebrated "profit" isn't profit at all. It's borrowed money, dressed up as a bonus, leaving farmers with a bill they never agreed to pay. It's the oldest trick in the book, just dressed up in agricultural jargon.

This isn't some minor overdraft; these are debts so hefty they're putting entire factories on life support. We're talking Sh34.05 billion outstanding for 69 factories alone by the end of 2025. Kapkoros Tea Factory is leading the pack of shame with Sh2.77 billion. You think your monthly rent is bad? Try servicing debt that's many times the value of your entire farm. And who shoulders this burden? The farmers, of course. The very people who own these factories and rely on them for their livelihood, now shackled by the "generosity" of their own management. It’s a classic case of [The Central Bank Gaslight](https://example.com/posts/the-central-bank-gaslight), where the real figures are hidden behind a smokescreen of supposed success.

The numbers, as usual, don't lie, even if the people in charge do. The western block, already struggling with cash flow, is drowning in debt. Factories there needed Sh6.37 billion for their second payment but borrowed a staggering Sh24.01 billion. That's nearly four times what they actually required for the bonus. Meanwhile, the eastern block, comparatively sane, borrowed only 29% of their bonus requirement. It’s a clear pattern of mismanagement and, dare I say, deliberate deception. They borrowed billions - Sh30.47 billion from the big banks like KCB and Stanbic - not just for bonuses, but to cover operating deficits, refinance old debts, and settle obligations like Sacco deductions and fertiliser bills.

And the governance? A joke. Board resolutions authorising loans without specifying the amount? That’s not oversight; that’s a blank cheque for disaster. In some cases, borrowing exceeded approved limits by hundreds of millions. The excuse? "Old stock accumulated over years, sold for less after reserve prices were lifted." Convenient, isn't it? The auditors call it deliberate overvaluation to enable "overpayment of the second payment and over-borrowing." They even tried to amortise losses over five years, which, according to international accounting standards, is a big fat no.

Even the internal borrowing scheme, where factories lent to each other, was deemed a mess before it was scrapped. Now, all that interest is flowing out of the KTDA network and straight into commercial banks, with penalties piling up. The sheer audacity of it all. Farmers are told they're getting a bonus, a reward for their hard work, when in reality, they're being handed a loan document disguised as good news. This entire system is designed to keep the farmers in a perpetual state of financial dependence, a debt cycle that benefits everyone but them.
