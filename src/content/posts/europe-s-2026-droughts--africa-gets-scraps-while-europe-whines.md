So, Europe's finally having a hot, dry summer. Big deal. Millions there face water restrictions and, shocker, it's costing them a few billion euros. Meanwhile, here in Kenya, we’ve been dealing with "widespread hunger" and "falling short" on emergency food programs for years. But now that the posh European countries are feeling the heat, suddenly they want to "share knowledge and finance" with us. It’s funny how crisis makes neighbours out of people who usually just see us as a place to dump their old clothes.

They’re talking about learning from African leaders, which is rich considering how many European governments are busy slashing development finance. That’s what’s really hampering adaptation and water management efforts on the ground. Northern Kenya is currently a testament to that, with people going hungry *again*. It’s the same old story: when the rains fail here, it’s just another Tuesday. When it happens in Europe, it’s a global emergency warranting a new strategy.

Kenya has apparently "revolutionised its approach" since that 2010-11 drought, building early warning systems and adaptation funding. Good for us, I guess. But the article itself admits we "struggle to find the resources to translate warnings into an effective response." It’s like having a crystal ball that tells you you’re about to get hit by a bus, but you can’t afford to move out of the way. And don't even get me started on how foreign entities manage to get banned from schemes worth billions here, while we’re begging for spare change. [World Bank Slaps 2 Kenyan Firms with 21-Month Ban Over Ksh149.8 Billion Scheme](/posts/world-bank-slaps-2-kenyan-firms-with-21-month-ban-over-ksh149-8-billion-scheme).

South Africa, bless their hearts, is trying nature-based solutions and even some fancy financial bonds to clear invasive plants. Eswatini is blending indigenous knowledge with early warning systems. These are the kinds of things we've been doing out of necessity, not because it's a trendy new strategy. And now Europe, with all its wealth, wants to be our partner. It sounds nice on paper, but let’s be real, it'll probably be another situation where they dictate the terms and we end up with more promises than actual progress.

They’re talking about global partnerships and trust, but it's hard to trust when the very countries that preach cooperation are the ones cutting the aid that actually makes a difference. They want our "know-how" and our "resources," but what about their obligations? It’s always "we’ll help you help yourselves," while conveniently forgetting to provide the actual help. Maybe when their reservoirs refill, they’ll remember us. Or maybe not. It’s Kenya, after all. [Kenya's Emerging Artists: Another Grind with Little Reward](/posts/kenya-s-emerging-artists--another-grind-with-little-reward) is just one example of how hard it is to get ahead when the system is rigged.
