KENYA POLICE CONFIRM: NO SUCH THING AS DOMESTIC VIOLENCE CRIME

WARNING: Nearly half of Kenyan women in some counties are victims of domestic violence, but the police admit they don't recognize it as a specific crime.

Victims of domestic abuse in Kenya will find little comfort in the latest pronouncements from the National Police Service. It seems that all those beatings, all that pain, all that trauma - it doesn't add up to a crime called "domestic violence." According to the ever-so-helpful Muchiri Nyaga, spokesperson for the NPS, the police deal with assault, battery, and grievous harm. The relationship between the perpetrator and the victim? That's apparently secondary, something that "can only come later." So, if your spouse decides to rearrange your face, don't expect the police to be too concerned about your marital status.

Nyaga’s explanation is a masterclass in bureaucratic doublespeak. He likens it to femicide, claiming that no charge sheet specifically accuses someone of killing a woman; it's just murder, with the victim being female and the relationship providing "context." This is how you sweep a pervasive societal ill under the rug, folks. It's not a problem with a name; it's just a collection of individual incidents that *might* be connected if the investigating officer feels like it, and if you, the victim, manage to keep the investigation from stalling.

The reality for those suffering is even more grim. The police require "sufficient evidence" to even forward a case to the Director of Public Prosecutions. This means you, the victim, must follow up relentlessly, ensure an investigator is assigned, and then help them find witnesses. If you disappear or fail to cooperate, the case dies. It’s another layer of institutional decay, much like the corruption we've seen exposed within the Police Federation itself. The onus is entirely on the victim to not only survive the abuse but also to navigate a system that seems designed to make reporting as difficult as possible.

And let's not forget the sheer absurdity of it all. While county statistics paint a grim picture of domestic violence affecting up to 48 percent of women in some areas, the police are essentially saying, "We don't have a box for that." So, when you walk into a police station bleeding and battered, and you mention your spouse is the one who did it, prepare for a shrug and a lecture on the specifics of assault charges. It’s a system that protects the abuser by refusing to acknowledge the pattern of violence, effectively gaslighting victims on a national scale.

This whole charade perfectly illustrates why so many feel let down by the authorities. It's not just about a lack of resources; it's a fundamental failure to understand or acknowledge the lived realities of countless Kenyans. The statistics are horrifying, but the police response is even more so. They are admitting, in so many words, that they can’t or won’t tackle domestic violence as a distinct, pervasive issue.

Perhaps we should all just accept our fate. If your partner beats you, it’s not domestic violence. It’s just assault. And if you can’t gather enough evidence, well, that’s your problem. Nairobi's streets might be swamped by police during protests, but when it comes to real, life-altering abuse, justice seems to be in very short supply.













The Kenyan National Bureau of Statistics is out here dropping bombshells, telling us that nearly half of women in some counties are getting roughed up at home. But here’s the kicker, the punchline that’s supposed to make us laugh but just makes us want to scream: can you actually walk into a police station and report this for what it is? According to the National Police Service spokesperson, Muchiri Nyaga, the answer is a resounding "no." "We don't have a crime called domestic violence," he stated flat out. Apparently, you have to get specific with your charge sheets.

Nyaga claims the police focus on the act itself - assault, stabbing, grievous harm. The fact that it’s your spouse, your partner, the person you share your life with doing the damage? That’s apparently a detail that "can only come later." He even bizarrely compares it to femicide, saying that murder charges don't specify the victim's gender. It’s a convenient way to avoid acknowledging the widespread pattern of abuse that disproportionately affects women.

The process, as described, is a bureaucratic obstacle course. Once an "offence" is reported, a file is opened. But for that file to go anywhere, you, the victim, must follow up relentlessly. An officer commanding station, speaking anonymously, lays it bare: "It is important to report, but for us to have a file to forward to the ODPP, we must have sufficient evidence to show that we have a case." That means you’re on your own to find witnesses and push for an investigator to be assigned. It’s another layer of institutional decay, much like the recent scandals involving police federation fraud.

The investigating officer might "link them all up" if there are previous episodes, but the "specific criminal act remains the primary focus." So, while your history of abuse might be noted, it’s not the crime itself. It’s just context, like the color of the sky during a robbery. Then, if you're lucky and persistent enough, the evidence gets punted to the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions, who then decides if your broken bones and shattered spirit are worth their time.

This is Nairobi for you. We get stats that scream of a crisis, but when it comes to actual acknowledgment and action, the system throws up its hands and says, "Sorry, not a defined crime." It’s a perfect illustration of why many Kenyans feel abandoned by the very institutions meant to protect them. The police are essentially admitting they can't or won't treat domestic violence as a distinct issue, leaving victims to navigate a system that seems to actively discourage reporting.

So, next time you hear about domestic violence in Kenya, remember: it’s not a crime. It’s just assault, with complicated relationship dynamics. And good luck getting justice if you can’t gather enough evidence to satisfy a police force that doesn't even recognize the term. It's a cynical reality check for anyone hoping for systemic change.
