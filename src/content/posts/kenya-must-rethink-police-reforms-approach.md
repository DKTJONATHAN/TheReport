KENYA MUST RETHINK POLICE REFORMS: THE MENTAL HEALTH JOKE
WARNING: The Koffi Anan Institute is already screaming about an 84.1% chance of chaos in the next election. And what are we talking about? Mostly just the usual blame game.
The election circus is gearing up again, and surprise, surprise, Kenya is already showing all the signs of another avoidable disaster. By-elections are a mess, political tension is bubbling, and everyone with a brain knows election violence is practically a given. But while we're busy arguing about who's accountable and whether the electoral commission is a joke, the real rot at the core is being ignored: the mental state of the very people supposed to keep us safe.

It's not just about whether the police will be biased this time, though that's a given. The real worry is that institutions meant to protect us could end up being just another tool in some politician's dirty tricks bag. We’ve seen it before, and if trust in the police continues to evaporate, expect the whole country to go up in flames. Yet, all the talk about police reform conveniently forgets the hell these officers go through daily. They're expected to be superheroes, witnessing the worst of humanity, dealing with riots, and facing down suffering, all without a proper ear to listen or a shoulder to lean on. And then we complain when they don't perform like choirboys.

The article mentions "Sick Barber Sam Kitiki Needs Prayers and Cash for Cancer Treatment." This isn't just some individual tragedy; it's a symptom of a system that barely acknowledges suffering. This constant exposure to trauma doesn't just disappear when the uniform comes off. It follows them home, gnawing at their peace, leading to burnout, emotional bankruptcy, and relationships in the gutter. How can we expect restraint and professionalism from officers drowning in unaddressed psychological burdens? It’s like asking a drowning man to swim a marathon.

We need to get real about police reform. Yes, officers must be held accountable. But accountability and their mental well-being aren't competing for attention; they should be two sides of the same coin. The government’s job isn't just about paying salaries and handing out badges. It’s about providing real, systematic support, counselling, and mental health interventions. This isn't some fluffy perk; it's a fundamental pillar of public safety. When the state shows it actually cares about its police force, maybe, just maybe, those officers will remember to be humane when dealing with us. It's time we realized that the path to peaceful elections might just start in a therapist's office, not just at the ballot box.
