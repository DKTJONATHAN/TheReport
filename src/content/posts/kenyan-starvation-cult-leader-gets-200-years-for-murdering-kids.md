Kenyan Starvation Cult Leader Gets 200 Years For Murdering Kids

The cult leader who convinced people to starve themselves and their children to death has been sentenced to 200 years. This disaster, however, was just another Tuesday for Kenya.

So, Paul Mackenzie, the "man of God," decided heaven was just a few days without food away. He convinced his followers, and apparently his accomplices were eager to help, that starving to death was the express ticket to paradise. Now, 191 souls, mostly children, are in the ground, not in heaven. Justice Diana Kavedza, bless her heart, dished out 100 years for starvation murder and another 100 for direct homicide. As if the distinction matters when you're shoveling bodies into shallow graves. Mackenzie, ever the showman, even managed a smile. Typical.

This whole Shakahola Forest mess is just another chapter in Kenya's ongoing saga of "Same Script, Different Date." We've seen this movie before, haven't we? Religious fervor turning deadly, authorities arriving late to the party, and then the usual calls for "tougher regulations" that will likely go nowhere. It's a miracle more people haven't ended up like this, given how easily folks fall for snake oil salesmen in robes.

The police apparently got a tip-off. A tip-off! After weeks of bodies turning up in forests and latrines, some buried alive. It's almost as if they *want* to be surprised. And Mackenzie? Arrested twice before, then what? Released. Because clearly, a history of questionable behavior is just a stepping stone to divine inspiration in this country.

This isn't just a Kenyan problem, though. It’s a global phenomenon, a chilling reminder of how susceptible people are to charismatic figures promising salvation, especially when the world feels like it's falling apart. We’ve seen it in Uganda, we saw it in Jonestown. The only difference here is the setting and the sheer, grim audacity of it all.

What happens next? More investigations, more outrage, maybe a few more arrests, and then… life goes on. Until the next disaster, of course. Because in Nairobi, the only thing more predictable than the traffic is our collective amnesia.
