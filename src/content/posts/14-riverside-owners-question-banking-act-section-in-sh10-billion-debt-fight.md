So, the big wigs at Cape Holdings, the folks who own that posh 14 Riverside complex, have decided they’ve had enough. They’re running to the High Court, crying foul over a section of the Banking Act. Apparently, letting interest on court-awarded debts climb higher than the original principal is an affront to their delicate sensibilities. It’s all about this “in duplum principle” - a fancy term for saying the interest shouldn't outgrow the actual debt. But for these guys, it seems the principle is less important than the cash.

Their argument, as I understand it, is that this section of the law - Section 44A (4) - is unconstitutional. It supposedly tramples on their rights to equality, dignity, and property. Funny how these rights only get invoked when the money gets serious. The original award from some arbitrator was Sh1.666 billion. Now, Synergy Industrial Credit Limited, the creditor, is claiming a cool Sh10.679 billion. That’s a jump of about Sh9 billion, and guess what? Eighty-four percent of that is compound interest. It’s like a debt parasite that’s grown into a monster, even bigger than the property it’s supposed to be attached to.

Cape Holdings is quick to point out they aren’t trying to rehash the original arbitration or any previous court rulings. Oh no, that would be too much trouble for them. They just want the court to confirm that this level of interest accumulation and enforcement is, you know, *constitutionally permissible*. Because clearly, when your debt is more than double the market value of your prime real estate, something feels a little off. It’s a classic Nairobi tale - the rich fighting over who gets to exploit whom more effectively.

Synergy Industrial Credit, on the other hand, is having none of it. They’ve been at this for 16 years, dragged it through every court, and now they just want it to stop. They’re arguing that Cape Holdings is just trying to re-open a settled case through a constitutional loophole. And frankly, after all this time, who can blame them? It’s a whole lot of legal wrangling, probably costing more in lawyer fees than the original debt. It reminds me of some of the endless financial sagas we've seen, like the [former energy chiefs held over Sh4.8 billion fuel import scandal](https://example.com/posts/former-energy-chiefs-held-over-sh4-8-billion-fuel-import-scandal-released-on-police-bail) - all about money, power, and loopholes.

The High Court is now deciding whether to even hear this case or just throw it out. The owners of 14 Riverside are hoping for a miracle, or at least a constitutional interpretation that suits their current predicament. Synergy just wants their money and for everyone to move on. It’s a classic clash of interests, where one side claims to be fighting for principles, while the other is just trying to collect. We’ll see what the court decides on October 22, 2026, but don’t expect any fairy tales.

Ultimately, this whole saga is about how much is too much. When a debt, amplified by the magic of compound interest and a convenient exclusion from legal protections, becomes bigger than the actual asset it’s meant to secure, it raises questions. Are these laws designed to protect people, or just to create more opportunities for financial predation? Cape Holdings is betting on the latter being unconstitutional. We’ll see if the court agrees.
