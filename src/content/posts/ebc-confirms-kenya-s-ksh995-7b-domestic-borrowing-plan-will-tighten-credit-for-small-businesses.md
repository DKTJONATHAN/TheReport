Well, here we go again. Kenya’s National Treasury, bless its heart, plans to hoover up another Ksh 995.7 billion from our domestic markets by 2026/27. Why? To cover the lion's share of a colossal Ksh 1.112 trillion fiscal deficit. Net external borrowing is a mere whisper compared to what they expect local investors - read: *banks* - to cough up. They say it shields the government from exchange rate drama. Convenient, isn't it? The government saves face, and the rest of us just carry the burden. EBC Financial Group is here to state the obvious: this will only intensify the competition for liquidity, meaning less for actual businesses. Shocking, I tell you. Absolutely shocking.

It’s not hard to see why banks will jump at it. Government securities are like a warm, safe blanket for lenders. Predictable returns, minimal due diligence - none of the messy "company checks" required for actual business loans. Banks already have about 27% of their assets - a cool Ksh 2.2 trillion as of March 2026 - tied up in state paper. Adding another nearly one trillion shillings? It’s a no-brainer for them, and a death knell for private enterprise funding. David Precious from EBC isn't mincing words; banks will simply offer smaller loans, demand more collateral, or shorten repayment periods for anyone they deem "riskier." Which, let's be honest, is practically every small business in Kenya.

And don't let those rosy numbers about private sector credit growth fool you. The Central Bank might brag about 8.1% year-on-year growth, but EBC rightly points out that "expanding totals do not guarantee equal access for smaller borrowers." It's a tale as old as time: the big fish get bigger, the small fish starve. Collateral demands remain a stubborn wall, with over 85% of MSME lending trapped behind stringent requirements. Non-performing loans keep banks wary, and with new capital reserve pressures - even with a slightly extended deadline for that Ksh 10 billion minimum core capital - banks will naturally gravitate towards the safest bets: government bonds or large, already wealthy, well-collateralised corporations. It's a rigged game, much like when [Ruto's New Bills Signed: More Taxman Trouble, Less Investor Joy](/posts/ruto-s-new-bills-signed--more-taxman-trouble--less-investor-joy) became law.

The ripple effects? Oh, they’ll be delightful. Manufacturers will delay investing in new machinery because financing tenors are too short. Distributors will cut inventory because overdraft limits are tighter than a politician's grip on power. And further down the chain, our hardworking suppliers and everyday workers will face thinner orders and reduced demand. So, while the government congratulates itself on shoring up its balance sheet and the banks enjoy their risk-free profits, the real economy - the one where people actually work and create value - will slowly, but surely, get squeezed dry. EBC might conclude that the "real test" is whether growth reaches MSMEs. I'll save them the suspense: it won't.
