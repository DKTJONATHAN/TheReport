So, Governor Sakaja, fresh from his spiritual uplift at St. Austin's, has announced another grand plan for Nairobi. This time it's 2,500 "trained" officers for a new Metropolitan Police Unit. Because apparently, our existing traffic cops are just too busy counting boda-bodas to stop actual criminals. He says they'll be trained for the "unique policing needs" of a city that swells to seven million during the day. Unique needs? We’ve got a unique need for functioning drains and reliable electricity, Governor. Let's start there.

The whole thing sounds like another expensive exercise in moving deckchairs on the Titanic. They're talking about shifting from traffic to patrols and rapid response. We’ll see how "rapid" that response is when your phone gets snatched in broad daylight. And transforming Kamukunji Police Station into a headquarters? Sounds more like a glorified bus station with a fresh coat of paint, promising to house a "command centre." Let's hope the actual commanders know what they're doing, unlike some we’ve seen. This whole show feels like a rerun of promises made, promises broken, a tale as old as time, mirroring the [The Global Contagion of Institutional Decay](/posts/the-global-contagion-of-institutional-decay).

Then comes the high-tech dream: a citywide CCTV network linked to a "Traffic Management Centre" at City Cabanas. Multi-billion shilling facilities, AI-powered surveillance, and integration with private cameras. They want to use cameras like they're going to magically solve all our problems. Nairobi's already a city where you need eyes in the back of your head, and now they want to cover every corner with digital peeping Toms. It’ll be another expensive toy for the city bosses to play with, while the actual streets remain a free-for-all.

Sakaja and his cronies have been jetting off to New York and London, apparently "benchmarking" modern policing. Because Nairobi is *exactly* like New York or London, right? Different continents, different problems, different money. We’re a city with its own brand of chaos, and these fancy foreign models will likely just add another layer of bureaucracy and misplaced funds. The goal seems to be less about actual public safety and more about looking like we're doing something sophisticated, all under the guise of a "cooperation agreement."

The real question is, who is actually benefiting from all this? More training, new centres, fancy cameras - it all sounds good on paper, especially when you're talking about "multi-billion shilling facilities." But for the average mwananchi trying to get home safely or just walk down the street without being harassed, what’s changing on the ground? We’ve seen these promises before. More officers, better equipment, grand pronouncements. The only thing that seems to expand is the expense. Let’s hope these 2,500 officers are actually going to be on the streets, not just filling out paperwork in a newly refurbished station.
