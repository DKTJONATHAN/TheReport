The global markets are currently betting on the mercurial whims of the American political cycle and the temporary postponement of a Middle East firestorm. To call the receding threat of an Iran-Israel escalation a "fundamental" for buying airline stocks is a delusion of the highest order. Global stability is now so fragile that the mere absence of an immediate missile strike is treated as an economic stimulus. The reality remains that Japan is an energy-starved island dependent on shipping lanes that could be choked at any moment, regardless of whether a military action is "postponed" or merely rescheduled.

The yen is languishing at 40-year lows, effectively turning Japan into a discount warehouse for foreign vultures. While the TOPIX celebrates a 1.37 percent rise, the average Japanese household is being crushed by the cost of imported survival. The Bank of Japan is staring down its upcoming meeting with no good options. Raising rates might provide a temporary floor for the currency, but it will simultaneously shatter the fragile fiscal balance of a nation buried under 370 trillion yen of promised public-private investment. The central bank is trapped in a loop where every move toward "normalcy" threatens to trigger a systemic collapse.

The "selective" nature of the semiconductor trade reveals the truth that the AI bubble is leaking. Names like SoftBank and Kioxia are no longer the golden tickets they were a month ago; they have become barometers of exhaustion. The money moving into "domestic-demand" shares is just capital hiding from the inevitable popping of the tech cycle. It is a desperate search for yield in a landscape where even a 10 percent profit guidance from giants like Shin-Etsu Chemical is treated as a failure. This ego-driven market cycle mirrors the "death rattles" seen in other over-hyped global trends.

Internationally, Japan remains the ultimate risk factor for the global carry trade. As the yen fluctuates near the 164 level and the Bank of Japan flirts with hawkishness, the cheap liquidity that has fueled global speculation for a decade is under threat. If Tokyo is forced to raise rates to protect its borders from import-driven inflation, the shockwaves will hit New York and London with more force than any geopolitical headline. The "rebound" of July 27 is a temporary distraction from the structural decay of a global system that can no longer afford its own debt.
