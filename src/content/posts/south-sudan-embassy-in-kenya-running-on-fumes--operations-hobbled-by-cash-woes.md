So, the South Sudanese embassy in Nairobi has decided to embrace the Nairobi hustle by cutting operating hours. Apparently, their "cash crunch" is so severe they can barely keep the doors open, forcing diplomats to play hide-and-seek with their responsibilities. Juba, of course, is quick to deny any complete shutdown, trotting out the tired old line about "temporary technical problems." It's the same song and dance they always pull when their ship is sinking - blame it on a glitch, not on blatant mismanagement and a black hole of corruption.

This latest drama follows reports that the embassy hasn't paid its rent for four months. Four months! You'd think for the salary they're (supposedly) getting, keeping a roof over their heads would be a priority. And it's not just rent; Kenyan staff are claiming they haven't seen a payslip in 18 months, their money allegedly "diverted." It’s a classic case of "do as I say, not as I do" from a government that seems more interested in appearing official than actually functioning.

The spokesperson, Apuk Ayuel Mayen, assures us that everything is being "resolved." Resolution usually means someone’s cousin is getting a nice fat commission, not that the actual problem is being fixed. This isn't the first time this circus has rolled into town; they were also shut down for rent arrears back in November 2025. It’s almost poetic, really, given how South Sudan's economy is crumbling under the weight of its own oil-dependent folly, unable to export anything thanks to the war in Sudan. It makes you wonder if this is just another facet of the broader economic instability that plagues the continent, something we’ve seen ripple through other sectors too, like in [The Ghost in the Ledger: How Private Equity is Cannibalizing the African Commons](/posts/the-ghost-in-the-ledger-how-private-equity-is-cannibalizing-the-african-commons).

And the timing? The new ambassador, Simon Juach Deng, just said his goodbyes to President Salva Kiir on August 6th, all set to grace Nairobi with his presence in September. Probably has no idea he's walking into a financial disaster zone. Meanwhile, Juba is busy telling its neighbours it's ready for elections in December, which, given their current state, sounds about as likely as a unicorn landing at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport.

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation is all about "official channels," advising the public and media to rely on them. That’s rich, coming from a ministry that can’t even keep its own embassy afloat. They're busy hobnobbing with Kenyan officials about "economic diplomacy" and "joint infrastructure projects" like the Lokichogio-Nadapal-Juba road corridor. It’s all a big show, a desperate attempt to project an image of stability and progress while the foundations are literally crumbling.
