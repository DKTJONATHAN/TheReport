Kenya Positions Itself as "Regional Investment Hub" - For Who Exactly?

These Americans are back, sniffing around for "opportunities" while our own people are still figuring out how to cross the road without being shaken down. Sing'oei's all smiles and promises of a "competitive, transparent" environment. We’ll see about that.

The Principal Secretary for Foreign Affairs, Korir Sing’oei, hosting another delegation from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the U.S.-Africa Business Center (USAfBC), led by its President, Kendra Gaither, in Nairobi. Apparently, this is the second "major" U.S. business shindig this year. They're talking about expanding trade, getting private sector cash, and deepening ties, all building on President Ruto’s big trip to America. It’s the same old song and dance, just with fancier suits this time.

Sing’oei’s mumbling about the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA) being extended to 2028. Great, more certainty for those who are already exporting and investing. For the rest of us, it’s just more jargon to keep us confused while the real deals are being made behind closed doors. Kenya's also pushing its "advanced financial ecosystem" and "skilled workforce" - code for cheap labour and easily accessible markets for them to exploit.

The priority areas they've identified are the usual suspects: digital economy, fintech, healthcare, minerals, and the "creative economy" to tap into youth energy. Basically, anywhere they can extract value with minimal effort on their part. And of course, Kenya will "reaffirm its full institutional support" for the AmCham Business Summit. More handshakes, more photo ops, and who knows what "concrete commercial outcomes" will actually trickle down. It's a familiar narrative, much like the ongoing debates around whether digital banking is truly reshaping our economy or just providing more shiny distractions, as we've seen before.

Sing’oei claims these visits are about converting diplomatic ties into "concrete commercial outcomes." Let’s be honest, the only concrete outcomes will be for the Americans and the select few Kenyans who manage to get a slice of the pie. The talk of a "competitive, transparent and investor-friendly environment" sounds nice, but we’ve heard it all before, haven’t we? It always seems to translate into an environment that's friendly to foreign investors and competitive for everyone else trying to make a decent living.

It’s a classic Nairobi script: foreign bigwigs come, get wined and dined, talk about opportunities, and then leave with more than they came with, while the local population is left hoping for crumbs. The "regional investment hub" pitch is just another way of saying we're open for business, meaning open for them to make money. Let’s just hope they don’t expect us to roll out the red carpet without asking for something substantial in return this time.







The Principal Secretary for Foreign Affairs, Korir Sing’oei, hosted a delegation from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the U.S.-Africa Business Center (USAfBC), led by its President, Kendra Gaither, in Nairobi. This is the second major U.S. business delegation to Kenya this year, focusing on expanding bilateral trade, mobilising private-sector investment, and strengthening business ties. It's all built on the back of President William Ruto’s State Visit to the United States in May 2024. It’s the same old script, dressed up in new diplomatic clothes.

Sing’oei seems pleased about the extension of the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA) through 2028, claiming it offers "greater certainty" for exporters and investors. Certainty for whom? For the ones who are already ahead, no doubt. Kenya is also urging American businesses to use the country as a launchpad for accessing markets across the East African Community, COMESA, and the African Continental Free Trade Area. They're touting our "advanced financial ecosystem," "skilled workforce," and "connectivity" - essentially, our resources and our people, available for their exploitation.

The talks identified key areas for resource mobilisation: the digital economy and financial technology, advanced healthcare and medical infrastructure, critical minerals and sustainable supply chains, and the creative economy and youth empowerment. In short, the sectors where they see profit margins. Kenya also pledged full institutional support for the upcoming AmCham Business Summit in Nairobi, which means more networking events and official photo opportunities.

"Private-sector partnerships are essential to translating the strong Kenya-U.S. bilateral relationship into sustainable economic outcomes," Sing’oei declared, reaffirming Kenya’s dedication to a competitive, transparent, and investor-friendly environment. This sounds good on paper, but we’ve seen these pronouncements before. Often, "investor-friendly" translates to easy money for foreigners while local businesses struggle with red tape and unfair competition. It’s a familiar tune, much like the persistent concerns over corruption, even in areas like police uniform tenders.

According to Sing’oei, the visit is part of a growing partnership to convert diplomatic ties into concrete commercial outcomes. The reality is, these "concrete outcomes" usually benefit the visiting party more than the host. While the government talks about positioning Kenya as a regional hub, the average Kenyan is still grappling with the daily grind, wondering if any of these grand plans will ever translate into tangible improvements in their lives.

Kenya is reaffirming its dedication to a competitive, transparent, and investor-friendly environment. Let's just hope that this time, "investor-friendly" doesn't just mean friendly to American investors looking to profit, while the rest of us are left negotiating the complexities of simply trying to get by. It’s a delicate dance, and we’ve seen the steps before.
