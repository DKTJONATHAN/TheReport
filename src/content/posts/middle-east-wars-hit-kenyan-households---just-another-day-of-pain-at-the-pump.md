Remember when Kenyans worried only about their own dusty villages? Life was simpler, a lot less smoky from the acrid smell of petrol, and cooking was done with firewood. Now, thanks to some rich folks playing war games in the Middle East, we’re all paying the price. A fight between the US, Israel, and Iran has sent fuel costs soaring, making it harder to move, farm, cook, and even produce basic food. This isn't about our villages anymore; it's about how deep our pockets are, or rather, how empty they're about to become.

As we've traded our village paths for city streets, more cars and more goods mean we’re tied tighter than ever to this global economy. We import nearly all our fuel, lining the pockets of Saudi and UAE companies, all to feed our insatiable urban appetite for cars and transportation. So when the US and Israel decide to poke Iran, and Iran retaliates by disrupting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, guess who suffers? We do. Super petrol is now a luxury, hitting Sh226.96 a litre in Nairobi, and diesel, the backbone of our economy, isn't far behind at Sh227.76. Politicians are sweating bullets, and honestly, so should we.

The experts, like Scholastica Odhiambo from Maseno University, explain it’s a complex mess. Iran hits US allies, who are oil producers, and the supply chain gets twisted like a kanga. Professor XN Iraki from UoN spells it out plainly: wars in Ukraine and Iran mean higher fuel and wheat prices, hitting our wallets and jobs. The government is in emergency meetings, trying to stop this economic crisis from turning into a political disaster before next year’s elections. President Ruto keeps assuring us the subsidy is working, that we have enough fuel, and that profiteers will be dealt with. We’ve heard it all before.

This isn't even the first rodeo. We're still reeling from the Ukraine war, which jacked up wheat and fertiliser prices, and indirectly, fuel costs. Remember when the then-President Kenyatta famously asked if he was in Ukraine or Wuhan when prices shot up? Funny how wars thousands of miles away can feel so personal when your grocery bill doubles. Back in March 2022, after sanctions on Russian oil, Brent crude hit $139 a barrel. Kenya’s fuel subsidy, which was supposedly cushioning us, buckled. We saw record high prices then, and now, it seems the government’s pockets are empty, leaving us exposed to the full brunt of global price shocks. This whole energy crisis is just another reminder that our problems aren't always homegrown; sometimes, they're imported with a hefty price tag.

And it’s not just motorists. Higher diesel prices mean transport costs go up, machinery costs go up, and manufacturing costs go up. It’s a vicious cycle that fuels inflation, especially when families are already struggling with rising food prices. We’re constantly emerging from one external crisis only to be smacked by another. This constant barrage of global events affecting our daily lives is exhausting. As the General Election looms, and this US-Israel war on Iran continues to unfold, you can bet the government will be scrambling to keep this energy crisis from becoming a full-blown political catastrophe. They’ll need to dig deeper, but let’s be real, the money rarely trickles down to us.

It's a sad state of affairs when faraway conflicts dictate whether your children eat properly. We've seen reports of families skipping more meals, and a staggering 2.7 million Kenyans facing high levels of acute food insecurity. Some people are even being approached by "intermediaries" claiming they can secure compensation for families of those killed in these distant wars, for millions. It's a circus. This entire mess highlights just how vulnerable we are, and how the "global village" we've embraced has also made us susceptible to every storm brewing elsewhere. We’re not just Kenyans; we're global pawns in this economic game.
