So, Kenya Railways and their top dog, Philip Mainga, thought they could just wave a magic wand and make the whole Maai Mahiu flood disaster disappear from the courts. 76 lives lost, buildings washed away, and these guys wanted to freeze investigations into their possible criminal responsibility? Ha! The High Court saw through that rubbish, telling them their excuse of "substantial loss" was thinner than a politician's promise. They failed to convince anyone that the wheels of justice grinding slowly would somehow break them.

The court basically said, "You built this mess, you face the music." The ruling keeps alive the possibility for victims to take matters into their own hands and pursue private prosecution if the Director of Public Prosecutions and the police continue their usual song and dance. It’s a slap in the face to the idea that state property failure is just "an Act of God." Apparently, when a government embankment bursts, it’s more like an "Act of Greed" or "Act of Negligence."

What’s really telling is the judge's comparison to the Solai Dam disaster. Back then, with a privately owned dam, charges were filed faster than a bribe can change hands. But with a state-owned railway embankment? Suddenly, investigations crawl at a snail's pace. This selective treatment, as the judge pointed out, stinks of discrimination. It’s the same old story: when it’s theirs, they drag their feet; when it's someone else's, they pounce. It's a stark reminder of how [Kenya Police Unleash Tear Gas, Arrest Ex-Chief Justice at National Park Protest](/posts/kenya-police-unleash-tear-gas--arrest-ex-chief-justice-at-national-park-protest), showing how different rules apply depending on who you are.

Kenya Railways and Mainga were hoping to escape the consequences by appealing. They argued that the court's timelines would expose them to prosecution before their appeal could even be heard. But the judge wasn't buying it, stating they hadn't shown any real "substantial loss" beyond the inconvenience of potentially facing consequences for their alleged failures. The court also wisely noted that it's the Court of Appeal's job to decide if the appeal is even worth listening to, not this court trying to stall for time.

The victims, bless their determined souls, pointed out that Kenya Railways failed to maintain the railway tunnel despite knowing the risks of flooding. They believe senior officials, including the former MD, should be held criminally accountable for their negligence. Meanwhile, the DPP is playing the waiting game, claiming they can't prosecute without a completed police file. The police? Oh, they're "investigating." It’s all a bit too convenient, isn't it?

This whole saga is just another episode of "Justice Delayed is Justice Denied," especially when state institutions are involved. The victims are left to pick up the pieces while those responsible try to shield themselves behind legal loopholes and bureaucratic red tape. Let's see if this time, the "state-managed property" comes with state-managed accountability, or if it’s just more hot air and empty promises from Nairobi.
