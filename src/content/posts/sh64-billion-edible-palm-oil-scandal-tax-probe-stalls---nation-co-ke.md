Sh64 Billion Palm Oil Scam Probe Goes AWOL: Parliament Too Busy Counting Pockets

A Sh64 billion missing revenue scandal involving misdeclared edible palm oil is exactly where you’d expect it to be: nowhere. Two years after Parliament decided to grace us with an investigation into this colossal loss, the National Assembly’s Finance and National Planning Committee has gone completely silent. Apparently, tracking down billions stolen by dodgy importers and possibly complicit officials is too much hard work, especially when key witnesses, like the former KRA boss Humphrey Wattanga, are too busy being ‘unavailable’ or submitting paperwork that’s apparently as shady as the oil itself. And let’s not forget the National Treasury CS, John Mbadi, who is allegedly more of an obstruction than a facilitator. Typical.

The whole charade kicked off with whispers of billions vanishing through palm oil misdeclarations at the Mombasa Port. The parliamentary committee, under Molo MP Kuria Kimani, launched their “probe” with much fanfare. But now? Crickets. Wattanga, the man who should have answers, couldn’t even get his documents right, or perhaps he just chose not to. Requests for his appearance were met with delays and no-shows, a classic Nairobi dance. It’s almost as if they *don’t want* this solved. The chairperson himself can’t even commit to when, or if, this report will ever see the light of day. We’re left wondering if this is just another elaborate theatre to distract us from the real rot.

The numbers themselves are enough to make your stomach churn. The Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) laid it bare: Sh16.5 billion lost in 2022, Sh32.54 billion in 2023, and another Sh13.83 billion this year, all thanks to some clever blending of crude and refined palm oil. They declare the whole lot as crude, bypassing the hefty 35% import duty on refined products. It’s a two-pronged attack on our pockets and the economy, hurting local industries that are supposed to be protected by these duties. It’s no wonder these scams thrive; it’s just too easy when the system is designed to be gamed. It reminds me of how [The Illusion of Safety: Engineering the Next Financial Trap](/posts/the-illusion-of-safety--engineering-the-next-financial-trap) was supposed to protect us, but clearly, someone’s always finding a loophole.

The corruption isn't limited to just the KRA. The committee was supposed to grill everyone from KEBS and AFA to KPA and even private labs. And then there are the big names implicated: Vipingo, Mazeras, ACEE, Mvita Oils, and LDC Kenya, which is apparently a repeat offender, misdeclaring shipments for Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Rwanda. The PBO document explicitly states a "large-scale tax evasion scheme" is happening at Mombasa Port. But when these entities are meant to appear before a committee that’s paralyzed by its own internal squabbles and missing witnesses, what do you expect? It's a circus, and we're the clowns footing the bill.

The sheer audacity of it all is breathtaking. By declaring refined oil as crude, importers not only dodge hefty import duties in Kenya but also benefit from lower export taxes from countries like Indonesia and Malaysia. They get a double whammy of savings, while the Kenyan taxpayer gets a slap in the face. This isn't just a tax evasion scheme; it's a well-oiled international operation that our so-called oversight bodies can't seem to touch. It’s disheartening, as one MP put it, to see investigations disappear because people "cannot honour the committee’s summons." Disheartening? It’s infuriating.

With the committee chairperson not even responding to inquiries and other members expressing utter frustration, the message is clear: Sh64 billion is just pocket change for some. The National Treasury CS is allegedly playing hide-and-seek, and everyone else involved seems to have vanished into thin air. This committee has "lost meaning," as one MP rightly pointed out. It’s just another grand spectacle of incompetence and corruption, proving once again that when it comes to protecting public funds, our institutions are more interested in grandstanding than in actually doing their job.
