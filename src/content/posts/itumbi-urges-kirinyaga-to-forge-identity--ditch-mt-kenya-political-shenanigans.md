Itumbi Urges Kirinyaga to Forge Identity, Ditch Mt Kenya Political Shenanigans

Kirinyaga residents are being told to ditch the usual political tribalism and focus on their own "development priorities." Presidential adviser Dennis Itumbi, bless his hopeful heart, wants them to stop being just another cog in the Mt Kenya political machine and forge their own path. He suggests they should organize around issues like agriculture and roads, and make politicians beg for their vote, instead of the other way around. Apparently, "We are neither west nor east, we are Kirinyaga" is the new rallying cry. Let's see how long that lasts before someone whips out a party slogan.

Itumbi’s grand vision includes turning rice farming into an "African success story." Good luck with that, especially when you consider the state of our roads. He points out the grim reality: 1 kilometer of road in Kirinyaga serves 5,000 people, compared to Nyeri's 1,000. That's a lot of bumpy rides and a lot of people crammed onto inadequate infrastructure. This isn't a new problem, just another symptom of a system that prioritizes political posturing over actual infrastructure development. It’s the same old story we've seen time and again, like watching [Kenya's Economy Forecasted Lower, Factory Prices Rise: More Gloom, Less Boom](/posts/kenya-s-economy-forecasted-lower--factory-prices-rise--more-gloom--less-boom) unfold.

The adviser also harps on the need for qualified Kirinyaga professionals to get government appointments. As if that’s a novel concept. We’ve got plenty of intelligent people, but getting them into positions of power often involves navigating a cesspool of political debts and ethnic bargaining. Itumbi’s plea to present priorities "neutrally" sounds nice in theory, but in practice, it’s a direct challenge to the established order. Politicians don't like their agendas being dictated to them; they're used to handing them down from on high.

He even throws in Martha Karua as an example of someone who *would* be president if Kirinyaga had organized better. That's a bit rich, isn't it? It's the same old song and dance: blame the voters for not being politically savvy enough. Itumbi wants to "change the game of politics," where the people tell leaders what they need. But let's be honest, the game has been rigged for decades, and trying to rewrite the rules with well-intentioned speeches is like trying to bail out a sinking ship with a teacup. The 2027 election is just around the corner, and I doubt this fresh narrative will magically untangle the mess of tribal allegiances and briefcase parties.

Itumbi’s critique of politicians rallying around "Chama" (party) is spot on. This constant manipulation, this "slogania way into development," is precisely what keeps us stuck. He wants a new narrative for Kirinyaga. Fine. But creating that narrative, and more importantly, enforcing it against the entrenched interests of party politics and regional power plays, is the real challenge. Expecting politicians to suddenly align with the people's agenda when their entire career is built on the current system is a fool's errand.
