So, Kenya's big wigs are gushing about Artificial Intelligence, how it's going to "transform" public services. Sounds grand, doesn't it? Except, according to Mary N. Kerema at WSO2Con Africa 2026, we've basically put lipstick on a pig. We've got the "digital front door" - apps, portals, chatbots, all that jazz to make you *think* things are modern. But behind that polished facade? It's the same old Nairobi mess: fragmented systems, people shuffling papers, data all over the place like a drunk's wallet. They call it an "integration gap." I call it the usual government incompetence dressed up in fancy tech jargon.

This whole "agentic AI" thing, where AI actually *does* stuff instead of just spouting facts, is supposed to be the next big leap. But if the systems can't talk to each other, how is an AI agent supposed to, say, get you that permit or sort out that land issue? It needs to access identity, pull data, interact with multiple departments - all the things that our current digital "infrastructure" is spectacularly bad at. It’s like asking a waiter to serve a meal when the kitchen is in shambles and the ingredients are scattered across town. We're talking about "one citizen, one journey, many governments," but right now it's more like "one confused citizen, many frustrating journeys, and no actual government coordination." Remember the Ksh.103 billion SHA mess involving Gachagua and Duale? That's the kind of operational chaos we're talking about, just with more robots.

Kerema makes a big deal about "agentic capability versus unrestricted autonomy." Basically, can the AI actually *do* things, or is it just a fancy calculator? The dream is "orchestration," where systems work together. The reality for us is likely more "automation," where a computer just follows a set of broken rules. And even if they manage to build these agents, they'll need boundaries, permissions, and human oversight. Which, knowing how things run here, will probably mean more bureaucracy and loopholes than actual efficiency. The governance question isn't *if* they'll use AI, but *how* they'll mess it up with accountability.

And let's not forget the foundations. Connectivity, energy, reliable data - the stuff that makes the "digital front door" even open. It’s all a bit shaky, isn’t it? Especially the data. If the information is dodgy, or locked in silos, what good is an AI agent? It's like trying to build a skyscraper on quicksand. Kerema talks about reusable capabilities and interoperability. That sounds nice, but it means actually redesigning things, not just slapping on a new app. And designing for "African operating conditions" - uneven connectivity, informal economies - that’s a whole other can of worms they'll probably try to gloss over with a few focus groups.

The real shift, as Kerema points out, is that agentic AI means we can't just tick boxes on digital forms anymore. It demands that the actual systems behind the services work. And that’s the problem the government has been kicking down the road for years. They’ve digitised the paperwork, but the actual machinery of government? Still stuck in the analog era, just with a bit of digital polish. So, while they’re busy talking about AI strategies and policies, the fundamental question remains: can the systems actually connect, be trusted, and be governed enough for these AI agents to do anything useful? Don't hold your breath.

Ultimately, Africa's "agentic AI opportunity" hinges on fixing that messy operating layer. It's not about the fancy AI models; it's about whether the core infrastructure can actually handle it. And based on our track record, I wouldn't bet my last shilling on it.
