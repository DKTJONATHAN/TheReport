"Nataka cement": Nadia Mukami's Unfinished Rental Apartments - Another Kenyan Dream in Progress?

Nadia Mukami, the 'sweet love' singer, is showing off her Kika Apartments project. She's posted some footage of the construction, looking like a skeleton of dreams and cement. Apparently, she wants "cement na chuma" - because what else do you need to build overpriced boxes for people to rent? It's another Kenyan artiste trying to squeeze money out of property, as if our streets aren't already filled with half-finished buildings and broken promises.

The singer claims this multimillion project is for her children, a "secure home" she's funding alone after her split from Arrow Bwoy. She's put other "investment plans" on hold because she "staki pressure." Honestly, who believes this? It sounds like another way to flex and maybe try to make some headlines, just like those [celebrity parenting stunts](/posts/oga-obinna-s-viral-parenting--kenyan-celebrity-culture-s-latest-gimmick) we keep seeing. Building a legacy through property is fine, but let's not pretend it's all altruism and no profit motive.

We've seen this movie before. Young, talented Kenyans making a quick buck and then scrambling to turn it into something "tangible." Nadia's done well for herself, no doubt, but this whole "building for my kids" narrative feels a bit too convenient, especially with the whispers about her financial situation and past projects. Is this a solid investment or just another speculative gamble in the Nairobi concrete jungle?

The footage shows the "skeletal structure." Yes, it's a building. It's also empty and probably costing a fortune to keep it from becoming a haven for [street gangs](/posts/kitengela-police-estate-gang-raid-a--shock--daylight-attack--leaves-cash-trail). She's calling it progress. I call it a work in progress, which in Kenya often means a work that will never truly be finished, or will be finished to a standard that makes you question your life choices.

This whole "financial legacy" talk for young Kenyans is a nice sentiment, but the reality is often much harsher. Many are chasing quick riches and ending up with more debt than assets. Nadia's KSh 50 million project, funded independently, sounds impressive, but what happens when the cement runs out and the "pressure" becomes too much? We'll be watching, and waiting to see if Kika Apartments are a genuine success or just another monument to Kenyan ambition that crumbles under its own weight.

She says she's doing it alone because she "staki pressure." The irony of building a massive rental complex and then claiming you want to avoid pressure is lost on me. This is Nairobi, darling. Pressure is the oxygen we breathe. And for Nadia, it seems the pressure to stay relevant and financially afloat is just as real as the need for cement and steel.
