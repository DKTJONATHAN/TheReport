So, Ruto’s big plan to make university education "universal" is apparently happening in less than a month. Except, nobody seems to have a clue how it's actually going to work. Universities are already calling students to report for the 2026/2027 academic year, with some starting as early as August 20th. Meanwhile, Parliament is still fiddling with a bill that’s supposed to replace HELB, the Universities Fund, and the TVET board with some shiny new entity called TEFA. It’s the classic Kenyan government special: announce a grand vision, then scramble to figure out the details later. Classic.

President Ruto, bless his cotton socks, is out there saying it doesn’t matter what background a child comes from, only how good they are. Easy for him to say. What matters is that thousands of students will be trooping onto campuses before this magical new funding framework is even approved, let alone implemented. Are they going to use the old system? The new one? Or will they just wing it and hope for the best? It’s like expecting a child to start school without knowing if they’ll have a desk or a teacher. This is the same old song and dance we’ve seen before, a bit like when they promised big infrastructure projects that turned out to be just a bigger plate of nothing, as covered in [Sh5 Trillion For The Boys: The New National Infrastructure Fund Is Just A Bigger Plate](/posts/sh5-trillion-for-the-boys--the-new-national-infrastructure-fund-is-just-a-bigger-plate).

The people in charge, like HELB CEO Geoffrey Monari and UF acting CEO Dr. Edwin Wanyonyi, are telling everyone not to worry their pretty little heads. “Applications are ongoing,” they chirp. “We just follow the law of the day.” So, the old system is still chugging along, processing loans and scholarships as if nothing is changing. They'll "adapt accordingly" once Parliament finally gets its act together. Meanwhile, HELB is apparently facing a Sh57 billion deficit. So, what exactly are they processing with? Hope and good intentions? It’s a miracle any of these students get anything at all.

The proposed solution involves a convoluted scheme to raise Sh100 billion annually from the government, investors, parents, graduates, and anyone else they can shake down. They’re talking about education bonds, sinking funds, investment banking, and listing savings on the Nairobi Securities Exchange. It sounds fancy, but what it really means is more layers of bureaucracy and more opportunities for things to go wrong. And let's not forget the Sh57 billion hole HELB is already in. Where is this extra Sh100 billion going to come from? Pull it out of thin air, I suppose.

This new Tertiary Education Funding Authority is supposed to administer everything, creating a unified plan for universities, colleges, and TVETs. They’ll issue scholarships, loans, and even try to mobilize resources from treasury bills and pension funds. It's a massive undertaking, and knowing how these things usually go in Kenya, it will be riddled with delays, mismanagement, and probably some shady deals. The government’s track record suggests this elaborate plan will likely end up being another expensive flop, leaving students and their parents even more disillusioned.

At the end of the day, it's the students who are caught in the middle of this mess. They’ve worked hard, gotten their admission letters, and are preparing to start their academic journey, only to be met with a cloud of uncertainty about how they’ll pay for it. This isn't "universal education"; it's a chaotic scramble for funds that highlights the government's inability to plan and execute even the most basic services. You can almost hear the collective sigh of exasperation from every parent trying to figure out how to get their child through university.
