"Market Walls" Crumble: Corporates Forced to Share Their Fridges, But Don't Hold Your Breath

The Kenyan market is being hailed as a liberal utopia where businesses are suddenly doing each other favours. EABL, the giant beverage maker, is now being told to share its precious fridge space with rivals. Just months ago, mobile money platforms were forced into some semblance of interoperability. This is presented as progress, a win for consumers. But let's not pretend this is about fairness; it's about regulators finally getting a grip, albeit slowly.

The Competition Authority of Kenya (CAK) has waved its magic wand, approving Diageo's Sh304.6 billion sale of a 65 percent stake in EABL to Asahi, but with a catch: at least 20 percent of those coveted fridge spots must go to non-EABL or Asahi brands. This comes after the CAK identified "competition and public interest concerns," which is corporate speak for EABL and its soon-to-be former owner hogging all the prime real estate in retail outlets. They've been using their own refrigerators as a fortress, keeping smaller players out.

Now, this directive on refrigeration space is supposed to maintain competition. It's also meant to ensure fair access for consumers, who, according to this narrative, will now have more choices. Funny how "fair access" usually means forcing the big players to loosen their stranglehold, rather than them voluntarily deciding to be generous. It's a classic case of powerful entities making their own rules until someone steps in. We’ve seen similar battles play out before, like the push for [mobile money interoperability](/posts/explained--what-happens-to-ruto-s-presidency-after-high-court-s-election-ruling---kenyans-co-ke), where the Central Bank had to prod telcos into action.

The exception to this fridge-sharing rule - top-end establishments, supermarkets, liquor stores at petrol stations, and hotels above two stars - tells you where the real power lies. These are the places where branding and exclusivity matter most, and where the giants can continue to lord over their domain. The cost of those branded fridges, the monitoring, the maintenance - it all adds up, and it’s a barrier to entry. This rule is a small crack in that wall, not a demolition.

And let's not forget the mobile money saga. Safaricom opened up its tills, then Airtel followed, a supposed victory for seamless transactions. But the reality on the ground is always more complex. While these "market walls" might be falling on paper, the underlying power dynamics and the constant struggle for market dominance remain. It's a constant dance between corporate greed and regulatory intervention, with the public often caught in the middle, hoping for genuine benefits rather than just a reshuffle of the deck.
