So, the KSh64.5 million dished out by Mdundo sounds like pocket change for a nation, doesn't it? For 150,000 creatives, that's barely enough for a decent cup of chai and a mandazi, let alone building a career. These digital platforms, they talk a good game about supporting local talent, but the reality for most upcoming artists is a constant struggle to even get noticed, let alone make a living wage from streams that pay peanuts. It's like being offered a single grain of rice when you're starving.

And then there are the live shows, the supposed saviors of the struggling artist. But don't fool yourself, promoters aren't running a charity. They want proof you can pack the joint, which means you need an audience before you can even get on stage. It's a vicious cycle, a catch-22 that keeps aspiring musicians trapped in obscurity. They need the gigs to build fans, but they need fans to get the gigs. It's a setup designed to favour those already on the inside, leaving the rest to fight for scraps. Reminds me of how even basic infrastructure projects can go sideways, like that [Poison at the Pump: Kenya's Fuel Marking System A Cancer Risk, Millions Exposed](/posts/poison-at-the-pump--kenya-s-fuel-marking-system-a-cancer-risk--millions-exposed) debacle, where the promises don't match the reality.

The live music market might be raking in a million dollars, but how much of that trickles down to the actual artists, especially the ones who aren't household names? The numbers might look impressive on paper, but for the emerging musician, securing a paid performance is an Olympic sport. You need to be more than just a singer or a guitarist; you need to be a marketing guru, a social media influencer, and a shrewd negotiator, all rolled into one. It’s a full-time job just to get your foot in the door.

Forget about just having talent. In this digital age, the modern Kenyan musician is a one-person band of hustle. They're expected to be their own manager, publicist, and accountant, all while churning out catchy tunes. This isn't about passion anymore; it's a full-blown business venture where the product is your soul, and the investors are an indifferent public scrolling through endless content. It's a tough gig, and most of them are likely to end up more jaded than famous.
