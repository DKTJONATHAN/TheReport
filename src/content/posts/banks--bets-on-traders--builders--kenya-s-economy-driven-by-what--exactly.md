So, the banks are throwing more than half their new cash at traders and builders. Sh195.5 billion, that's 54.22 percent of new credit, went to commerce and construction up to May 2026. Meanwhile, the manufacturers, the folks who supposedly make things, are busy paying back loans. Makes you wonder if they've finally realised that churning out goods is a lot harder than moving them or slapping them up.

Trade is the real kingpin here, swallowing Sh153.5 billion. Why? Because traders move inventory, they need working capital, and that's quick money for the banks. Construction is the second favourite, raking in Sh42 billion. Apparently, the government finally decided to pay some bills for stalled road projects - a predictable boom that's more about clearing old debts than creating sustainable growth. It’s all about domestic demand and government projects, the easy wins.

This shift comes after a bit of monetary easing, with lending rates dropping, but they're still a far cry from what they were before the global chaos. Banks aren't just lending willy-nilly; they're selectively directing funds, betting on sectors that generate cash fast. So, if you're in manufacturing, transport, or real estate, tough luck. Those sectors are seen as too risky, too slow, or too exposed to those ever-rising costs. It’s a stark divide, favouring quick returns over long-term investment.

The governor, Dr. Thugge, says manufacturers are repaying loans because of "net loan repayments" in April and May. In plain English, they're paying back more than they're borrowing. It's like saying a drought is because people are drinking less water. The real issue is that the sector is still struggling, weighed down by taxes, high electricity, and a global market that doesn't play fair. Even with policy efforts, it's a long way back from the Covid days, as KCB’s CFO pointed out. The Kenya Association of Manufacturers’ agenda reads like a cry for help, listing everything from heavy taxation to counterfeit goods.

Agriculture, surprisingly, is also getting a slice of the pie, with credit rising a significant 32 percent. Favourable weather is the official reason, but banks have always been wary of farming. This increase, coupled with more lending to consumer durables, points to an economy increasingly reliant on food, distribution, and what households can scrape together. It's a domestic-focused recovery, fueled by consumption and the hope that people will keep buying, despite the constant cost pressures. It’s a bit like that Kipchoge Keino Stadium story - a lot of talk, some progress, but nowhere near a complete revival.

Ultimately, this isn't a broad-based recovery; it's a reallocation. Banks aren't leaving the economy, they're just picking the winners, the sectors they believe will cough up cash faster. So, while the numbers might look like growth, it's a growth built on moving existing stuff and slapping up buildings, not on creating new industries or expanding what we already have. It’s the Nairobi realist's dream: quick returns, less risk, and everyone else just has to hope for the best.
