Ruto: Judge Our Health Reforms By Patients' Experience, Not Empty Promises

WARNING: The government is patting itself on the back for health sector "reforms" while Kenyans continue to suffer. Promises of dependable, affordable, and dignified care are just that - promises.






So, the president wants us to judge their "rosy picture" of healthcare by listening to the "experiences of Kenyans." Well, Mr. President, the experience on the ground is that this system is still a shambles. We’re told of promises delivered, of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) being revived from its "deathbed," and of four new laws birthing the Social Health Authority (SHA). Millions are supposedly registered, and millions have received treatment, including safe deliveries and surgeries. It sounds like a fairy tale, doesn't it? It's the same old song and dance, just with new acronyms.

The talk about UHC and economic empowerment feels particularly hollow. Illness wiping out savings and pushing families into poverty is the reality for many Kenyans who can’t afford decent healthcare *now*. The SHA, trumpeted as the solution, is apparently helping over 8 million Kenyans. Great. But what about the millions more who are still struggling? And let's not forget the ongoing strikes by healthcare workers - how does that fit into this "progress"? It just proves that reforms are not events, but indeed a continuous journey of closing the distance between the glossy promises and the grim reality.

Even on the sidelines, the cracks are showing. Experts point out that while policy might be written beautifully on paper, consistent implementation at the point of care is still a pipe dream. We're talking about sustainable financing, adequate supplies, a supported workforce, and functional systems - basics that seem to elude our leaders. It’s like building a mansion with a leaky roof; looks good from afar, but you’ll get soaked when the rains come. Some of these policies remain on paper because, surprise, surprise, resources aren’t aligned. It’s a familiar story, much like how [Kenyans Use Google Search and AI to Master the Technical Skills Needed for the Future, Apparently] - a lot of talk, but the follow-through is questionable.

Then there's the issue of financing for critical areas like HIV and TB. Donor funding is drying up, and counties are scrambling. The SHA’s initial packages didn’t even include HIV services, a glaring oversight given its prevalence. And let’s not even start on the industrial strikes and the abysmal state of Human Resources for Health (HRH) management. Nepotism, exploitation, burnout, depression, and even suicide among health workers - this isn't a health system; it's a crisis management exercise. The proposed centralized HRH management and a Health Service Commission sound good, but history teaches us to be skeptical.

The Council of Governors chair blames "toxicity of politics," while union officials highlight fragmented leadership, lack of adherence to legal and policy imperatives, and a failure to meet even basic national and international targets like the Abuja Declaration. Public health activities are still struggling for funding and embrace, with low absorption of officers and specialists. Food safety and border disease surveillance are particularly hit hard, especially with ongoing strikes. It seems everyone agrees there's a problem, but the solutions are as fragmented as the system itself. The suggestion to have governors handle primary healthcare and the national government handle curative care might be a glimmer of hope, but then again, we've heard a lot of suggestions before.
