###

So, NCBA Group is out here playing patron saint, sponsoring ten Kenyan creators for some "entrepreneurship programme." Apparently, the goal is to equip these artists with the business skills to build "sustainable enterprises." Because what every starving artist in Nairobi needs is more jargon and less actual cash. The Burns Brothers and Strathmore University Business School are apparently involved, so expect a lot of talk about "unlocking potential" and "bridging gaps." We've heard this song before, haven't we?

The program, Creative Economy 101, promises to teach them the nitty-gritty: business management, financial planning, intellectual property protection. Oh, and enterprise growth. As if a few online modules are going to magically turn a painter who can barely afford canvases into a mogul. Mike Burns, bless his optimistic heart, claims Africa's creative sector has "enormous untapped potential." Sure, it does. It also has a massive untapped demand for basic survival. He’s talking about creativity becoming Africa’s next big export. We’ll see if that’s before or after we solve the problem of people being able to afford food.

This whole thing is being delivered through the "Creative Economy Africa Institute," which is apparently going to create a million jobs by 2035. A million jobs. Right. Because that's how economic growth works. You just declare it into existence. UNESCO estimates the global creative economy is worth billions, but Africa barely gets a sniff. And why? Structural barriers. Fancy word for "nobody in power actually cares enough to fix anything." It's almost as if we're dealing with the same systemic issues as those revealed in leaked chats exposing the daily life of a scam compound’s enslaved workforce.

Strathmore University Business School's Executive Dean, Dr. Caesar Mwangi, is all about universities "professionalising" the creative economy. Professionalising. Such a dry word for what should be about passion and expression. He also mentioned collaboration between academia and industry. Translation: more meetings, more reports, more degrees that don't guarantee a job. Nelly Wainaina from NCBA says banks view creators as entrepreneurs. If only they viewed them as people who need to pay their bills *now*, not in some nebulous future of "economic growth."

The seven-module curriculum, certified by Strathmore (because everything needs a certificate these days), covers scaling, finance, IP, and market expansion. Pretty standard stuff. The organisers insist on "sustained collaboration" between universities, governments, investors, and the private sector. What that really means is more committees, more talking, and less tangible support for the actual creators struggling to make rent. Let's be real, this is a PR exercise for NCBA, a way to look good while the real economic barriers remain firmly in place.
