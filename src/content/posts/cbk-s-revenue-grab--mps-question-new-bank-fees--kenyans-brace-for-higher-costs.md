CBK's New Bank Fees: Another Way To Squeeze Kenyans Dry?

The Central Bank of Kenya's (CBK) shiny new plan to change how banks are licensed has the smell of trouble, with MPs already kicking up a fuss. They're worried about whether the whole thing is even legal, and, surprise, surprise, if it's going to cost us more to keep our hard-earned cash in a bank. The CBK claims their fancy new revenue-based fees are the bees' knees, a huge improvement on the old dusty system. But when has the government ever come up with a plan that *doesn't* end up digging deeper into our pockets?

Apparently, the CBK looked at other ways to charge banks, like based on their deposits or assets, but decided those would hurt the banks too much - heaven forbid any of them actually struggle. So, they settled on gross annual revenue, a move that conveniently allows them to tap into a bigger pot of money. The MPs are rightly asking if this even has a leg to stand on legally, pointing out that the Banking Act doesn't exactly mention "banking fees" in this newfangled way. It’s a classic Kenyan move: change the name, change the game, and hope nobody notices the extra charges popping up.

Then there's the calculation of this "gross annual revenue." The MPs are practically screaming, "Are you going to include customer deposits in this?" Because if so, that’s just adding insult to injury. Dr. Thugge, bless his cotton socks, tried to reassure them by listing all the things that *will* be counted, like interest income and foreign exchange trading. It all sounds very official, but the bottom line is the CBK wants more money to beef up their supervision - probably to catch up with all the new ways banks find to fleece us, like those pesky [cybersecurity threats](/posts/pro-iran-meme-machine-trolls-trump-with-ai-lego-cartoons--a-cynical-take-on-digital-warfare) that are now all the rage.

And for the new kids on the block, the banks that are just getting their licenses? They have to pay fees based on projected revenue for their first three years. Realistic projections, you say? With these kinds of regulations, I wouldn't bet my last shilling on that. Plus, if you miss a payment deadline, you pay double, and then they can take your license. Talk about a slap on the wrist followed by a swift kick in the teeth. It’s a bit much, even for Kenyan standards. The old system, where you paid a flat fee, might have been outdated, but at least it was predictable. Now, it seems the CBK is just trying to catch up with other countries that are already using these revenue models, probably because they see a sweet opportunity to increase their own coffers.

The parliamentary committee is still chewing on this, and good for them. They’re even talking to banks and consumers - imagine that, actually listening to the people who will be affected. Let's hope they see this for what it is: another attempt to create a new revenue stream that will inevitably trickle down to us, the poor banking public. Because no matter how you slice it, when the banks have to pay more, guess who ends up footing the bill?







The Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) is facing a grilling from MPs over its proposed overhaul of bank licensing fees, and frankly, it smells like another Kenyan money-making scheme. The parliamentarians are raising serious questions about the legal basis for these new fees and, more importantly, whether they're going to end up making our banking services more expensive. The CBK is touting this as a modern solution, a move away from the outdated flat-fee system that doesn't reflect how banks actually operate these days. But when has the government ever introduced a new fee without it eventually landing squarely on the taxpayer's shoulders?

The CBK claims they've explored all the options, including deposit- and asset-based models, but apparently, those would have hit the banks' profitability too hard. So, they've landed on gross annual revenue, a move that conveniently allows them to tap into a potentially much larger pot of money. The Vice-Chairperson of the parliamentary committee, Robert Githinji, is rightly questioning if the Banking Act even supports this newfangled "banking fees" terminology, suggesting it could face legal challenges. It's a classic move: rebrand something to make it sound new and improved, while the underlying goal is still just to extract more money.

Then there’s the nitty-gritty of how this "gross annual revenue" will be calculated. MPs are understandably concerned that customer deposits could be swept into the calculation, significantly inflating the fees banks have to pay. Dr. Thugge assured the committee that it would be based on audited revenue streams like interest income and fees, but one has to wonder how broadly those definitions might be interpreted. With the increasing complexity of risks banks face, from cybersecurity threats to AI, the CBK says it needs more resources for supervision. It’s a convenient excuse to tighten the screws a little further.

For newly licensed banks, the prospect of paying annual fees based on projected revenue for their first three years before they even start making a profit is particularly concerning. The CBK argues it’s the only practical way, but the lack of a clear mechanism to assess whether those projections are realistic is a red flag. And the kicker? Missing the annual payment deadline means double the fee within 90 days, followed by the threat of losing your banking license. It’s a disproportionate sanction, as one MP pointed out, showcasing the CBK's rather aggressive approach to enforcing its new rules. It’s a far cry from the current KSh 400,000 flat fee for a head office, and it feels like we're heading towards a system where only the biggest banks can afford to operate, squeezing out smaller players and potentially reducing competition - much like how one might struggle to get decent service at those new [Airtel customer care centres](/posts/airtel-opens-four-new-nairobi-customer-care-centres---your-new-lines-to-wait-on).

The CBK is adamant that the old model is outdated, especially with the shift to digital banking, and that their current fees are the lowest among domestic financial regulators. They point to Uganda and Rwanda as examples of countries already using gross annual revenue models. But the parliamentary committee is wisely continuing its examination, indicating they might even seek views from banks and consumers before tabling their report. Let's hope they remember that the ultimate beneficiaries of these banking fees are the ones who have to pay them, and that doesn't bode well for the average Kenyan.
