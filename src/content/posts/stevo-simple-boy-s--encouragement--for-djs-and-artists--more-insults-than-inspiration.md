Stevo Simple Boy's "Advice" to New Talent: A Recipe for Disappointment

Don't get it twisted, Stevo Simple Boy might think he's dropping pearls of wisdom for the aspiring DJs and musicians of Nairobi. But let's be real, his "encouragement" sounds more like a thinly veiled plea for airplay and collabs, served with a side of patronizing realism. He talks about "ups and downs," "criticism and insults," as if that’s some groundbreaking revelation. Welcome to the Nairobi entertainment scene, folks. It’s always been a dog-eat-dog world, and anyone expecting a smooth ride to stardom is living in a fantasy land.

The core of Stevo's message is simple: "Don't give up." Easy for him to say when he’s already got a name, right? He’s essentially telling these young creatives to endure the rejection, the shady promoters, the sound systems that are always broken, and the constant struggle to even get a gig. It's a tough pill to swallow, and frankly, sometimes giving up is the sanest option when the system is rigged against you. This whole "soft life" dream is a scam for most, and the entertainment industry is no different; it’s more about who you know than how good you are.

His appeal to DJs to play his music and fellow artists to collaborate is where the cynicism really kicks in. He’s not just offering a shoulder to cry on; he’s trying to boost his own brand by leveraging the desperation of newcomers. It’s a classic move in this town - everyone’s out for themselves. The idea that playing his songs will bring "blessings" is pure PR fluff, designed to make him seem generous while subtly reminding everyone of his existence. Just look at Pritty Vishy, who’s already taken a break from social media because of the backlash. This industry chews people up and spits them out.

This whole narrative about "supporting local music" sounds noble, but in practice, it's a brutal climb. Stevo’s advice, while seemingly earnest, conveniently skips over the systemic issues that make it so hard for new talent to break through. It’s not just about perseverance; it’s about having access, resources, and a bit of luck. And let’s not forget the constant pressure and criticism that can leave artists like Pritty Vishy emotionally drained. It’s a harsh reality that needs more than just a pep talk to overcome, and frankly, Stevo’s plea for airtime and collabs doesn't quite cut it.







Stevo Simple Boy, the artist whose name conjures up images of a less-than-polished, yet undeniably persistent presence in the Kenyan music scene, has decided to impart some "wisdom" onto the next generation of DJs and musicians. In a video that’s already circulating faster than gossip at a matatu terminus, he’s urging these hopefuls not to buckle under the weight of criticism and rejection. It’s a noble sentiment, I suppose, but coming from Stevo, it feels less like genuine encouragement and more like a cautionary tale wrapped in a self-serving plea.

He acknowledges the "ups and downs" and the "challenges, criticism and insults" that come with chasing a dream in this concrete jungle. He’s basically saying, "Yeah, it sucks, but keep going!" This is the kind of advice that sounds good on paper but rings hollow when you’re the one facing the slammed doors and the endless stream of “no’s.” In Nairobi, the entertainment industry isn’t just about talent; it’s a brutal arena where only the most connected or the most resilient - or perhaps the most strategically shameless - tend to survive. Stevo’s message is a stark reminder that expecting a smooth ride is like expecting a clean Nairobi River.

Then comes the real kicker: Stevo’s appeal to DJs to spin his tracks and fellow artists to collaborate. Suddenly, his message of perseverance morphs into a blatant call for personal gain. He’s not just sharing his journey; he’s actively trying to leverage the struggles of others to boost his own career. The idea that playing his songs will bring DJs "blessings" is as cynical as it gets. It’s a transactional approach disguised as camaraderie, a common theme in this city’s cutthroat entertainment business. It makes you wonder if the encouragement is genuine or just a clever marketing ploy to get his music heard.

His words echo the recent drama surrounding Pritty Vishy, who, after facing a barrage of negativity for venturing into DJing, announced a social media hiatus. Stevo’s advice, while not mentioning her by name, serves as a stark reminder of the emotional toll this industry takes. He tells them to stay focused, to not lose hope, but he conveniently omits the systemic hurdles and the emotional exhaustion that often accompanies the pursuit of fame. It’s a tough game, and Stevo’s thinly veiled request for airplay doesn’t exactly make it any easier for the true newcomers trying to find their footing in this chaotic landscape. Anyone looking for genuine guidance might be better off looking elsewhere, perhaps at how the [digital banking sector] has reshaped the economy, for a less emotionally taxing pursuit.
