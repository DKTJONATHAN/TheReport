So, Baba is gone, and what's left? A goldmine, apparently. A year after Raila Odinga shuffled off this mortal coil, the real politicians, the ones who thrived in his shadow, are now busy turning his memory into a political ATM. They're digging through his speeches, his manifestos, even his grocery lists, looking for anything to justify their own grubby ambitions ahead of 2027. It's not about his actual vision anymore; it's about who can best twist his words to serve their present political needs. A classic Kenyan playbook, really.

Inside ODM, it’s a free-for-all for the "Baba mantle." Catherine Omanyo is out there telling everyone to be loyal because Raila "left it for us" - left what, exactly? A party full of desperate individuals? And Gladys Wanga is waxing lyrical about his "lasting contribution." Meanwhile, his former allies are off charting their own courses, but they still need a piece of the Raila pie. Everyone's suddenly an expert on what "Baba wanted," conveniently forgetting their own past disagreements. It's all about positioning, folks.

And now, the family is getting in on the act. Mwezi wa Baba, they call it. A whole month of celebrating his "ideals." But then you have the official family events, the candle-lighting, the commemorations. The distinction is deliberate, isn't it? They know Raila was more than just ODM. He was a patriarch, a national figure. But trying to separate his party legacy from his family legacy? That's just another angle to control the narrative, to decide who gets to speak for the deceased. It's a performance, pure and simple.

The timing, of course, is crucial. With 2027 looming, Raila's network is a juicy prize. The question is whether that influence, built over decades, can survive without the man himself. His death left behind a void, and everyone's trying to fill it with their own self-serving interpretations. It's a messy scramble, where "unity" can mean supporting the government or forming a new opposition. The language of legacy is just a weapon in this ongoing political arms race. It makes you wonder if our identity itself isn't just another commodity being sold, much like our [Kenyan passport is now a red flag at every border](/posts/our-identity-for-sale--why-your-kenyan-passport-is-now-a-red-flag-at-every-border).

Ultimately, the real test isn't whether Kenyans remember Raila Odinga. They will. The question is what they'll remember him *for*, and more importantly, who gets to dictate that memory. As October 15 approaches, his legacy is becoming the new language of argument, the currency of political ambition. And in 2027, that argument will likely matter more than any genuine remembrance of the man himself. It's all about the next election, and the next opportunity to exploit the memory of the fallen.
