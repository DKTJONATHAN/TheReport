Nairobi’s elite probably don't spare a thought for who's scrubbing the grime off their designer threads while sipping their overpriced cappuccinos. Lorenzo Group, with its fancy names like Lorenzo Drycleaners and Vogue Drycleaners, is just another Nairobi outfit built on the backs of the desperate. Their Kamiti Two facility isn't a temple of clean clothes; it's a testament to how far management will go to squeeze every last cent out of people, treating them like disposable rags.

The stories coming out are predictable, really. Salaries late, paid in bits, because apparently, the entire workforce is crammed into one bank account. Jacklin Rotich in Accounts and Grace Mwambeo, the General Manager who’s apparently become "irresponsible," can't seem to grasp the concept of paying people when they're due. Then there's Faith Mbai in HR, who thinks tribalism and threats are part of a job description. Workers are told they’re lucky to even have a job, especially when there are masters graduates willing to do it for peanuts. It’s the classic Nairobi playbook: threaten them with unemployment. This whole song and dance about being replaced reminds me of how [some Kenyan men find themselves in dire straits after being lured to fight in Russia](/posts/the-ruble-meat-grinder--why-kenya-exports-sons-to-die-in-russia), only here, the battlefield is a laundry facility.

And the "morning meetings"? Pure theatre for humiliation. Daily targets are jacked up arbitrarily, and if you don’t meet them, you get punished. The ultimate threat? A forced transfer to the opposite end of Nairobi, turning a short commute into a day-long ordeal. Faith Mbai’s charming promise to "rotate them until they can no longer bear it" is just a thinly veiled threat, a reminder that their lives are less important than the company’s bottom line. They’re forced to open accounts with specific banks too, no doubt to line the company's pockets with interest from salary advances. It’s a trap, plain and simple.

Forget about your final dues when your contract ends or you get the boot. That’s when the real Nairobi nightmare begins - a "merry-go-round" of delays that can stretch for over a year. Public holidays? Leave days? Not at Lorenzo. You’re expected to show up, or you’ll get a warning letter. And don't even think about bringing your own lunch; management claims they're "too busy." They operate on a "replacement mentality," constantly reminding staff they're expendable. It’s this arrogance, this casual cruelty, that defines Lorenzo Group, a company that preaches premium care for fabrics but offers none for its own people.

The workers are finally shouting from the rooftops, begging the Ministry of Labour and other authorities to step in. They want fair treatment, accountability, and respect. But in Nairobi, hope is a fragile commodity. We’ve seen these stories before, the shiny promises of growth and opportunity masking a reality of exploitation. Let's see if anyone actually listens, or if this is just another Nairobi tale of the rich getting richer while the poor get poorer.

It's the same old story in this city. Lorenzo Group might be a "flagship division" for some private investment firm, operating out of posh addresses like Kilimani and Westgate, but for the people on the ground, it’s just another employer proving that the real luxury in Nairobi is a fair wage and a bit of dignity.
