So, Nairobi is the "epicentre" of Kenya's startup scene, they say. Access to capital, talent, the whole shebang. Naturally, all the money and bright ideas get sucked into the capital, leaving Mombasa, Kisumu, Nakuru, and Eldoret with crumbs. Now, these counties are waking up, building their little innovation hubs and digital infrastructure. Entrepreneurs are supposedly building businesses around local strengths. But let's be real, is this a genuine shift, or are these regional ecosystems just waiting for Nairobi's leftovers in terms of cash, talent, and the all-important networks? It’s the age-old question, isn't it?

Mombasa, bless its touristy heart, is now being touted as a tech hub because it's a port. Watu Credit is out there dishing out billions in loans for motorcycles and smartphones, expanding to Nigeria and South Africa. StockApp is helping small businesses digitize. Revital Healthcare, Red Splash, Aqualedger - all names whispered from the coast. Mombasa even ranked higher than Nairobi in some global index, with a growth rate that sounds impressive until you realise the baseline was probably closer to zero than Nairobi’s already established base. It's like saying a toddler who learned to walk is faster than an Olympic sprinter.

Then there's Kisumu, "emerging" because it's cheaper and near Lake Victoria. AquaRech is helping fish farmers, AgriBORA and Rafode are doing their thing. They talk about cross-border fintech with Uganda and Tanzania. But the real test, they admit, is whether Kisumu is becoming a genuine startup ecosystem or just building the props for one. Meanwhile, Konza Technopolis, this grand plan for a tech city in Machakos, is attracting billions. Fifty-one startups from twenty. It’s a start, I guess, but is it creating enough jobs and real innovation, or just more government white elephants? We’ve seen similar grand projects before, like that [Office of the President Ambulance Scam](posts/office-of-the-president-ambulance-scam--sh62-million-down-the-drain--swedes-left-holding-empty-pockets).

Nakuru and Eldoret are also eyeing opportunities in agriculture, manufacturing, and healthcare. They’re supposed to leverage their regional economies. But the real kicker, the one they conveniently brush over, is capital. Nairobi-based companies grabbed a staggering 95.7 percent of all the cash raised by Kenyan startups between 2015 and late 2024. Mombasa got a respectable chunk, but the rest? Pocket change. So, while you can cook up a product in Kisumu, scaling it likely still means a pilgrimage to Nairobi for the actual money and the connections that matter. Investors offer more than cash; they offer doors. And those doors are mostly in the capital.

Even with digital businesses going remote, finding experienced engineers and product managers is still a Nairobi game. Forty-four percent of graduate engineers are there. So, these counties are investing in hubs and infrastructure, but is it enough? They need more than just fancy buildings and pronouncements. They need genuine special economic zones, regulatory clarity, and reliable services. The county that actually gets this right, not just on paper, might have a shot. But until then, the real startup boom is still happening in Nairobi, and everyone else is just trying to catch the overflow.
