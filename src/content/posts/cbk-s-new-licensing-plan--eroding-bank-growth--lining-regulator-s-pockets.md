The Central Bank of Kenya has finally decided our big banks are too powerful, too interconnected, too vital, essentially- too successful. So, what's a regulator to do? Slap them with higher capital requirements and new, fatter licensing fees, of course. Apparently, their "growth" was getting a bit too robust, and the CBK felt left out of the party.

The new rules mean the likes of KCB, Equity, and Co-op Bank- the ones who've been raking it in and expanding across the region- now have to hold more capital. Much more. Their minimum core capital ratio could jump from 10.5 percent to a hefty 15.5 percent. And then there are the new licensing fees, which aren't fixed per branch anymore. Oh no, those are now pegged to their *gross revenue*. Imagine that- the more money you make, the more you pay the people regulating you. Convenient, isn't it?

This isn't just about banks holding more cash. It's about what they *can't* do. Dividend payouts, which have been quite generous for shareholders in recent years- remember that Ksh117.2 billion payout in 2025?- are now capped based on capital adequacy. So much for those easy returns. And those grand regional expansion plans? Equity looking at Angola, KCB eyeing Ethiopia? All need CBK approval now. It's almost like the regulator wants to clip their wings, just as they were learning to fly further afield.

The Central Bank claims it needs "more resources" to handle its oversight responsibilities, what with banks growing and all. Funny how those "resources" suddenly look like a massive jump in licence fee collection- from Ksh335 million to over Ksh950 million in the first year alone. They say bank profits might only decline by 0.4 percent from these fees, but that's just the tip of the iceberg. The real cost? Stifled innovation, slower growth, and a regulator with an even tighter grip. It makes you wonder if some of these institutions are just getting a grand, expensive "vacation" from their usual operations, much like [the Navy's Grand Mauritius Vacation](/posts/high-seas-cocktails-and-ghost-ships--the-navy-s-grand-mauritius-vacation) that always seems to find funds for itself.

So, what does it all mean? For the ordinary Kenyan, probably not much change in their daily banking experience, unless you're a shareholder watching your dividends shrink. For the banks, it's a clear message: get too big, get too rich, and the regulator will come knocking, not with a congratulatory handshake, but with a bigger bill and a longer leash. It's not about fostering a robust financial sector; it's about control, and ensuring the Central Bank gets its cut.
