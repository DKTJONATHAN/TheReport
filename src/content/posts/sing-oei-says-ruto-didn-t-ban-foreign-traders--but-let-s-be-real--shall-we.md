So, Le Monde, that fancy French newspaper, splashes a headline saying President Ruto wants all small foreign-owned businesses shut down, and naturally, here comes Principal Secretary Korir Sing’Oei to do damage control. Apparently, the President was just *really* passionate about the Local Content Bill, 2025, and his remarks about telling people to "close them" were merely a robust discussion on parliamentary proceedings. Sure, whatever you say.

Sing’Oei assures us that as long as these foreign nationals have their papers - work permits, licenses, all that jazz - they’re safe and sound to peddle their wares or offer their services. It’s not about them being foreign, it’s about them following the rules. He even specifically points out our brothers and sisters from Burundi and other East African nations, saying they’re free to live and work here, provided they play by Kenya’s laws. It’s almost as if they’re treating us like we’re not capable of understanding basic English.

This whole kerfuffle stems from Ruto’s chat with small-scale traders where he allegedly said, “From next week, all traders doing those small businesses should close them.” He questioned why foreigners should be hawking and running small shops here. This, of course, coincides with the Local Content Bill which is all about giving Kenyans a bigger slice of the pie. The bill wants foreign companies to source 60% of local goods and employ 80% Kenyans. Sounds fair on paper, but we’ve seen this song and dance before.

The truth is, Kenya has always had requirements for foreign nationals - permits, licenses, capital. It’s not a new concept. But the timing and the President’s direct words certainly fanned the flames of existing anxieties among Kenyan traders. We’ve had tensions brewing, whispers of xenophobia, and now this. It’s a messy situation, and Sing’Oei’s clarification feels like trying to put toothpaste back in the tube. It's all about enforcing laws, he says, not outright exclusion. Easy for him to say from his government office; the ground reality for many Kenyan traders is a constant struggle for space. This feels like just another chapter in our ongoing saga of economic aspirations and practical realities, much like the financial tightrope we saw the Treasury walking with [IMF loans in new budget](/posts/treasury-ducks-imf-loans-in-new-budget--gambles-on-world-bank-and-empty-promises).

The Le Monde report cited specific examples of Burundian, Tanzanian, and Ugandan nationals involved in everything from food vending to security and construction. These are the same informal sectors where many Kenyans fight tooth and nail for a living. So, while Sing’Oei is busy telling the world that Kenya is open for business for those with the right paperwork, the perception on the ground is that the President himself stirred the pot, making everyone - Kenyans and foreigners alike - wonder what the real deal is.

Ultimately, this is about optics and managing perceptions. Le Monde reported what they heard and saw, and Sing’Oei is trying to smooth over the rough edges. But the underlying economic pressures and the public sentiment remain. Whether foreign traders are being targeted or just held to stricter account, the conversation has been had, and the unease lingers. It’s Kenya, after all. Things are rarely as simple as they seem.
