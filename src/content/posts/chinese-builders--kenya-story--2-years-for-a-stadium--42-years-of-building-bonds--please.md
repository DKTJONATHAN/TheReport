So, the Chinese are at it again. This time it’s a 60,000-seat football stadium, the Talanta Stadium, apparently completed in a blink of an eye - two years, they say. Meanwhile, back home, these things take three times as long. Du Xiao, the project manager for China Road and Bridge Corporation (CRBC), is brimming with "pride." I’m sure he is. It’s easy to be proud when you’re building for someone else’s glory. This stadium, soon to be the Raila Odinga International Stadium, is another shiny bauble for the next big event.

They’ve even got some fancy design elements - no running track, putting the fans right in the action, the Kenyan flag colours splashed everywhere, and Maasai shield and spear motifs. Very authentic. And the construction method? "Cable-first, steel-after." Sounds impressive, saves three months. Meanwhile, 1,300 containers of materials shipped from China in one year alone. Makes you wonder who's really getting the benefit here, doesn't it? It's just another chapter in CRBC's 42-year Kenya saga, from roads to railways, and now, temples of sport.

Du Shan, the CRBC Kenya boss, reminisces about how Nairobi used to be cut off, a three-hour journey between districts. Now, thanks to their ring roads, it’s a breezy 50 minutes. And they’re widening more roads. They even built wildlife corridors so giraffes can "pass with their heads held high" on the Mombasa-Nairobi railway. It’s all very noble, isn't it? Du Xiao, who’s been here since 2011, hopping from one mega-project to another, preaches about adapting to the local community and building "trust between people." I’d like to see that trust when the next bill comes due. It’s a familiar narrative, one that echoes Nairobi's ‘Middle Power’ Play: More Friends, Same Old Hustle.

Then there’s the heartwarming story of Sammy, a local kid from Kibera, who got a job and can now "support his whole family." And hundreds of other young workers, trained up, some becoming "assistant engineers." Twenty years ago, that would have been a miracle. Today, it’s just another line item in a corporate report. Thirty years of CRBC employees getting gold medals for dedication. Forty-two years of "not easy." They talk about building trust, but it sounds more like building dependency.

Du Xiao himself admits he can't even make it home for his child's birth because he’s so engrossed in these projects. He lives in "two worlds." And he’ll likely leave one day, but the stadium will remain. "This country has shaped who I am today," he says. Oh, I’m sure it has. It’s shaped everyone who’s ever worked on these projects, one way or another. It’s the usual script: foreign expertise arrives, builds something grand, trains a few locals, and then moves on, leaving behind a legacy that’s part infrastructure, part unanswered questions about who truly benefits.
