So, the President, currently hobnobbing with the world leaders in New York, has decided it's the perfect time to tell us back home to "forget the fake stories" about Kenya’s economy. He’s out there, sipping on that crisp American air, assuring everyone that his administration has *stabilised* things. Apparently, inflation is down, interest rates have magically shrunk, and our foreign exchange reserves have exploded from pocket change to a cool Ksh 1.98 trillion. Sounds like a fairy tale, doesn’t it? Almost as believable as some of those PR scripts we see.

He’s throwing around numbers like confetti, comparing today’s supposed economic utopia to the "difficulties" of 2002, when, shocker, things were supposedly worse. He even talks about debt default threats vanishing like smoke. It’s all very deliberate, very intentional, he assures us. These are decisions made by the "right people," not the "storytellers" we should apparently ignore. One wonders if these "storytellers" are the same ones who can’t afford basic necessities or find decent jobs.

Now, I’m no economist, but I do live in Nairobi, and the reality on the ground feels a bit more like navigating a minefield than strolling through a stabilized economy. While he’s busy attracting capital for energy and infrastructure projects, and even pushing an AI initiative - because that’s what we all need right now, more robots to take the few jobs left - many of us are just trying to make sense of the daily grind. The talk of investment and partnerships is all well and good when you’re on foreign soil, but back here, the cost of living feels anything but stable.

He’s urging Kenyans abroad to check the Central Bank for his figures. Good advice, Mr. President. Perhaps we should all start spending more time on the Central Bank website than trying to make rent. It's quite the contrast, isn't it? He’s pitching grand plans for the East Africa Refinery and a Ksh2.2 trillion project, while the average Kenyan is just hoping their matatu fare doesn't go up again. It’s a different world when you’re signing declarations of intent with UN bodies and Equity Group, and a different world when you're queuing at a local shop.

And let’s not forget the lofty goals for artificial intelligence. While that sounds all very futuristic, and perhaps akin to the snitch era we're entering with technology, the immediate concern for most Kenyans isn’t how AI will transform Africa, but how they’ll pay for tomorrow’s bread. The UN General Assembly might be buzzing with talk of debt sustainability and climate finance, but for us, it's about whether we can afford to keep the lights on and food on the table. The President's confidence is admirable, but maybe a little more grounding in the everyday struggles of his people wouldn't go amiss, even if it doesn't make for as good a headline in New York.
