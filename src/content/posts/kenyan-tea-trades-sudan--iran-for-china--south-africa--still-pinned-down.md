So, the government’s patting itself on the back because our tea is no longer solely reliant on the chaos of Sudan and Iran. Big news, right? Now we’re shipping it off to China and South Africa instead. Apparently, some duty waivers were magically conjured up to make our biggest agricultural export more "competitive." Let’s be real, competitiveness in this country often means finding new ways to get fleeced, either by us or by them.

The whole drama about losing millions because we’re too dependent on a handful of buyers is nothing new. It’s the same old song and dance. We get burned by political instability, and then we scramble to find new, equally unstable markets. Minister Kinyanjui chirps about "costly lessons" from Sudan and Iran. Yes, Minister, we learned that relying on a single point of failure is a terrible business strategy. Shocking, I know. It's like saying you learned a lesson after driving your Sh500m Airbus H130 chopper into a tree - you learned it was a bad idea, but the damage is done.

Now they’re all excited about South Africa removing a 35% duty and "improving market access" with China. Wonderful. So we've shifted our tea from one pot of simmering instability to another. The minister himself admits we can't keep depending on just a few countries, but then proudly announces the new arrangements. It’s a classic Kenyan move: acknowledging the problem while celebrating the same old approach with a fresh coat of paint.

And let's not forget the mess here at home. While the government's busy chasing international deals, the actual business of moving tea is becoming a highway robbery. County levies, licensing nightmares, and transport charges all along the Northern Corridor are making it more expensive to get our tea to Mombasa than to fly it to the moon. Exporters are screaming about harmonizing taxes, and the minister promises to talk to the governors. We'll see how much of that actually happens.

Then there's the bureaucratic circus at the Port of Mombasa. Overlapping inspections, redundant documentation - it’s a recipe for delays and extra costs. And don’t even get me started on the taxes on packaging materials. It’s cheaper to buy tea from neighbouring countries because their materials aren't being taxed into oblivion. It’s enough to make you wonder if they actually want us to export anything profitably, or if this is all just a elaborate charade to keep a few people employed and a lot of people frustrated. We're still clinging to the idea that diversifying is "no longer optional; it is an economic necessity." For now, it’s just a painful, expensive reality.
