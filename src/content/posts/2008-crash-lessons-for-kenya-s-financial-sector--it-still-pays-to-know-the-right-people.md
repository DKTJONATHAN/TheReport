So, you want to talk about the 2008 financial meltdown? The one where Uncle Sam threw billions at Wall Street fat cats like it was going out of fashion. Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Wells Fargo - all got their pockets lined. And for what? Because they were too big to fail, or perhaps, more accurately, too well connected to *not* get bailed out.

The really galling part, as this piece points out, wasn't the bailout itself. It was the blatant political favoritism. While banks in New York and California, home to the Treasury Secretary at the time, got the lion's share, poor National City Bank in Ohio, stuck in the middle of nowhere, got the short end of the stick. They were strong armed into being bought out instead of getting direct help. It’s the same old story, isn’t it? The big boys get the gravy, the little guys get crumbs, if they're lucky. It reminds me of how [The Dollar Feasts While the World Burns](/posts/the-dollar-feasts-while-the-world-burns), always the same players profiting while others suffer.

This whole mess screams corruption, not just the kind where someone stuffs cash in their socks, but the slick, sophisticated kind that whispers in the right ears. Studies showed lobbying and political connections directly increased a bank's chances of getting that sweet bailout money, and by more. It literally paid to have friends in high places, and the closer those friends were to the decision-makers, the bigger the payout. National City might have lobbied, but their $2 million spend looks like pocket change compared to the "financial networks" the big New York and California banks operated within.

Now, here in Kenya, we're supposed to think about this and wonder how it applies to us. We've got laws about Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) in our anti-money laundering regulations, telling banks to watch out for them. But that's for clients. What about when these PEPs own the banks themselves? Our regulators might tell banks to scrutinize PEP customers, but we, the consumers, should be scrutinizing the banks and insurers that have significant PEP ownership.

Do these politically connected institutions actually serve us better, or are they just looking out for their own interests? Do they act kinder, or is their integrity compromised because the owners have friends in government who might look the other way? This article is just scratching the surface. We need to dig into this whole mess of political ownership in our financial services sector and figure out if we can actually trust these institutions with our hard-earned money.

We're talking about your deposits, your loans, your insurance claims. If the people running the show got there through connections rather than competence, what does that mean for you when things go south? It’s a question that needs more than just a passing thought.
