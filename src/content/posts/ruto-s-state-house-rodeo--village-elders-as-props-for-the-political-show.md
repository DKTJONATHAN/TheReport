Ruto's State House Rodeo: Village Elders as Props for the Political Show

The State House, usually a fortress of power and hushed deals, decided to roll out the red carpet for the country's village elders. President William Ruto, along with a phalanx of Cabinet Secretaries and Principal Secretaries, trotted out to greet these supposed pillars of the community. The official line? A "consultative meeting" to discuss "national development, security, and community cohesion." Honestly, it sounds like a script straight from a government PR department trying to convince us they're actually doing something for the common mwananchi.

Of course, the list of attendees reads like a who's who of the current administration, plus a few token opposition figures like Oburu Oginga, probably there to give the illusion of inclusivity. They're all there, nodding sagely, while the elders are told how "critical" their role is. Critical for what? To be the government's eyes and ears, sure, but more importantly, to lend a veneer of legitimacy to whatever policies the big wigs have cooked up. It's the same old song and dance: promise to listen, then carry on as usual. Just look at how genuine peace is often just a [SERVICE FOSTERS LASTING PEACE](/posts/service-fosters-lasting-peace) propaganda piece, with real change remaining elusive.

Interior Principal Secretary Raymond Omollo, bless his optimistic heart, declared the gathering "historic." Historic? Bringing people to State House is now historic? I suppose if you’ve never been past your local chief’s office, it might feel that way. But let’s be real, this is less about empowering elders and more about co-opting them. They're meant to be the bridge, the mobilizers, the dispute resolvers. In other words, they're supposed to do the thankless, ground-level work that the suits in Nairobi can’t be bothered with, all while ensuring government "programmes" get the rubber stamp of local approval.

And this is all happening before "key government projects" are implemented. Translation: before the next round of land grabs, resource exploitation, and potentially more controversial policies are pushed through. The government is "intensifying engagement" to "accelerate development." Sounds suspiciously like they're trying to soften the blow and ensure there's less grumbling when the real impact of these projects hits home. It’s a classic move to control the narrative, especially when you consider how often global conflicts, like the ones fueling the [The Petro-Illusion: Why Global Conflict Only Enriches the Few while Starving the Many](/posts/the-petro-illusion--why-global-conflict-only-enriches-the-few-while-starving-the-many), are conveniently spun to benefit the ruling class.

The pronouncements about elders being "influential" and their role being "critical" are designed to make them feel important, to keep them onside. But will this translate into actual tangible benefits for their communities? Or is this just another photo opportunity, a chance for some good optics before the real work - the kind that actually changes lives - gets ignored? We’ve seen this play out countless times, with grand pronouncements leading to precisely nothing for the people on the ground.

So, while the State House might be buzzing with these "historic" consultations, don't be fooled. It's a calculated move, a way to tick boxes and ensure the grassroots are, shall we say, "managed." The elders are the tools, the government programmes are the agenda, and Nairobi remains the centre of all decision-making. Nothing new here, just a slightly more elaborate performance.
