JUST TRANSITION FOR KENYAN WOMEN: POLICIES ARE THERE, THE REALITY IS NOT.

The government talks a good game about climate change, but for women like Anna Fatuma, it's just more hot air. Laws are on the books, but life on the ground? That’s a whole different story of backaches and endless work.

Anna Fatuma, 31, knows this better than anyone. She runs a kibandaski in Tumboni village, Kitui, slaving away from 6 a.m. to 8:30 p.m. Her most expensive meal is a paltry $2. She started this whole hustle hoping to improve her life and her husband Sam’s. But the cost of cooking gas? Forget it. Firewood is cheaper, but collecting it means a constant backache. Sometimes she pays someone else, just to get her “hotel” open on time. This isn't some abstract climate policy issue; this is Anna’s life.

Then you have the academics and the UN types, all talking about "legal frameworks" and "lived realities." They write papers, they make commitments - 35% emissions reduction by 2035, 100% renewable electricity. They even have an action plan for gender and climate change. Sounds nice, doesn't it? But for Anna, or for women in Isiolo walking for hours to fetch water, it’s just noise. Malkia John from Sauti Salama points out the obvious: "Gender roles are so deeply entrenched." Women do the care work, the fetching, the long walks. It’s unpaid labor, invisible to the policies that are supposed to help. They are expected to carry the burden, just like in the days of colonialism when only men's paid labor was recognized. It's like we are stuck in a loop, dealing with the same issues as if we were talking about [High-Flying Rhetoric and Empty Pockets: The Addis Ababa Special](/posts/high-flying-rhetoric-and-empty-pockets--the-addis-ababa-special).

Sure, a solar lantern might make Anna’s work a *little* easier, expanding her hours and making her feel safer. It’s a small win, a drop in the ocean. But it doesn't fix the underlying problem. The challenges are layered, Malkia reminds us. One intervention doesn't solve everything. Terry Owino, another expert, echoes this, stressing that women aren't "vulnerable by nature," they are *put* in vulnerable situations by systems. Systems that don't value their unpaid labor, systems that talk about climate action while leaving basic needs unmet.

The big players in climate policy, the ones drafting technical language in Bonn, they aren't the ones collecting firewood or worrying about floods in Kibera. They are at the table, making decisions, while women’s lived experiences are treated as just "sad stories" instead of valuable expertise. And this "just transition"? It can't be a one-size-fits-all solution. What works in wealthy Karen won't work in Kibera. Especially when African governments are spending more on debt servicing than on education, health, and climate combined. Companies offsetting their emissions by buying up land across the Global South? That's not solving the climate crisis, that's just buying a cover-up.

So, all these targets, all these plans, they sound good on paper. Building capacity, community education, women participating in financing mechanisms. But will it mean women like Anna get to shape decisions, or will they just be seated at the table after the agenda is already set, a token gesture? The backache is still there, the long walks for water continue. Adding women into an already unjust system doesn't magically make it just.
