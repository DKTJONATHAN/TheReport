So, Salva Kiir is telling us there's money, but people haven't seen salaries in nine months. Classic. He blames it on needing the "right people" to fix the economy, which apparently means playing musical chairs with ministers and governors. It's like watching a bad comedy show, except the punchline is everyone being broke and unable to withdraw their own cash. And elections? Forget about it. Unless, of course, they find a magical money tree overnight.

This whole mess is why you see people lining up for hours, only to be told the bank is out of physical cash. They deposit money, then can't get it back. It's a beautiful system, really. And it's not just salaries; road construction is stalled, diplomatic missions are practically begging landlords not to lock them out - the Kenyan embassy even had to cut hours. It's a real testament to their governance, much like how some libraries struggle with basic logistics, as seen in one book's "incredible journey" that was more a monument to sheer incompetence.

The government insists non-oil revenues are enough, but then an MP comes out saying he and his colleagues haven't seen a salary in *two years*. Two years! He's basically saying "no pay, no 'SPLM Oyee'," which is probably the most honest thing anyone has said in that country for a while. They fought for this nation, and now they can't even pay their own elected officials. The irony is thicker than the dust on those unmaintained highways.

And let's not forget the oil. The very thing that's supposed to keep the country afloat is also a mess, thanks to the war next door. Juba's making deals with warring factions to keep exports going, which is a sign of desperation, not stability. Analysts are saying this liquidity crisis is here to stay because of the oil problems, the cash hoarding (because who trusts a bank that can't give you your money?), and the "deep-seated structural governance challenges" - which is a polite way of saying rampant corruption.

The UN Human Rights Commission is practically shouting from the rooftops that political leaders are siphoning off oil and non-oil revenues. They're plundering a nation, and it's unleashed a human rights crisis. Meanwhile, Kiir is talking about restoring public confidence and taming the liquidity crisis. It's a good story for the press, but the reality on the ground is that people are struggling, and the promised elections are looking more like a fantasy than a plan.
