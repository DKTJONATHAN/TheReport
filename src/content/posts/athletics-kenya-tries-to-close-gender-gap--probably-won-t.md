So, Athletics Kenya is suddenly all about equality, talking a big game about getting more women into coaching, officiating, and leadership. They're even trying to match World Athletics' targets, aiming for 40% female representation on their executive boards and in coaching by 2029. Susan Kamau, the chief administrative officer, is spouting all this inspirational nonsense about inspiring girls and creating environments. It's the usual song and dance, isn't it?

They’ve already "trained" 44 women on some basic coaching course. One of them, Jane Kiende, a primary school teacher, is gushing about how eye-opening it was to be a safeguarding officer and coach at some African Championships. Apparently, she learned to communicate and cooperate. Groundbreaking stuff. This is the same kind of empty rhetoric we’ve seen before, like when [FIFA Leadership Has Lost Confidence, Says UEFA, After Pulling Plug on Investment Plan](/posts/fifa-leadership-has-lost-confidence--says-uefa--after-pulling-plug-on-investment-plan) - lots of talk, not enough actual change.

World 800m champion Lilian Odira chimes in, saying she prefers being coached by a woman because they "understand more" about her life as a mother. Jacinta Muraguri, her coach, agrees, adding that women coaches can discuss "reproductive health." Oh, how progressive. It's almost like they’re discovering women have lives outside of running. Meanwhile, Athletics Kenya has a grand total of five women on its Executive Committee - a real powerhouse of gender balance.

They’re also "pushing" for gender balance in other roles, like technical officiating and physiotherapy. Kamau even claims they're putting this in the budget. How generous. They’re also parading retired athletes like Milcah Chemos and Janeth Jepkosgei as examples of women making an impact. What they don't mention is how many of these women actually get significant roles or if this is just a way to tick boxes and get some good PR.

Right now, there are a whopping 200 registered female coaches, and a measly 30 are "vetted and validated" enough for senior national teams. They’re encouraging those who didn't make the cut to "repeat the process." Because that’s what everyone needs - more bureaucracy and hoops to jump through, especially when you’re already battling cultural stereotypes, family responsibilities, and financial constraints.

Look, closing this gender gap isn't going to happen because a few women get a certificate and a pat on the back. It takes real investment, real power shifts, and less of this performative nonsense. Until Athletics Kenya shows us more than just good intentions and token gestures, don't hold your breath for any genuine change.
