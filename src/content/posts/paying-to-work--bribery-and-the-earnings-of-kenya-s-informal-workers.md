PAYING TO WORK: BRIBERY EATS KENYA’S INFORMAL EARNERS

WARNING: INFORMAL WORKERS LOSE UP TO 20% OF DAILY EARNINGS TO BRIBES, FURTHER HOLLOWING OUT AN ALREADY SHAKY ECONOMY. THIS IS NOT TAXATION, IT IS THEFT BY THOSE MEANT TO SERVE.

Street vendors display second-hand household goods and electrical items in Korogocho, Nairobi. Informal trade provides livelihoods for many residents, often in public spaces where access to work is precarious. July 2026 Photo: Fabrizio Floris

So, you thought working hard was enough? Here in Nairobi, and frankly all over this country, earning a living means paying for the privilege, over and over. The motorbike taxi guy, the woman selling groundnuts on the corner, the mama mboga - they all know the drill. A police officer, a county inspector, a random suit with a clipboard - they all want their cut. It’s not a tax, not a fine, just a daily “contribution” to keep the wolves from your door. The World Bank, bless their fancy spreadsheets, says these informal workers are bleeding up to 20 percent of their daily takings. Twenty percent! That’s the food money, the rent money, the school fees money, gone.

This whole mess is neatly laid out in some World Bank report, probably written by people who’ve never had to hustle for their next meal. Kenya’s economy is growing, apparently. 5.2 percent GDP growth in the first half of 2026. But who’s benefiting? Certainly not the 83.8 percent of us stuck in the informal economy, where every shilling earned is a prayer answered, and protection is as thin as tissue paper. It’s a country that boasts about growth while its people are being systematically fleeced. This isn’t some far-off political scandal; this is the daily grind, the frontlines of corruption where your ability to simply *work* depends on the whim of whoever’s wearing a uniform or a semblance of authority. Kenya is rotten to the core, and this report just confirms it with numbers.

The report tries to dress it up, talking about estimates and sporadic surveys. But let’s be real. For motorbike riders, it’s 5 to 20 percent of their daily earnings, or KSh 50 to 100 a day, sometimes more. This isn’t just an inconvenience; it’s the difference between eating and starving. Unlike proper taxes that are supposed to fund public services, these bribes entitle you to absolutely nothing. They’re a brutal display of power, and guess who’s bearing the brunt? The poor. The World Bank data shows that those living in the deepest poverty are far more likely to be shaking down by the police, especially if they’re asking for help. Kenya stands out, with half of those who encountered the police paying up to avoid trouble, and a staggering 68 percent of those seeking help also paying. It’s a country where even asking for assistance comes with a price tag.

And it’s not just the street-level harassment. Businesses are feeling it too. In 2025, nearly 40 percent of Kenyan firms called corruption a major problem. That’s up from 25 percent in 2013. Firms dealing with these bribe requests are less likely to innovate, their productivity tanks, and sales shrink. For the government, this isn't just a moral failing; it’s a gaping hole in their finances, widening annual deficits by a conservative two percent of GDP. The report even hints at the political side - how power and resources are doled out to keep the elite happy, a "rent-sharing" that maintains a fragile stability at the cost of real progress. This isn't an anomaly; it's how the system is designed to work.

The report offers some wishful thinking about reforms, promising GDP boosts if Kenya cleans up its act. But for the average person just trying to make a living, the real test is simpler: how much of the money they earn actually stays in their pocket? Can they keep more of their day’s takings? Because this country can grow on paper, attract foreign investors with promises of a stable, if corrupt, environment, and still leave millions of its citizens struggling. If a portion of their hard-earned income is being siphoned off through arbitrary demands, the question isn’t just about how much is produced, but who gets to hoard it. The true measure of any reform will be whether the informal worker, the backbone of this economy, can finally keep what they earn.
