So, our man Hassan Ali Joho, the “domestic political heavyweight” who probably knows more about coastal politics than actual diplomacy, decides to have a little chinwag with the Taliban. On the sidelines of some mining conference in Dar es Salaam, no less. To him, it was just networking, shaking hands with whoever was there. He even bragged about it on X, pairing it with a chat with Australia’s high commissioner. Honestly, the man’s idea of foreign policy is probably as refined as a matatu tout’s.

But in Kabul? Oh, this was a red carpet rolled out for the apocalypse. The Taliban, starved for any shred of legitimacy since they took over, treated this handshake like they’d just discovered gold. Their commerce ministry went into overdrive, issuing communiqués about mining cooperation, Kenyan tea and coffee imports - the works. For a regime shunned by the world, a photo-op with a Kenyan minister is apparently like finding a unicorn. Joho, bless his heart, handed them a propaganda victory without even breaking a sweat, or likely even realizing what he was doing.

Kenya, meanwhile, is mum. Our foreign ministry, the sensible one, has been deliberately keeping its distance from these chaps. When asked about any formal talks, the principal secretary went radio silent. And that silence speaks volumes, doesn't it? It screams, "We didn't sanction this amateur hour." It’s the classic Nairobi dilemma: sectoral ministers wanting to push Kenyan products like tea, forgetting that for isolated regimes, every little interaction is a "diplomatic breakthrough." It’s like trying to sell tusker to a hyena; they’ll just see an opening to bite.

Look, other countries are doing this dance too, separating trade from recognition. China and some Central Asian neighbours are playing this game of transactional diplomacy. Even Tanzania’s officials met with Azizi. But Russia, bless their cynical hearts, has already gone all in, recognizing the Taliban as the legitimate government. Still, this whole affair reeks of a lack of discipline. Effective statecraft isn't about grabbing whatever handshake you can get. It's about knowing who's signing what and who's getting the photo.

If Nairobi genuinely wants to explore commercial opportunities with Kabul, that’s a decision for the foreign ministry, with clear boundaries, not some unscripted corridor chat. Otherwise, we’re just making it easier for the other side to write their own narrative, and frankly, their communiqués are probably more dramatic than anything our local news can cook up. It’s like when the police gift title advantage to Gor after clobbering AFC Leopards; sometimes, you just hand the win to the other guy without trying.
