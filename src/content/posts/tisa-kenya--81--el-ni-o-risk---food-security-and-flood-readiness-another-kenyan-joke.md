Here we go again. The meteorologists are puffing out their chests, spouting numbers like an 81% chance of a "very strong El Niño" this October. TISA Kenya and the Right to Food Coalition are waving their hands, screaming about preparedness, financing, and early warnings. But let's be real, in Nairobi, warnings are like those endless political rallies - lots of noise, little substance, and usually too late. They want to know if the government's "plans" and "funds" will actually do anything *before* the rains turn our streets into rivers and our farms into lakes.

The government, bless their hearts, claims they’re doing things. Mapping hotspots, clearing drains, even telling counties to find "safer evacuation grounds." Sounds good on paper, doesn't it? But after the mess earlier this year, where floods killed dozens and mudslides swallowed homes, you have to wonder. And let's not forget the Ksh187 billion damage from 2024 - pocket change for some, but a death sentence for livelihoods in other parts of this country. The Coalition's central question is a good one: will these preparations be visible and verifiable on the ground, or just another box ticked in a dusty report?

This isn't just about soggy shoes; it's about food prices going through the roof because farms are flooded and markets are inaccessible. They're calling it a "right-to-food issue," linking climate resilience to whether Mama Mboga can afford to feed her kids. It's a nice sentiment, but when roads are blocked and supply chains collapse, who benefits? Probably not the ordinary Kenyan struggling to make ends meet. We've seen this script before, much like how the same faces seem to reappear with different promises every election cycle - [Same Faces, Different Pulpit: The 2027 Deception Begins](/posts/same-faces--different-pulpit--the-2027-deception-begins).

And what about the money? Ksh7.1 billion is being touted as "climate-resilience financing." Great. But the real test isn't the amount, it's whether that money actually results in functioning drainage, reliable warnings, and safe places to run to when the water rises. Or will it disappear into some politician's "contingency fund"? The Coalition wants transparency, public participation, and accountability. Noble goals, but in this city, transparency is often as clear as a flooded river.

The truth is, Kenya is *not* starting from scratch. Agencies are activated, counties are supposedly clearing drains, and health officials are sending out warnings. The WHO is chiming in too. But the question remains: will all this activity translate into tangible protection for the people who need it most - the farmers, the pastoralists, the residents of informal settlements who are always the first and worst hit? The real measure of success won't be another government announcement or a fancy forecast, but what happens when the skies open up and the actual disaster strikes.
