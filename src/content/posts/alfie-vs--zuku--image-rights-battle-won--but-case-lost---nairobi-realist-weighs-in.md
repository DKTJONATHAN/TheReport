So, our man Alfie, the guy you know from "Mother-in-Law" and all those fancy Zilizopendwa classics that steal the show, thought he'd score big. He went after Zuku for using his mug in an ad without asking. And guess what? The High Court said, "Yeah, you're right, they totally messed up." They admitted Wananchi Group nicked his image, his personality rights, the whole shebang. Big win for image rights, they say. But hold your horses.

Turns out, Alfie was supposed to go through some obscure office called the Data Protection Commissioner first. You know, the guys who probably take longer to reply than a matatu driver on a lunch break. Justice Aburili, bless her procedural heart, said Alfie jumped the gun. You can't just march into the High Court demanding justice when there's a whole maze of administrative hoops to jump through. It’s like trying to buy ugali at a petrol station - just not the right channel.

The court did, however, clarify something important. They drew a big, fat line between copyright on a film and your right to, you know, control who slaps your face on a billboard. Appearing in a movie, Alfie's lawyers argued, doesn't mean you've sold your soul, or your likeness, to the highest bidder. And the judge agreed. Wananchi can't just say Kadi Media, the production company, gave them the green light to use Alfie’s face. Your image rights are personal, she basically said, like your mama’s cooking - not transferable.

Still, all this talk of personal rights and dignity doesn't pay the rent. While Alfie got the theoretical victory - a clear distinction between performance and persona - the compensation he was after? Vanished. Wananchi Group, the same outfit that had to cough up Sh5 million to Kadi Media for copyright issues, walks away with a slap on the wrist for the image rights fiasco. It’s a stark reminder that in Nairobi, even when you're right, you might still be wrong if you don't play the game the exact, tedious, bureaucratic way.

So, what's the takeaway? Actors, influencers, anyone whose face is their fortune: get your paperwork in order. And maybe, just maybe, have a backup plan that doesn't involve hoping a court will see the obvious injustice. Because while the law might recognize your rights in principle, the practicalities of getting them enforced can be as frustrating as finding parking on Moi Avenue during rush hour. It's a lesson for the whole advertising and influencer scene, really. Your face is yours, but getting someone to pay for it when it's used without permission is a whole other battle.

This whole saga makes you wonder about the other guys, the ones whose stories are never even whispered in these fancy courtrooms. Just another day in the city, I suppose.
