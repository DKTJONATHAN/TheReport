Katiba Institute: IEBC Commissioner's "Treating" Talk is Just Nairobi Slickness

WARNING: IEBC commissioner Anne Nderitu's defence of 'voter treatment' before the Ol Kalou by-election reveals a troubling naivete or calculated deflection, exposing the shallow roots of electoral integrity in Kenya.

IEBC defends government 'goodies' during elections.

CRIME

ELECTIONS, BRIBERY, KENYA

GOODIES

The whole song and dance from Anne Nderitu, one of our esteemed IEBC commissioners, about the Ol Kalou by-election is enough to make your teeth ache. "Not every gift or government project launched during a campaign period is illegal," she chirps. As if we don't know that. The Roman ancestors, bless their chalky togas, had their own ways of buying votes. What Nderitu is peddling here is the same old Nairobi slickness - a thin veil over blatant manipulation. She claims there's a difference between bribery and "treatment." Right. Because handing out mattresses and gas cylinders, branded with government insignia no less, is just a little "hospitality" to sweeten the deal.

You see, the law, or what's left of it, tries to draw a line. Bribery is a deal, a quid pro quo. "Treating," on the other hand, is the slightly more sophisticated cousin, where you just broadly dish out refreshments and pleasantries, hoping it sways the mob. It’s like saying buying a round of drinks for everyone at the local is just being a good neighbour, not trying to influence the upcoming MCA election. The Kenyan Elections Act, a descendant of dusty English laws, used to criminalise both. But then, in 2016, they conveniently scrubbed "treating" from the main Act. Senator Kiraitu Murungi famously admitted, "you cannot win any election if you don’t meet your supporters and buy them refreshments." So much for integrity.

And let’s not pretend this is some benign cultural practice. The Oxford English Dictionary calls this "treatment" usage "obsolete." But in Kenya, where public funds are just fancy campaign coffers, it's apparently still the norm. Even if these handouts don't actually change votes - a debatable point, given that a 2022 report suggested over 50% still believe bribery affects voting - it’s still a blatant violation of Chapter Six of the Constitution. Public money is not for campaigning. The Election Campaign Financing Act says as much, but then again, it only punishes candidates and parties, not the state officials doling out the cash. It’s like watching Kenya Banks Chase Scammers With Shiny New Toys, Still Getting Robbed; the system is designed to look like it’s working, but the same old problems persist.

The real kicker is the IEBC's position. Nderitu's remarks sound less like an election regulator and more like a government spokesperson. It creates the impression that the commission is defending actions that most Kenyans see as glaringly improper, if not outright illegal. This isn't independence; it’s a self-inflicted wound to public trust in elections, a wound that government is only too happy to keep prodding. While other countries have laws to prevent governments from influencing elections with new projects or payments, here, it’s just another Tuesday. And that, my friends, is the Kenyan way.
