Tanzania Overtakes Uganda in Kenya's Informal Trade - Because Kenya Is Starving

Don't let the fancy numbers fool you. While the "experts" at KNBS are busy counting every stray goat and sack of maize crossing our borders unofficially, the real story is simpler: Kenya's food supply is a mess, and our neighbours are stepping in to fill the void. Tanzania is now our second-biggest "informal" trading partner, not because they're suddenly brilliant businessmen, but because we can't feed ourselves.

This isn't some new, exciting development. This is just the latest chapter in Kenya's ongoing saga of mismanagement. We've seen this play out before, haven't we? Remember when the fuel strike ground everything to a halt? [Kenya Fuel Strike Grinds Transport, Businesses to Dust - As Expected](/posts/kenya-fuel-strike-grinds-transport--businesses-to-dust---as-expected). It's the same story, different crisis. This time, it's our stomachs that are feeling the pinch. Food and livestock imports from Tanzania are surging, accounting for nearly 80% of all informal imports. Rice, goats, sugar, cattle, maize - the essentials we’re apparently too incompetent to produce ourselves. And the deficit? It's ballooning. We're sending less to Tanzania and taking more, leaving us with a massive informal trade gap.

The border towns are the real beneficiaries here, of course. Places like Namanga and Isebania are buzzing, not with official commerce, but with the desperate hustle of filling hungry bellies. Namanga is now the top informal import point, raking in over $213,000 in just two weeks. Meanwhile, our trade with Somalia has practically evaporated, likely due to the same old security nightmares that plague that region. And Uganda? Even our long-standing informal partner is seeing a decline. It seems everyone else is navigating this economic chaos better than us.

The sad reality is that this informal trade, while a lifeline for our food security and rural incomes - according to the KNBS Director-General, bless his optimistic heart - is a symptom of our larger failures. We're still relying on motorbikes, head carriage, and bicycles to move essential goods because our official infrastructure is either lacking or deliberately ignored. It highlights how these informal networks are acting as a shock absorber for our failing domestic food market. It’s a temporary fix, propping up a system that’s fundamentally broken.

The numbers from the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) paint a grim picture. Informal trade with Tanzania shot up 62% to $1.28 million in June 2025, dwarfing previous figures. This comes as overall informal trade with neighbours shrunk by 16.7%. It’s a stark illustration: while we might be trying to push more commerce into the formal economy, when push comes to shove, and especially when the weather decides to be cruel and our harvests fail, we’re back to the dusty border crossings and the informal routes.

Don't get me wrong, the KNBS is doing its job, measuring what it can. But their "estimates" are based on two-week surveys, uprated to monthly figures. It’s a bit like trying to gauge a raging fire by looking at a few dying embers. The real extent of this informal trade, the desperation it represents, and the systemic issues it exposes are probably far more significant than these polished reports let on.







Tanzania has officially leapfrogged Uganda to become Kenya's second-largest informal trading partner in June 2025, a grim testament to our nation’s struggling food supply. The surge in food and livestock imports from our southern neighbour isn't a sign of booming bilateral business, but a desperate scramble to fill domestic supply gaps. According to the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), informal trade with Tanzania skyrocketed by 62 percent to an estimated $1.28 million in June, a stark contrast to the overall 16.7 percent contraction in Kenya's informal trade with its neighbours during the same period.

This isn't some economic marvel; it's a symptom of a larger failure. Kenya's informal imports from Tanzania more than doubled, driven by a demand for rice, goats, sugar, cattle, and maize - staples we are increasingly unable to produce ourselves. The KNBS report details how our informal trade deficit with Tanzania has widened significantly, a clear sign that we're buying more than we're selling, especially when it comes to food. This comes on the heels of a record-hot 2024 and subsequent weather disruptions, pushing food insecurity to alarming levels, with 2.15 million Kenyans needing assistance by February 2025.

The impact is palpable along our border towns. Namanga has become Kenya's primary informal import entry point, with its value surging from $57,937 to a staggering $213,209. Isebania and Taveta also saw significant jumps in informal imports. This pattern is particularly pronounced at the Tanzania-facing borders, highlighting how these informal corridors are acting as essential conduits when official channels and domestic production fall short. It’s a lifeline, yes, but one that exposes the gaping holes in our national food security strategy.

Meanwhile, our trade with other neighbours paints a less rosy picture. Informal trade with Somalia has plummeted to a mere $174,584, a direct consequence of ongoing security concerns rendering land border stations inoperable. Even Uganda, historically a major informal trading partner, has seen a decline in overall trade. This shift underscores the precariousness of our regional economic ties and the specific vulnerabilities we face, especially when it comes to stability and supply. We've seen how disruptions can cripple the nation, remember? While the KNBS Director-General, Macdonald George Obudho, touts the importance of informal trade for "macroeconomic stability and social development," it's hard not to be cynical. He speaks of enhancing food security and generating income, particularly for rural populations. And yes, that's happening, but it's happening because we've created a system where informal networks are the only reliable shock absorbers for our food markets, especially when domestic production falters. The dominant modes of transport - motorbikes, head carriage, and bicycles - are a stark reminder of the limited infrastructure and the gritty reality of this essential, yet informal, commerce.

Ultimately, this surge in informal trade with Tanzania is less a success story for cross-border commerce and more a flashing red warning light about Kenya's own agricultural and supply chain deficiencies. While the government might be focused on improving customs compliance and formalizing trade, the reality on the ground is that informal networks are what keep food on the table when official systems fail. It's a stark reminder that even as we aim for progress, our reliance on the informal sector for basic needs speaks volumes about our persistent challenges.
