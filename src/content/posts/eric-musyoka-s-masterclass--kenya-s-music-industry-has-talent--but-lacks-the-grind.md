So, Eric Musyoka, the man who’s heard more Kenyan beats than we’ve had hot dinners, dropped some truth bombs at that Spotify do. Forget streaming algorithms and viral hits for a second. This guy was crafting the sound of Nairobi when we were still figuring out if CDs were the future. He’s churned out hits for everyone from Sauti Sol to Octopizzo, and his fingerprints are all over films and ads you probably didn't even realise were local. He’s seen it all, from cassette tapes to TikTok trends. And his take on Kenyan music in 2026? "Eclectic, experimental and fun." Yeah, right. Fun until the money runs out, I suppose.

Musyoka admits that if he were starting today, he’d be focused on 'Eric Musyoka the brand' before he even picked up a mic. Which is a polite way of saying, today's artists are more interested in chasing clout than crafting a decent song. They grew up with the internet, so they think they know it all. They’ve seen music evolve from a passion project into a "career that pays." Pay for what? For chasing hype? Because that's what it looks like. He talks about how younger artists have more examples to learn from, but I bet most of them are just learning how to pose for Instagram.

The real kicker is when he talks about ambition. "Being too ambitious sometimes can cripple you. Because it blinds you to the faults that are at hand. You throw caution to the wind and start believing in the hype." Exactly! These kids fall in love with the applause before they've even learned to sing in tune. They're "over-believing," as Musyoka so delicately puts it. They can't tell the difference between real progress and just being shouted at by a bunch of strangers online. It’s a classic Kenyan problem, this instant gratification obsession. It’s the same story we see with everything from political scams to those bizarre dating apps for the 'vetted' narcissist. [Log-Drums and Lattes: Kenya’s Identity Crisis and the New Dating App for the 'Vetted' Narcissist](/posts/log-drums-and-lattes--kenya-s-identity-crisis-and-the-new-dating-app-for-the--vetted--narcissist).

And then there’s the Gengetone disaster. Musyoka calls it an inspiration and a warning. It redefined Kenyan music, produced stars overnight, and then… poof. Gone. He says they didn't build a foundation to sustain the virality. They rode the wave, sure, but when the ocean ran dry, they just crashed. It’s not a lack of talent, he says, it’s a failure to build careers. They were too busy chasing the temporary high of going viral to think about actual longevity. It’s the same short-sightedness that plagues so many other sectors here.

The biggest slap in the face? "We don't market," Musyoka says. That's the whole problem. We have the talent, the innovation, the beats that could conquer the world. But we can't be bothered to put in the work to actually get it out there. Sauti Sol and Bien are doing their thing, fine, but it’s not enough. We need resources, a serious strategy, not just hoping our music magically lands in someone's Spotify playlist in London. We’re still trying to sit at the table with the big boys while bringing a packet of crisps to a banquet.

Honestly, the most fascinating part for me wasn't the music talk. It was his preference for scoring films. Why? Because, unlike commercial music, film gives him boundaries. He doesn't have the pressure of asking, "Will this work?" because there are visuals to accompany it. It’s predictable. It's certain. While the rest of the industry is chasing the ephemeral highs of virality and the endless questions of a commercial track, Musyoka is finding comfort in the concrete. It says a lot about the chaos he sees elsewhere.
