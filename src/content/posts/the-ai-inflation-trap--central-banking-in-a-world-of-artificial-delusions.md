Much like the crumbling economic illusions recently witnessed in India, where the hollow promise of sustained growth was swallowed by the sudden, violent return of inflation, the AI revolution is built on a foundation of immediate costs and distant, theoretical rewards. We are entering the era of the Productivity J-Curve, a period where the massive capital expenditures required for AI infrastructure actually degrade measured productivity in the short term. Corporations are hemorrhaging cash on specialized semiconductors, cloud capacity, and data centers long before they see a single dollar in efficiency gains.

This creates a phenomenon that the architects of the new economy conveniently ignore: transition inflation. Central banks, blinded by their own obsolete frameworks, are likely to misinterpret this as a temporary surge in demand. They will be tempted to raise interest rates to cool an economy that is actually struggling to modernize. This is the ultimate policy trap. If they tighten too much, they kill the investment needed for the future. If they stay loose, they allow speculative bubbles to inflate until they inevitably burst, leaving nothing but digital debris and systemic debt.

The core problem is that human expectations move at the speed of fiber-optic cables while physical infrastructure moves at the speed of concrete and steel. Investors and consumers are already pricing in the gains of 2040, driving up prices today for goods and services that the current economy cannot yet produce efficiently. This mismatch between digital dreams and physical reality is a recipe for a global stagflationary nightmare. By the time the supposed productivity gains arrive, the social and economic fabric may already be torn beyond repair.

In the end, AI will not save the global economy from its inherent structural flaws. It will merely accelerate the speed at which we hit the wall. The transition will be marked by massive labor displacement, broken monetary models, and a widening gap between those who own the algorithms and those who are discarded by them. Central banks are no longer in control; they are merely passengers on a train with no brakes, heading toward a destination that their models are fundamentally incapable of perceiving.
