EXPLAINED: MALINDI COURT RULING MEANS RUTO STAYS PUT, ELECTIONS STILL A FARCE
WARNING: Don't get your hopes up about a quick election. The courts have spoken, and it means precisely nothing for President Ruto's grip on power, at least for now. Another legal charade to distract you.

This Malindi High Court ruling, the one everyone's buzzing about, is just more noise in the Kenyan political theatre. They've declared that general elections *must* be held within the fifth year. Big deal. What they *haven't* said is that President Ruto needs to pack his bags this year. The court, in its infinite wisdom, has suspended its own declaration. So, no immediate transfer of power, no sudden rush to the ballot box. Just another day in the office for the man at State House.

The real question isn't about when the election *should* have happened, but what the dusty old Constitution says about presidential terms and vacant offices. Turns out, Article 142 is the grumpy old man in this scenario. It clearly states the President's term ends only when the *next* President is sworn in. So, the fact that an election date might have passed is irrelevant. Ruto stays put until someone else is officially installed, not just declared a winner on paper. It's like saying a race is over when the starting gun fires, but the finish line is actually back at the beginning.

And don't even think for a second that this ruling automatically makes Deputy President Kithure Kindiki the man in charge. Article 146 lays out the *real* reasons for a presidential vacancy: death, resignation, or removal through proper channels. A court saying an election was "due" doesn't cut it. This whole saga just highlights how our supposed leaders can twist and turn the law to their advantage, much like how protests on Outer Ring Road over missing persons drag on with [nothing changing](/posts/outer-ring-road-blocked--one-dead--kiamaiko-s-missing-man-protests-drag-on--nothing-changes).

Even Article 134, which is supposed to rein in presidential powers during election transitions, is toothless until the election process *actually* reaches that stage. It doesn't mean the President loses his powers just because a judge made a pronouncement. So, brace yourselves, because the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) is still likely aiming for August 10, 2027, for the general elections. Nothing has fundamentally changed, except perhaps to give us all a good laugh at the state of our justice system.

This whole exercise is just another reminder that the big decisions are made behind closed doors, not in courtrooms. The system is designed to keep the incumbents in place, no matter what the courts say. So, while the lawyers debate and the politicians posture, the real business of power continues uninterrupted. Don't expect any seismic shifts; this is just politics Kenyan-style.
