So, the Education CS, Julius Ogamba, has decided to point out the obvious: Rigathi Gachagua is busy turning politics into tribal training, and the kids are apparently the ones being forced to sit in the front row. Ogamba’s saying this ethnic mumbo-jumbo Gachagua's peddling is making young Kenyans hate each other, see their neighbours as enemies, and generally forget that Kenya is supposed to be a single country, not a collection of warring villages. Apparently, some leaders want us to believe we can only trust people who share our mama's dialect.

Ogamba's essentially saying, "Wake up, Rigathi, and everyone else chanting this nonsense!" He’s reminding us that most of us have lived, studied, and worked with people from all corners of this country. We’ve got cousins in Ukambani, friends in Kisii, and workmates from every single tribe you can name. The idea that we can just draw lines on a map and say "don't touch this region" is as laughable as expecting our politicians to pay taxes. We’ve already "touched" everything and everyone long before these guys started their tribal chest-beating.

This whole drama is conveniently unfolding as we inch closer to the next election. Gachagua’s busy building his Democratic for Citizens Party (DCP) into some kind of Mt. Kenya fortress, pulling in whoever he can from other parties. He’s talking about "locking backyards" and consolidating regional power, which is just political speak for "let's reinforce our tribal base so we can negotiate from a stronger position." It’s the same old song and dance, just with a new beat.

And let's not forget the National Cohesion and Integration Commission, bless their well-meaning hearts, has already flagged Gachagua for potentially inflammatory remarks. They’ve even sent his file to the DPP. It’s a broken record, really. The debate is always about identity and regional strength, especially when elections are around the corner. It's like they forget that most of us just want a decent job and to not be viewed with suspicion based on where our parents hail from.

But Ogamba’s point, however obvious, is crucial. He’s saying these political narratives shouldn't be shaping how we see each other, especially not the young ones who are supposed to be learning about unity, not division. It's a shame we even have to have this conversation, considering how much integrated society already is, or at least tries to be. It feels like a desperate attempt to drag us back to the dark ages, when our country was simply a collection of tribes, not a nation. One wonders if this constant tribal politicking will ever lead to anything resembling a stable nation, or if it's just another cycle of [Higher Learning or Tribal Training Grounds?].

This tribal posturing is a tired script. While Gachagua rallies his troops and Ogamba preaches about unity, the reality is that Kenyan politics remains a complex, often frustrating, negotiation of identities and perceived interests. Let's just hope the kids are smarter than their leaders.
