So, here we go again. The weather boffins are screaming about El Niño, and this time it's not just a whisper, it's an 81 percent chance of a "very strong" season. Apparently, it's going to mess with everything from our food to our power. Southern Africa and the Sahel can expect drought - more of the same for them, really. But here in East Africa? We're looking at the classic heavy rains, floods, and landslides. Because why have one problem when you can have a whole buffet of them?

The numbers are grim. The Southern Oscillation Index has taken a nosedive, hitting levels not seen since the good old 1982-1983 El Niño. And it’s not just a fleeting visit; they're predicting this mess will drag on into early 2027. Oxford Economics Africa is already warning about depressed food production, strained water supplies, and power outages. It’s like they forget we’re already dealing with high energy and fertiliser costs. This isn't news to us; it's just Tuesday.

The economists are pointing fingers at Southern Africa and parts of the Sahel, saying they're "particularly vulnerable." But let's be real, the whole continent is vulnerable. They mention "recent bumper harvests" offering "a degree of resilience." Oh, how generous. Meanwhile, last year's El Niño-driven drought saw maize output plummet by over 50 percent in some countries, forcing governments to declare national disasters. It's a cycle of disaster and "resilience" that never seems to break.

Here in East Africa, it's a bit of a mixed bag, as usual. We've got El Niño chatting up the Indian Ocean Dipole, which means more rain, more floods, more devastation. The Kenya Meteorological Service Authority is in constant calls, probably trying to figure out how to spin the inevitable chaos. Remember 1997-1998? Deadly floods, destroyed crops, crippled transport, millions needing help. Somalia lost 2,000 people and half its crops. Kenya got floods, deaths, and a Rift Valley Fever outbreak. It's a predictable pattern, like watching a bad rerun. And let’s not forget the ongoing dryness we're already experiencing, even as they predict more rain. It's almost as if the weather here is as confused as the politicians. This whole predictable cycle of disaster feels like something you'd read about in a particularly depressing political drama, not unlike when [Rich Men Shouting in Suits: The Negreira Circus is Just a Power Trip](/posts/rich-men-shouting-in-suits--the-negreira-circus-is-just-a-power-trip) highlighted how power plays lead to widespread disruption.

The meteorologists expect El Niño to start making its grand entrance around November and December, and stick around until January 2027. So, for the next few years, expect more of the same: floods, droughts, food shortages, and the usual government pronouncements about "challenges" and "resilience." It’s not about "signals becoming clearer and stronger"; it's about the same old story playing out with slightly different numbers. Just another day in Africa, waiting for the next disaster to hit.
