POLICE CLAIM PATROL AT MP MUKUNJI'S HOME JUST ROUTINE - SURE, AND I'M THE QUEEN OF ENGLAND.

The police are out here spinning tales as thick as matoke stew, claiming their heavily armed presence around Manyatta MP Gitonga Mukunji's home was just "routine patrols." Apparently, "routine" now means surrounding a man's house before dawn with enough firepower to take down a small army, all because he had the audacity to show up late to a Deputy President's meeting. This isn't about maintaining peace; this is about sending a message, loud and clear, that dissent will be met with intimidation.

Mukunji himself is crying foul, and who can blame him? He points out the sheer waste of taxpayer money - a valid concern when the streets are crawling with potholes and the economy is doing the cha-cha downhill. A simple summon would have sufficed, but where's the drama in that? The police, meanwhile, are playing dumb, with the Eastern Regional Commander Stephen Chebet insisting they weren't looking for the MP, just "patrolling." Conveniently, they'll "visit him" if investigations link him to the chaos. So, they're not looking, but they might look later. Makes perfect sense, doesn't it? It's the kind of logic that gets you nowhere fast, much like trying to get a straight answer from these chaps.

This whole circus erupted after a meeting presided over by Deputy President Kithure Kindiki in Embu turned into a riot. Vehicles were torched, lives were lost, and suddenly, all eyes are on Mukunji. He denies involvement, but the police, under pressure, have to point a finger somewhere. It’s the usual script: violence erupts, someone important condemns it, and then the heavy hand of the state comes down, often on the most convenient target. It makes you wonder if these political gatherings are becoming more about theatrical displays of power than actual policy, a bit like the Billion-Dollar Golf Mirage Collapses we saw earlier.

Kindiki, of course, is spouting about issue-based politics and development, a narrative as convincing as a politician promising lower taxes. He's out there telling Kenyans to give President Ruto a "fair assessment," as if the last four years have been a walk in the park. He's touting a scorecard of "transformative projects" while the cost of living feels like a personal attack. His opponents, he claims, offer nothing but "lies and useless slogans." Meanwhile, his own party seems to be orchestrating a rather dramatic display of "issue-based politics" in Embu.

The DP's also busy defending the administration against critics who, he says, predicted economic collapse. Well, here we are, still standing, but at what cost? The economy might not have crashed entirely, but many Kenyans are feeling the pinch more than ever. And while they're busy defending their track record with flowery language, the common man is left wondering if all this talk of development and progress is just hot air, a bit like the promises made around movable collateral registries. The real question is, when will the "routine patrols" stop being a thinly veiled threat and start being about actual public safety?
