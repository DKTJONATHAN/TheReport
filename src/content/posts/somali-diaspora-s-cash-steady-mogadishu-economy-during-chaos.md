So, the Central Bank of Somalia is all puffed up because their diaspora sent home a record $6.78 billion last year. Apparently, these expats are the "backbone" and "main drivers" of the economy. Fancy words for people sending cash to keep their families from starving while the country lurches from one disaster to the next. It’s great they’re sending money, but let’s not pretend this is some flourishing economic miracle. This is stop-gap measures, people.

The data is clear: these "multibillion-dollar annual remittances" are five percent up from the year before, and they're significantly more than official foreign aid. While Uncle Sam and his buddies toss a few crumbs, the real action is coming from Somalis sweating it out in Dubai, London, or wherever else they can find work. This cash is what props up everything from household consumption - meaning people can actually afford to eat - to private sector activity and even those so-called humanitarian operations.

And it’s not just individuals. Business transfers and NGO flows are also chipping in, though the bank seems to be trying to make it sound like a coordinated effort. Commercial banks are raking it in, processing a good chunk of this, especially for businesses. Meanwhile, the old-school money transfer businesses are still the go-to for most households, proving that sometimes the tried and tested, even if a bit slower, works best for the common person trying to get by. It's a bit like how even in Kenya, you see these huge infrastructure projects touted, but the real economy often hums along on the back of folks like the "Digital Proletariat: Kenya’s Invisible Backbone of Global AI."

This whole debt relief thing, HIPC completion point, blah blah blah - it’s supposed to be a big deal. Somalia’s external debt has apparently shrunk to pocket change. But let’s be honest, when your economy relies on people sending their hard-earned cash from abroad, a reduced debt figure doesn't magically create jobs or build a stable government. It just means the few who can get their hands on international finance have a slightly cleaner slate to play with, while the masses are still dependent on the goodwill and generosity of their relatives overseas. It's a precarious stability, built on the backs of those who left.
