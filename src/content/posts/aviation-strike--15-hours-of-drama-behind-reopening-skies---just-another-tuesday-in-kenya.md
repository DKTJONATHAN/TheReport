So, the "negotiations" to end the aviation workers' strike went on for 15 hours, stretching into the wee hours of Tuesday. Fifteen hours. You'd think they were brokering peace in the Middle East, not sorting out airport chaos. And who’s surprised? It’s Kenya, darling. The government and the union played their usual game of "who blinks first," while everyone else was stuck at home, staring at cancelled flight notifications, or worse, stranded at the very airports meant to be gateways. This entire circus, by the way, happened while President Ruto was getting ready to jet off to Zambia. Priorities, I guess.

The whole mess at JKIA, Mombasa, Kisumu, and Eldoret basically brought the country to a standstill. Airlines were in a panic, cancelling and delaying flights like it was their job. The President, finally jolted into action, probably after his phone started ringing off the hook with complaints from important people, ordered his ministers to "fix it." And so, the high-level meeting convened, with all the usual suspects - union officials, CSs, COTU's Mr. Atwoli (who always seems to be around when there’s free food and drama), and the SRC folks. Standard procedure for a Kenyan crisis: gather the suits, make a lot of noise, and hope it goes away.

From the get-go, the union made it clear they weren't playing games. They wanted better working conditions, the head of KAA, Moses Wekesa, and his entire board gone, and a signed collective bargaining agreement. The government, predictably, wanted the strike over, yesterday. It was a classic standoff, with the union holding its ground while the government tried to chip away at their demands. The Wekesa issue, however, became the real sticking point. Apparently, firing him would be too much of a legal headache, as he'd only been in the job for six months of his three-year term. Because, you know, legal challenges are far scarier than stranding thousands of passengers and crippling the economy. It’s like that time [DP Kindiki Demands Sh450m More for Fancy Rides and Fancy Meals, While the Rest of Us Scrape By](/posts/dp-kindiki-demands-sh450m-more-for-fancy-rides-and-fancy-meals--while-the-rest-of-us-scrape-by) - the audacity!

By 4 am, the President himself had to get on the phone. "He said that he would face legal challenges if they remove him," claims union official Daniel Yatich. Imagine that. The President, concerned about legal ramifications, while people are missing flights, businesses are losing money, and the country’s reputation takes another hit. He then asked for time to find someone "who would sign the return to work formula." So, the man whose job was the biggest problem was conveniently absent from the signing and the press briefing. Brilliant strategy, really. The other win for the union was the salary adjustment - a measly 1.5% per year for the next three years. They'll be buying caviar next month, I'm sure.

And because it's Kenya, the negotiations also got a bit political, with whispers of the 2027 elections and Ruto’s quest for support in Western Kenya. Apparently, Wekesa's scalp was a political bargaining chip. Meanwhile, the aviation industry is left reeling, with operators complaining about the lack of contingency plans and timely communication. "Safety cannot be improvised," declared the KAAO CEO. No kidding. But what do they expect when the system is designed to improvise around every single predictable mess?

In the end, the skies reopened, the President jetted off, and the union got some concessions, albeit minor ones. The rest of us are left with the usual aftertaste of government incompetence and political maneuvering. They say the strike exposed shortcomings. Yes, the shortcoming of expecting anything else. We've been through this before, and we'll go through it again. Just another Tuesday.
