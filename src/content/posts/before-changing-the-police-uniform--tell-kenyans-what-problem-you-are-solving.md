Before Changing the Police Uniform, Tell Kenyans What Problem You Are Solving, Says Belinda

Nairobi realists like myself know that when the government starts talking about billions for new police uniforms, it's not about keeping up with the Joneses on the global stage. It's about making sure someone's pockets get a nice, fat upgrade. We're being told the current threads are "ugly," but I'm asking the same question Professor Naituli is: What *operational* problem is this massive, multi-billion shilling wardrobe change actually going to solve? Because last I checked, our officers are more likely to get stuck in the mud without a working vehicle than they are to be defeated by a poorly tailored shirt.

President Ruto himself admits money is tight, talking about better housing, healthcare, and equipment for our boys and girls in blue. So why is this fancy new uniform suddenly top priority? Is a new jacket going to magically fix leaky police stations, disconnected electricity, or the fact that officers are pooling their own cash for fuel? This isn't about looking good; it's about deciding whether our police force needs a fashion show or functional resources. Even the legitimate concern about maternity wear for female officers could be addressed with simple design adjustments to the existing uniform, not a complete overhaul costing upwards of Sh3 billion.

Three billion shillings, people. That's enough to actually *transform* policing. It could mean a reliable patrol car for that rural station, actual running water, and maybe even a decent meal for officers who are currently digging into their own pockets. Instead, we're likely looking at a procurement exercise that smells suspiciously like a government-sponsored shopping spree. It's the same old story, isn't it? This government just loves replacing what's already there, creating new agencies, new systems, and yes, new tenders. It's a dangerous governance culture, and it makes you wonder if the problem being solved is just the need for another tender opportunity.

And let's not even get started on the whispers about who's linked to this uniform deal. Media reports about connections to individuals close to the First Family are just the icing on the cake of suspicion. Transparency is key here. Publish the bids, the prices, the evaluators, the criteria, and who exactly is set to benefit. Every Kenyan paying taxes, from the unga buyer to the boda boda rider, has a right to know why their hard-earned money is being spent on uniforms when basic operational needs are unmet. This isn't just about appearance; it's about whether government understands the difference between actual reform and just dressing up its problems.

We've been told to tighten our belts, taxes are up, businesses are struggling, and young graduates can't find jobs. Yet, the government treats procurement as if money grows on trees. President Ruto himself said you can't elect a thief and expect no stealing. Well, we need to be even more vigilant about what elected leaders do with public resources. So, let's apply that vigilance here. If this uniform change is so critical, show us the operational necessity. Show us the public benefit. Because until we know what was wrong with the old one, why it couldn't be improved, and who truly benefits, it's hard not to suspect this is more about procurement than policing. It’s not unlike the endless interest in institutional failure we've seen before.

The Interior Ministry has now issued a statement trying to set the record straight on the new police uniform tender and has even named the company awarded the contract. But that's just the tip of the iceberg. We need to see the entire process, the justification, and the tangible benefits to policing. Otherwise, we're left with the absurd image of officers in crisp new uniforms while their stations are crumbling and their vehicles are out of commission. It's enough to make you question if they're really trying to fix policing or just looking for a new procurement scam.
