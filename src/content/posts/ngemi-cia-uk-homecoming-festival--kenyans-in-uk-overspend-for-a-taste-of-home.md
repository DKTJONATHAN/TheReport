Ngemi Cia UK Homecoming Festival: Another Excuse for Kenyans in the UK to Overspend

Let's be honest, this "Ngemi Cia UK Homecoming Festival" sounds like another expensive jamboree for Kenyans in the UK to pretend they're still in Kenya. They talk about reconnecting with the "spirit of Mount Kenya," but what they really mean is an excuse to drown themselves in nyama choma and ugali, which, let's face it, tastes nothing like the real deal back home anyway. Twelve hours of "entertainment and cultural experiences" - more like twelve hours of desperately trying to recapture a past that's long gone, all while emptying their pockets.

Of course, there will be traditional dances and storytelling, for the benefit of the "younger generations born or raised in the diaspora." Translation: forcing kids who probably prefer TikTok to sit through what amounts to a history lesson disguised as fun. And the fashion? Lesos and shukas are all well and good, but let's not pretend this is anything more than a mild nod to heritage before everyone heads back to their Primark and Asda reality.

The lineup is a predictable mix of names that might ring a bell if you're really trying to stay connected, like Samidoh and Njugush. But let's not kid ourselves, this isn't about genuine artistic evolution; it's a curated nostalgia trip. They promise to keep the energy high, which probably means loud music and questionable DJ sets to drown out the nagging feeling that this is all a bit much for what it is. It's a bit like that feeling after you've caught flights, not feelings, only to realize the feelings of regret about your auto-renewal are still very much present. [Catch Flights, Not Feelings-Unless Those Feelings Are Regret Over Your Auto-Renewal](/posts/catch-flights--not-feelings-unless-those-feelings-are-regret-over-your-auto-renewal)

And then there's the food. The "nyama choma and street food village" is the main draw for many, apparently. Because nothing says "cultural immersion" like paying inflated prices for food that will likely be more grease than flavour. They say it’s about experiencing the tastes and aromas that remind people of home, but really, it's about the temporary fix, the fleeting comfort before the reality of life in the UK hits again.

This whole event is built on the idea of identity and belonging, a way to keep "culture alive." But is it truly alive, or is it being embalmed for public display? For the Mount Kenya diaspora, it's a platform to celebrate where they come from. For younger generations, it's a curated glimpse into traditions that might not always be part of their everyday lives. For everyone else, it's probably just a day out, a chance to see and be seen, before life returns to its usual, uninspired routine. And don't forget the after-party in Coventry, because apparently, the need to cling to this manufactured sense of home doesn't end at midnight.

So, you "should not miss" this festival. Right. Because Coventry, on a random Saturday in August, needs another excuse for Kenyans to convene and pretend their connection to home is as strong as their connection to their smartphones. It’s a display of ancestral pride meeting modern UK entertainment, which mostly translates to a bunch of people trying too hard to prove they haven't forgotten where they came from, while simultaneously embracing where they are.
