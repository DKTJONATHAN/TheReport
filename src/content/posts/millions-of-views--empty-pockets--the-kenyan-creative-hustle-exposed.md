So, millions of Kenyans are glued to their phones, watching these young hopefuls chase the next viral TikTok or trending hashtag. A million views, a thousand followers overnight - it sounds like a dream, doesn't it? These ordinary smartphones have become stages, and suddenly, everyone's a storyteller. But when the likes and comments stop flooding in, many of these "stars" are back to reality: uncertain income, one-off gigs, and the relentless pressure to create more just to stay visible. It's a cycle that’s as predictable as the Nairobi matatu rush.

This whole digital circus is the biggest contradiction in Kenya's so called "creative economy." Young Kerry Amani, bless his heart, is out here exposing it all. He’s saying visibility is easy now, anyone with a phone can get it. The platforms win, the brands win, the audience wins - but what about the creator? It's a system that celebrates clicks but barely rewards the people generating them. We've got talent, we've got audiences, but the real problem, as Amani points out, is turning that attention into actual businesses.

It’s like these creators are mastering the art of shouting into the void, but have no clue how to collect the change. They meet people with bigger followings than themselves who haven't a clue about recurring revenue, digital products, or building a community that pays. The problem isn't audience growth; it's a glaring gap in commercial knowledge. It’s the same old story, really. We're good at making noise, but not at making money. It reminds me of how even seemingly sophisticated tech can be used for mundane surveillance, as seen in stories about things like [The Surveillance Teddy: Your Child's Secrets Are Now Corporate Waste](/posts/the-surveillance-teddy--your-child-s-secrets-are-now-corporate-waste).

And don't even get me started on the "accessibility." Sure, a smartphone and data are all you need to launch a career. Musicians dump music online, photographers hawk their wares, filmmakers distribute digitally. But for many, this accessibility hasn't translated into prosperity. It's a constant hustle with irregular income, unpredictable algorithms, flaky brand deals, and limited ways to actually make a decent living. Viola Karuri, a sound engineer and producer, puts it plainly: it’s not about being a star, it’s about earning a living doing what you love. But even that seems like a pipe dream when your profession is still stuck in the "Other" box on official forms.

The truth is, while we’re busy consuming their music, films, and endless social media feeds, the creatives behind it all are often left financially vulnerable. They have to keep investing - in equipment, in production, in marketing - just to stay relevant. Talent might help you survive, as Karuri says, but why survive when you can barely thrive? The Kenya Film Commission CEO, Timothy Owase, is preaching that creators need to see this as a real business, not just a hobby. He's calling creativity "the new oil," but if that's the case, why are so many creators still starving?

The barriers are clear: lack of finance, weak protection for their work, rubbish negotiation skills, no business training, and a fragmented market. They can create compelling content, sure, but understanding licensing, taxation, investment, or how to scale their "enterprises"? That's a different story altogether. Mike Burns, one of the partners, hammers it home: the biggest shift starts with creators seeing themselves as businesses. When you see yourself that way, you look at your finances, marketing, and growth differently. It’s not about building new systems, but about better coordination between government, universities, investors, and the private sector to unlock the opportunities that are already there. The real challenge isn't creating more content; it's creating more creators who understand business. Africa doesn’t need more creators; it needs more creator CEOs.
