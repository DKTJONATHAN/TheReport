So, President William Ruto, the "hustler" himself, decides the small-time hustle isn't for everyone else. Suddenly, Burundian nationals, the folks slinging tea, fixing shoes, and keeping Nairobi’s informal economy ticking, are told to pack up. It’s a classic Nairobi move - stir up some nationalist fervor, blame the foreigners, and pretend it’s all about protecting the Kenyan dream. The panic was real, folks lining up at the embassy with their bags, ready to bail. Honestly, who can blame them? This city is tough enough without the head of state pointing fingers.

Now, the Kenyan authorities, bless their bureaucratic hearts, have thrown a 90-day lifeline. "Regularize your papers," they say, as if sorting out immigration status, business permits, and work licenses is as simple as buying a chapati. They even sent some official to apologize for the "misunderstandings." Misunderstandings? Please. This was a calculated play. While they promise to fight xenophobia, the message has already been sent loud and clear. It’s a dance as old as time: promise order, create chaos, then offer a temporary fix. It’s the Nairobi hustle, but for governance.

The Burundian human rights groups are, predictably, crying foul. Discrimination, dialogue, blah blah blah. They're right, of course. These are people who've poured their sweat and savings into this city, often employing other Burundians and contributing in ways the official statistics can't even begin to capture. They’re the backbone of places like Kawangware, quietly making things happen while the politicians pontificate. It makes you wonder how much of the real economic engine of Nairobi is just a bunch of folks trying to survive, a theme explored in [The Nairobi Hustle is Just a Digital Graveyard](/posts/the-nairobi-hustle-is-just-a-digital-graveyard).

Ruto claims he wants to protect Kenyan traders, but let's be honest, these are often the same small operations being squeezed by bigger players anyway. This is about optics, about projecting strength by picking on the most vulnerable. The irony is that many Kenyan business owners actually appreciate this labor. But when the President speaks, everyone else falls in line, or pretends to. The 90-day grace period is just enough time to create more anxiety and possibly extract a few more "facilitation fees" from those desperate to stay.

So, what happens in 90 days? Some Burundians will manage to untangle the red tape, others will go home and wait for the dust to settle, and some will likely continue to operate in the shadows, becoming even more vulnerable. Kenya remains a magnet for economic migrants, especially with the dire situation back home. But this kind of unpredictable policy swing just adds another layer of precarity to an already challenging existence. It's a harsh reality for people who are just trying to make a living, a constant reminder that in this city, even the ground beneath your feet can shift without warning.

The whole situation is a messy, predictable drama. The Burundian community, like many others here, is caught in the crossfire of political posturing. They contribute, they build, they hustle, and then they’re told they’re the problem. It's a narrative that’s as old as time in places like Nairobi, where the promise of opportunity often comes with a hefty dose of uncertainty and the ever-present threat of being told you don't belong.
