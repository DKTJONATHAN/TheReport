So, Kalonzo and Matiang’i, our supposed opposition leaders, have finally decided to grace us with their "insights" into why they can't even agree on who should pick their nose, let alone a presidential candidate. Kalonzo, bless his four decades in politics, is out here crying foul about "State agents" and manipulated polls, as if we haven't seen this movie before. He’s peddling the usual line about his "experience" and "anti-corruption record" - laughable, really, considering the political circus he’s been part of.

Meanwhile, Matiang’i is busy dissecting the "hypocrisy and insults" within their ranks. He’s right about one thing: this isn't some seminary for saints. It’s a collection of individuals with bulging egos and a desperate hunger for power, all pretending to be a united front. Matiang'i’s analogy of building a team before picking a captain is apt, but these guys are more interested in who gets to wear the captain’s armband than in actually playing the game. It’s the same old story, a drama we’ve seen play out countless times, like Kenya’s leaders stuck in the mud while Gen Z wants out.

The Ol Kalou by-election drama Matiang’i points to is just a small symptom of the rot. Promises are made, then conveniently forgotten when it suits someone's ambition. They talk of consensus and painful negotiations, but what they mean is a brutal tussle for dominance. Kalonzo’s non-committal answer about being Edwin Sifuna’s running mate? Pure political theatre, designed to keep his options open while pretending to be open-minded. It's all about keeping their names in the conversation, hoping something will stick.

And let's not forget the subtle digs. Matiang’i's jab at those calling him a "mole" and linking them to the raid on former President Kenyatta's estate? Classic Nairobi political soap opera. Everyone’s got a past, and everyone’s happy to dredge it up when it serves their agenda. They’re all just circling each other, waiting for the opportune moment to strike, all while pretending to be on a noble quest for unity.

The truth is, these conversations are less about finding a genuine candidate to challenge Ruto and more about positioning themselves for the inevitable fallout. They're already showing their true colours: suspicion, rivalry, and a healthy dose of self-interest. The "hard road" they speak of isn’t just difficult; it's a self-made obstacle course of their own making.

By the time they're done with their internal squabbles, Ruto will probably have signed his third term into law. This opposition is a joke, and frankly, Nairobi is tired of the punchline. They preach unity but practice division, a classic Kenyan political paradox that always ends in disappointment for the voters.
