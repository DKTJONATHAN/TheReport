Ruto at 4: So, About Those Jobs? More Smoke and Mirrors, Darling.

This "job creation" saga under Ruto sounds like typical Nairobi hustle - a lot of talk, a bit of movement, but is anyone actually getting rich? The numbers are in, and surprise, surprise, most of it is more of the same grind in the informal sector. Don't get too excited about your neighbour suddenly buying a new car because he's now a "consultant" for a housing project.

Let's talk numbers, shall we? Between 2022 and 2025, we're told 3.27 million jobs popped into existence. Sounds grand, right? Except 87% of that, a whopping 2.84 million, are in the informal sector. That means hawking samosas on the street, driving a boda-boda with questionable brakes, or doing Kazi Mtaani for peanuts. The "modern sector" jobs? A mere 420,400. Those are the ones with payslips and perhaps a pension fund, if you're lucky. It's the same old story: the big promises are for the masses, but the actual stability is for the select few.

And the Affordable Housing Programme? Ruto's pet project, apparently. He boasts about 500,000 people working on it, up from a measly 200,000. Then PS Hinga pipes up, saying 428,000 are directly on site, and a million more are "supplying materials and services." What does that even mean? Likely someone's cousin's nephew delivering cement, or a woman selling chai to the construction workers. It’s a web of connections, not necessarily genuine economic uplift for the majority.

Then Labour CS Alfred Mutua throws in his two cents, claiming over 6.2 million jobs and "opportunities" have been created. Oh, and the Affordable Housing Programme? A cool 640,442 jobs, apparently. Kazi Mtaani churned out 583,868. These figures are inflated, designed to impress, not to reflect reality. It's like counting every single person who briefly touched a construction site or sold a bottle of water to someone who *might* have been working. It's about perception, not substance. The underlying issue of people outside stable employment, a problem Ruto himself admits, remains. This whole economic model feels like a rehash of the same old struggles, a situation that mirrors the broader issues of [The Global Contagion of Institutional Decay](/posts/the-global-contagion-of-institutional-decay).

Ruto himself admits unemployment and lack of income are "major challenges." He talks about MSMEs and access to credit - familiar talking points, aren't they? Meanwhile, people are still sleeping hungry. The gap between the official figures and the lived reality for most Kenyans is as wide as the Nairobi traffic jam on a Friday evening. We're creating "jobs" that barely keep body and soul together, while the real wealth remains out of reach.

So, four years in, and what do we have? A lot of informal hustles, a few well-placed relatives employed, and a government happy to trumpet inflated numbers. The promise of real economic transformation still feels like a distant mirage, shimmering on the horizon but never quite within reach for the average Nairobi resident just trying to make ends meet.
