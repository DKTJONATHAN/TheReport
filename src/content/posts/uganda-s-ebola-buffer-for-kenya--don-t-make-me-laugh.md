So, we finally got it. Ebola. October 5, 2026, Nairobi. A Kenyan, coming back from the DRC. All these years, Uganda was supposed to be our human shield, our big, friendly buffer zone. And it worked, for a while. But you see, borders are just lines on a map, not magic force fields against microscopic nasties. And this one decided to skip Uganda’s supposed quarantine and land right here, in the capital. Figures.

Apparently, this poor chap was already sick in the DRC, got treated (or not, who knows with those places?), travelled overland to Kampala, then hopped on a plane to Entebbe, and finally flew into Nairobi. He ended up at Nairobi Hospital, where, surprise, surprise, he tested positive. Died the same day. Ministry of Health announced it the next day. Another one of us, gone. Because, as usual, we wait until it’s already knocking on our door.

And let’s talk about Uganda. They’ve had their share of Ebola fun, haven’t they? Two outbreaks, hundreds dead. Yet, somehow, they managed to keep it on their side. But this time? This time, the virus decided to take a scenic route. It proves what I’ve always said - relying on other countries to keep you safe is like trusting a politician to keep their promises. You might get lucky, but don’t bet your life on it. It’s a tangled mess of roads, airports, and family visits, and a border guard can’t stop that.

The DRC is a whole other disaster zone, with thousands of cases and deaths. We’ve screened over 600,000 travellers, trained thousands of health workers, tested hundreds of samples. And what do we get? One positive case. Shows you how much good all that paperwork really does when a virus is playing hide-and-seek. You can’t screen your way out of this, especially when people are incubating the thing or just looking a bit under the weather. It’s not about xenophobia, it’s about reality; you can’t just suspect everyone who crosses a border. That just drives things underground. It’s like [OpenAI Wins the Right to Plunder Talent While xAI Fumbles the Legal Ball](/posts/openai-wins-the-right-to-plunder-talent-while-xai-fumbles-the-legal-ball) - you think you're being clever, but you're just making things worse.

The prof here drones on about surveillance, good clinics, reliable labs, contact tracing, and honest communication. All sounds lovely on paper. But in this city, we know how things really work. Trust is fragile, and when the authorities start barking orders, people tend to either panic or just ignore them. We need quick alerts, sure, but we also need to remember that ordinary life, trade, and work keep this place running. If we start treating every visitor like a walking plague, we’ll cripple ourselves before the virus even gets a proper foothold.

Ultimately, no one is safe alone. That’s the big takeaway, isn’t it? We need to connect with Uganda, with the DRC, with everyone. But let’s be honest, the real border that matters is the one between knowing there's a problem and actually *doing* something about it. And in Kenya, that interval is usually long enough for the damage to be done. Uganda did its job as a buffer, for a while. Now it’s our turn to prove we can handle it, or at least try not to make it worse.
