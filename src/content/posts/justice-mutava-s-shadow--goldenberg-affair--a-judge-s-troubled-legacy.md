So, Justice Joseph Mbalu Mutava has shuffled off this mortal coil. To his family, he was a loving man. That’s the official story, the one they spin to make the living feel better. But for the rest of us Kenyans, the ones who actually pay taxes and watch our country get fleeced, Mutava leaves behind a legacy that’s less about love and more about, well, a very long, dark shadow. His short stint on the Bench might be over, but the damage done? That’s a different story entirely.

The main act, of course, was the Kamlesh Pattni and Goldenberg circus. Mutava, bless his heart, decided to shut down that particular show. Not because the evidence was weak, or because Pattni was innocent - oh no. But because, according to the judge, the trial was taking too long and infringing on Pattni's "rights." Rights? While billions of shillings, money that could have funded our crumbling services, vanished into thin air? It’s enough to make you sick. Kenya still doesn't have a clear answer on who was behind the whole rotten scheme, who got rich, and how the state protected its own thieves. All thanks to Mutava, we were denied the chance to see the evidence tested in a real criminal trial. It’s a tragedy that keeps on giving, just not to us.

Goldenberg was a masterclass in state-sponsored robbery. Fake paperwork for gold and jewellery exports, money that Kenya desperately needed, all conjured out of thin air. Pattni’s scheme was so elaborate, it involved the Central Bank, the Treasury, Customs, banks, and, naturally, the political elite. It was the perfect corridor for looting state coffers, and conveniently, it helped finance the 1992 Kanu campaigns. The fallout? Monetary instability, inflation, lost jobs, shrinking incomes, and deteriorating services for ordinary Kenyans. It’s the usual song and dance, isn’t it? We suffer, they prosper.

Then came the legal wrangling, a civil and criminal circus that dragged on for years. Even after the Bosire Commission, which basically called Pattni a "thief" and demanded his prosecution, Mutava stepped in. Pattni wanted a plea bargain, but with new rules, the DPP said no. The stage was set for a trial. But of course, Pattni, the shrewd businessman he is, found a way. He sought to quash the DPP's direction, to stop the Chief Magistrate, and to prevent anyone from arresting, charging, or prosecuting him or his companies over Goldenberg. It sounds like a long shot, but with the right judge, anything is possible.

This is where Mutava’s "troubled legacy" truly takes centre stage. The case was filed just before a court vacation, and incredibly, it ended up before Mutava, even though he wasn’t the designated vacation judge. A tribunal later found that Pattni's lawyer had "schemed" to get the case before Mutava, and then manoeuvred to keep it there. Mutava, meanwhile, maintained he was asked to handle it, a claim the tribunal shot down. He was found to have irregularly allocated the case to himself, a big no-no designed precisely to stop judges picking cases they fancy.

Mutava’s judgment, delivered in March 2013, was the nail in the coffin for any hope of justice. He barred the State from prosecuting Pattni and his companies in any other matter arising from Goldenberg. Pattni walked free, not because he was proven innocent, but because the High Court, thanks to Mutava, slammed the door shut on the trial. Mutava’s constitutional justification? Prolonged delay violating Pattni's right to a fair trial. Memories had faded, witnesses had died, and the Bosire Commission's findings had supposedly impaired his presumption of innocence. And a settlement over the Grand Regency Hotel? That also helped. It’s a classic case of turning the state's own delay into permanent immunity, leaving us with more questions than answers, just like the economic woes that continue to plague us, perhaps linked to wider global conflicts that rewrite Kenya's economic playbook.
