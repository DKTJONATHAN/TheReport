So, Kenya Railways is now complaining about their own houses in Kibra. Apparently, these units, meant for displaced families and going for a song at Sh500 to Sh1,000 a month, have become a goldmine for some clever individuals. They're subletting them for up to Sh20,000, raking in a cool Sh4.5 million monthly, while the very people these houses were built for are left in the lurch. It's the classic Nairobi hustle, but with public land and a side of government neglect.

The Managing Director, Philip Mainga, is wailing to the Senate about "political interference" and "attacks by youths mobilised by local politicians." Oh, please. This isn't interference, it's just how things work here. When there's a chance to make a quick buck off a failed government project, politicians and their hired goons will always show up to "protect their interests." Meanwhile, legitimate beneficiaries are still stuck in temporary sites, wondering if the promises made during projects like the East Africa Trade and Transport Facilitation Project, which started way back in 2013, will ever materialize. It makes you wonder if the government even has a clue about what's happening, or if they're just as lost as these displaced families.

And the indignity of it all! Mainga paints a grim picture of children playing near active railway tracks, a disaster waiting to happen, all because of "extensive encroachment." He asks, "Are we going to sit down and watch cartels benefiting at the expense of protecting human dignity?" A bit late for that, Mr. Mainga. Dignity left this country a long time ago, replaced by the constant scramble for survival. The petitioners are even saying some of these "good quality" houses, supposedly occupied by civil servants who can afford the fancy cars parked outside, are lacking basic water and sewer connections. People are using toilets as houses because there's no sanitation. That’s not a governance issue, that’s a symptom of a deeply broken system.

The Senate is now demanding a list of occupants, promising to work with the police to evict the "illegal" ones. Good luck with that. You think these cartels, with their political backing and the Sh20,000 a month flowing in, are just going to pack up and leave? This isn't just about houses; it's about a system that consistently fails the common mwananchi while enriching the connected few. Just like the issues with stalled construction and inadequate facilities highlighted by Victor Wamae and his group, it's a tale as old as Nairobi itself. The World Bank program ended years ago, and the government can't even finish the job, but somehow, the subletting scheme is thriving. It's a mess, and we're all just watching the show.

It’s the same old song and dance. Projects start with fanfare, promise the moon, then stall due to "funding" or "court disputes." The World Bank programme, meant to provide 4,380 residential and 3,445 business units, ended up around 85% complete back in 2015. By 2020, they'd finished some, but then the government decided to close it because there wasn't enough money to finish the rest. Yet, here we are, with houses supposedly occupied by civil servants and others subletting them for exorbitant amounts. It’s a mockery of the entire resettlement effort, and frankly, it’s exhausting. The only thing consistent in this city is the persistent struggle for those at the bottom, while the middlemen and the politically connected feast. It's a harsh reality, but someone has to call it like it is, and that's just how it is in Nairobi.
