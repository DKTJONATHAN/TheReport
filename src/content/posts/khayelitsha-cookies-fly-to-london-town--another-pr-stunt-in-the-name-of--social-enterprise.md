So, Adri Williams and her Khayelitsha Cookie Company are being hailed as some sort of saviour. Apparently, every biscuit they churn out is a golden ticket for women in the "surrounding communities." From local stalls to the hallowed skies of British Airways, they're telling us this is a story of "purpose-driven entrepreneurship" and "lasting social change." Sounds like a well-baked fairytale, doesn't it?

They want us to believe that measuring success by "lives changed" instead of just profit is the new norm. Please. In Nairobi, we know how this song goes. It's usually the same tune: a bit of feel-good marketing, a few jobs created to quiet the critics, and then the same old system humming along. It’s a neat trick, making people feel good about buying overpriced cookies.

This whole "Odds In Our Favour" show with Saskia Falken, asking what happens when "ordinary South Africans stop waiting and start fixing things," is just more of the same optimistic fluff. While they're busy celebrating cookies on planes, back here on the ground, things aren't exactly being "fixed." It reminds me of when we hear about police "intelligence-led" operations, and you wonder if the "intelligence" is just another way to keep the status quo from wobbling, like that story about the Trans Nzoia West Police. [Trans Nzoia West Police Nab Nine in 'Intelligence-Led' Operation - Another Day, Another Gang?](/posts/trans-nzoia-west-police-nab-nine-in--intelligence-led--operation---another-day--another-gang)

And let's not forget the sponsors. Old Mutual Wealth, of course. Helping clients "grow, protect, and preserve what matters most." Because nothing says preserving what matters most like investing in a company that sells cookies to the West while the continent grapples with its own, far more pressing, economic realities. It’s a classic move: wrap your profit-making in a pretty bow of social good.

This isn't about social change; it's about market penetration. British Airways flights? That’s not about dignity; that’s about a lucrative contract. The "women from surrounding communities" are likely earning minimum wage to produce a luxury item for a global market, while the real power and profit stay firmly in the hands of the entrepreneurs and their wealthy sponsors.

So, before we get too swept up in the aroma of freshly baked success, let's remember what's really being served. It's a story, yes, but is it one of genuine upliftment, or just another chapter in the endless narrative of how the developing world is packaged and sold to the West, one cookie at a time?
