Farmers, Vets Moan About Food Chain Mess at Nairobi Expo

Nairobi's Sarit Expo Centre is currently drowning in a sea of glossy brochures and empty promises, all under the guise of the AgroFood Expo. MXM Exhibitors and the Kenya Veterinary Association (KVA) have managed to corral a crowd of farmers, food processors, and enough international suits to make you wonder if they’re actually here to fix things or just scout for new markets to exploit. They’re all talking about "strengthening the food value chain," which in Nairobi terms usually means more meetings, more committees, and less actual change for the folks doing the hard graft.

Enoch Mutai from KENAFF trots out the usual lines about farmers being "key players" and needing to "understand market requirements." Brilliant. So, farmers should somehow magically know what some city slicker in Europe wants while also dealing with unpredictable weather and a government that can't even get passports printed on time. He also drones on about cost-benefit analysis and quality standards. It’s easy to talk about these things when you’re not the one getting pennies for your hard-earned harvest. We’ve seen this song and dance before, much like how [Ruto's 'Fast' Passport Promises: Another Mirage for Kenyans?](/posts/ruto-s--fast--passport-promises--another-mirage-for-kenyans) turned out to be just that, a mirage.

The vets, bless their hearts, are chiming in about food safety and public health. Dr. Ambrose Kipyegon Ng’eno of KVA is ready to "work with stakeholders" to ensure food safety, particularly for animal products. This sounds noble, but let's be real, how much of that translates into actual action on the ground when the system is already so fractured? Meanwhile, Sri Lanka is here, probably looking for cheap labour and raw materials, pushing for "stronger economic and agribusiness ties." It's the same old story - developing nations get to be the suppliers while the developed ones get the profit.

The expo is plastered with shiny new gadgets: smart irrigation, AI farm management, drones, and biodegradable packaging. It’s all meant to impress, but are these tools accessible to the average Kenyan farmer struggling to make ends meet? Or are they just another way for the big players to squeeze more out of them? They talk about efficiency and meeting consumer preferences, but the real issue remains getting farmers a fair price and ensuring they aren’t left behind by all this "progress."

The talk of aggregation, value addition, and digitisation is endless. It's all about integrating farming with agribusiness. But this integration often benefits the middlemen and the processors far more than the actual farmers. They highlight the need to move "beyond increased production" and strengthen market linkages. Yet, the same old problems of poor storage, limited processing capacity, and a lack of direct market access persist. It’s a constant cycle of grand pronouncements and frustrating inertia.

So, here we are, another expo, another round of speeches. Thousands of visitors from across the globe, all here to talk about revolutionising Kenyan agriculture. The real revolution would be a system that actually benefits the farmer, not just another platform for businesses to network and for officials to look important. Let's see if any of these "innovations" actually end up in a farmer's hand at a price they can afford, or if it's just more hot air blowing through the Sarit Centre.
