So, President William Ruto is playing dress-up again at State House, this time with a new long-term development proposal titled "Developing a New Vision for Kenya: Towards a First World Nation." Apparently, it's a blueprint to turn us into a high-income, industrialized economy, a real "road to Singapore." They've even got big names like Governor Nyong'o and a former IMF chap on the team. But let's be real, behind all the talk of "bottom-up development" and "AI-driven innovation," it’s just the same old story dressed in new clothes, designed to keep us exactly where we are.

Nairobi has been dancing to regional rhythms for 60 years, a tune composed of militarization, dispossession, and enough violence to make you weep. Add in foreign money and global financial games, and what do you get? Not a new development trajectory, my friends, but a fancier way to become a strategic platform for imperial finance, logistics, and geopolitical flexing. This "strategy" just tightens the chains of neo-colonial extraction, making sure East Africa and the Indian Ocean continue to be milked dry. And don't even get me started on the talk of an East African currency - with this setup, deeper regional integration is about as likely as a politician keeping their promises.

Since we ‘got independence’ in 1963, Kenya has been a golden child for Anglo-American geopolitical strategy. Our land? A convenient stopover for military bases, operations, and intelligence gathering across East Africa and the Horn. This alliance has let our ruling elite play nice with NATO powers and project their own interests. Now, it's all about "security partnerships" and military deals, with Kenyan forces doing the bidding for international strategic interests. The failed Haiti intervention? Just another chapter in the book of Kenyan military institutions being used to serve imperial capital. And it's getting cozier, with deals like the Strategic Trade and Investment Partnership (STIP) and expanded military facilities. It's all connected: economics, military might, and political power, all reshaping Kenya’s place in the global pecking order.

The creeping presence of France, coupled with Britain’s old security relationship and Kenya's deep intelligence ties with Israel - a bond forged back in Charles Njonjo’s era - shows how smoothly we’re being absorbed into Western security structures. We're increasingly becoming part of an Israeli-US digital security complex, fusing cybersecurity, telecommunications, AI, intelligence sharing, border control, and surveillance into one big state power apparatus. In this new digital ecosystem, our digital infrastructure isn't for our benefit; it's for policing us, securing their financial networks, protecting their investments, and projecting their influence. It’s a slick operation, far more advanced than the old days of public health failures, like the Ebola response, which just highlighted how readily our political leaders will sacrifice citizens' welfare for external considerations. This reminds me of the whispers about the past, and how even things like the KSh 44.52 Million 'Unsanctioned Spending' by the Deputy President's Spouse's Office could be seen as just another symptom of the same old rot.

Historically, this whole charade has unfolded in phases: Cold War covert finance, gold smuggling tied to regional wars, shady commercial systems from the Mobutu era, the Great Lakes wars, moving South Sudanese militarists' cash, and the "war on terror" in Somalia. The faces and the goods might change, but the core structure of exploitation remains. Patricia Daley’s concept of "genocidal economics" nails it - wealth accumulation built on dispossession, displacement, and the destruction of populations deemed expendable. Kenya became a central hub for this, a financial, commercial, and logistical nexus for networks profiting from war, displacement, and violence. Forget about economic development; war, ethnicity, displacement, and blatant looting have been the engines of accumulation across Eastern and Central Africa. Nairobi, the shiny city, became the place where wealth generated from all this plunder could be safely stashed, laundered, and legitimized. You think these financial institutions and property markets are clean? Think again. The evidence is overwhelming, from investigations into Cold War finance to UN reports on the DRC and research on South Sudanese assets. It all points to Kenya as a regional center for organizing, financing, and legitimizing systems of plunder.

And let’s not forget the legacy of Vision 2030. It promised a middle-income status, but what did it really deliver? For a select few, it meant riches gained through fraud, land grabbing, and patronage. For the rest of us, it meant continued unemployment, debt, and structured deprivation. Jaramogi Oginga Odinga saw it coming, arguing that political independence hadn't dismantled the settler colonial economic structure, leaving us with a political class dependent on foreign capital. Subsequent events, from land redistribution and manipulated agricultural boards to inflated infrastructure projects and scandals like Goldenberg and Anglo-Leasing, proved him right. Public institutions became mere tools for private enrichment. And to maintain this system, progressive voices like Pio Gama Pinto and Josiah Mwangi Kariuki were silenced, while others faced detention and harassment. The persecution of intellectuals, teachers, and activists reinforced an order where economic concentration thrives on suppressing dissent. Even the much-touted multiparty politics in the 1990s didn’t change the game; organized plunder just found new avenues with liberalization. The 2014 Eurobond saga, with its missing billions and blocked audits, is just a modern twist on the same old story of capital leakage and systematic theft.
