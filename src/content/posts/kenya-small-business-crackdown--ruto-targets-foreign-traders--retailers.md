So, President Ruto thinks he’s the big man now, eh? Deciding who gets to hawk mandazi and who gets to open a tiny shop in Kenya. Apparently, these small-time hustles are strictly for Kenyans, while foreigners with big pockets can come build factories and all that jazz. This whole “Kenyans First” rhetoric, especially when it comes to scraping by, is just as old as the Uhuru Highway traffic jams. He’s even given these foreigners a deadline, September 7th, to pack up their wares and leave. Nice way to greet people, really.

The Burundians are already sweating bullets, offering free travel documents because rumour has it, their citizens are getting a rough time. How many of these foreigners are actually affected? Nobody really knows, but the government's already scrambling to clarify things. Turns out, some visitors are "deliberately misusing" their tourist visas to do what they came here to do - run businesses. Who would have thought? Apparently, being a tourist or an investor doesn't give you the right to sell anything here. You need the proper paperwork, which most of them probably don't have, or don't bother getting.

Now, the Foreign Affairs Principal Secretary is backtracking, saying the President was just talking about the Local Content Bill. So, if you’ve got the paperwork, the work permits, and the licenses, you’re supposedly safe. But then Ruto himself went and said someone from China or another country shouldn’t be hawking or opening a small shop here. Make it make sense. It sounds a lot like the same old song and dance, a populist move to whip up some nationalistic fervor, which, as we know, can easily spill over into [Nyalenya Station: Where The Uniform Is Just A Bullseye](%2Fposts%2Fnyalenya-station--where-the-uniform-is-just-a-bullseye) kind of chaos.

Burundi’s Foreign Minister is not impressed, warning that if this "hate speech" continues, things will change. He’s right to be worried. Kenyans are also running small businesses in other East African countries. This isn't just about Burundians; it’s about regional stability. The head of the Association of Burundians living in Kenya is urging patience but also pointing out the obvious: if Kenya starts this, don't be surprised when others retaliate. It's a tit-for-tat situation waiting to happen, and the ordinary person, the small trader, is always the one caught in the crossfire.
