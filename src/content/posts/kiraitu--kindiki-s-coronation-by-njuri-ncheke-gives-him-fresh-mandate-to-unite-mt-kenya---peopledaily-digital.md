Kiraitu: Kindiki's Coronation by Njuri Ncheke, A Fresh Mandate to Unite Mt Kenya - And For Whom?
Kiraitu Murungi's endorsement of Kithure Kindiki as the Mt Kenya unifier sounds like a broken record played on repeat, especially with the 2027 elections looming. The former governor, himself a veteran of shifting political winds, is now pushing Kindiki, fresh from some "crowning" by elders, as the region's savior. Apparently, this Njuri Ncheke business and a few traditional tools are supposed to magically mend a mountain famously fractured by ambition and ego. Don't be fooled by the talk of unity; it's usually just a preamble to the next scramble for power.

The "fresh mandate" Kiraitu speaks of is more likely a well-timed political maneuver. He claims Kindiki, with his fancy education and professorship, is the man to bring peace and unity to the "politically divided mountain." But let's be real, these divisions are as old as the highlands themselves. They've been a feature, not a bug, of Mt Kenya politics for decades, from the days of Matiba and Kibaki to Kenyatta and Kibaki. This whole "one house" rhetoric conveniently ignores the fact that this "house" has always had leaky roofs and squabbling tenants. The elders might bless it, but the ground realities are far messier.

Kiraitu’s dismissal of calls to split the mountain is predictable. Why would he want to dilute his own influence or that of his allies by breaking off a piece? He conveniently forgets that these disagreements are often about tangible gains, not just abstract unity. He slams Rigathi Gachagua for "fuelling divisions," as if Gachagua isn't playing the same political game everyone else is. It’s a classic move: point the finger elsewhere while you secure your own position. The talk of "losing what other communities are fighting for" is a veiled threat about marginalization, the same old song sung whenever Mt Kenya feels its leverage slipping.

The endorsement of Kindiki, particularly his supposed appeal to rivals like Gachagua, Mithika Linturi, Peter Munya, and Kawira Mwangaza, is a pipe dream. Kindiki extends an "olive branch," asking for forgiveness and promising not to fight. This sounds sweet, but we've seen this script before. Political forgiveness is often temporary, a lull in the storm before the next wave of power plays. The idea that they will "speak with one voice" and "put aside personal political ambitions" is frankly laughable. It's akin to expecting the bulls in a fighting ring to suddenly start a choir. We see the same kind of political maneuvering, but with less fanfare, playing out in the economic sphere too, much like the complexities of royalty taxes under the Finance Bill.

Kiraitu’s warning about "moving out of government" and the consequences for the region - reduced development and influence - is the real kicker. It's a stark reminder that for all the talk of unity and regional pride, it always boils down to who's holding the purse strings and making the big decisions in Nairobi. The calls for a split by CS Ruku, while perhaps reflecting genuine grievances from Mt Kenya East, are dismissed by Kiraitu as not being the "majority opinion." This is how established powers often silence dissenting voices, by claiming to represent the "true" will of the people.

Ultimately, Kiraitu Murungi's pronouncements are less about genuine unity and more about positioning for the 2027 elections. Kithure Kindiki's coronation is being framed as a strategic move, a potential bulwark against further fragmentation. But the history of Mt Kenya politics is a testament to its inherent volatility. Whether Kindiki can truly bridge the divides or simply become another pawn in the grand game remains to be seen. For now, it's the same old song and dance, with new faces singing familiar tunes of power, unity, and the ever-elusive regional bargain.
