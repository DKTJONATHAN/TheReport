Ruto: Even My Maize Dried Up - So What? Drought Hits Kenya Farmers Hard

President Ruto claims his maize crop withered thanks to climate change, trying to make himself relatable to suffering farmers. Meanwhile, his government promises a dam-building spree, but don't hold your breath for actual water.

Belinda Achieng reporting from Nairobi.

Politics

Drought, Agriculture, Climate Change

Maize

President William Ruto, fresh from a church service in Uasin Gishu, has decided to grace us with his personal drought experience. Apparently, even his "home farm" maize got roasted by this relentless climate change. He's painting himself as just another suffering Kenyan farmer, which, let's be honest, is about as believable as a politician promising less corruption. The man's got more farms than sense, and yet, his precious maize dried up. Shocking, I tell you.

Now, the big solution being peddled is a shift from rain-fed agriculture to irrigation, complete with a grand plan for dams - 50 large, 200 medium, and 1,000 small ones. This is supposed to fix food security and boost exports. It sounds impressive on paper, like most government promises. We've heard these grand plans before, haven't we? Remember when they said they'd fix the roads and we’d all be zooming around like it was Germany? This feels like another round of "trust us, we're building things," while actual food remains a luxury for many.

Interior CS Kipchumba Murkomen chimed in, confirming that yes, even the President's farm is a bust this year. He even translated his own Swahili lament, telling Ruto, "I passed by your farm and saw that you won't harvest anything this year. I was crying, but when I saw that even the President will suffer losses, I realised a lot of people are counting losses." Talk about a pity party for the privileged. It’s almost as if the CS is trying to earn points by highlighting the President's "suffering," while conveniently forgetting that many Kenyans have been counting losses for years, long before the current administration.

Despite the gloom, Murkomen did manage to praise the government for reducing the cost of subsidized fertilizer. A small victory, perhaps, but a victory nonetheless. Meanwhile, Ruto was off inspecting his cattle in Kilgoris, looking all rugged in a cowboy hat. Because nothing says "I understand your struggle" like checking on your prize-winning livestock while the rest of the country is wondering where their next meal is coming from. It's a classic "them and us" scenario, isn't it? One might even say it echoes the feeling of being sidelined, much like some feel when [Oburu Oginga's New Riverside Drive 'Power Seat' is touted as progress](/posts/oburu-oginga-s-new-riverside-drive--power-seat---just-another-seat-of-rot).

The whole narrative seems designed to soften the blow of continued hardship. The President’s personal maize failure is supposed to be a unifying moment, a shared struggle. But for those of us who live beyond the presidential farms, it’s just another Tuesday, another reminder that our fortunes are tied to unpredictable weather and even more unpredictable political agendas. Let's see how many of those dams actually see the light of day before the next election cycle.
