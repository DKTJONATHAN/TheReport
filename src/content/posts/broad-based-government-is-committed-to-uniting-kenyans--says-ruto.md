Ruto and Oburu 'Unite' Kenyans in Naivasha - For Whom?

A grand meeting of 250 MPs, 46 Senators, 20 governors, and a host of CSs in Naivasha, all supposedly to unite Kenyans and push a development agenda. President Ruto, flanked by ODM’s Oburu Oginga, waxed lyrical about political competitors joining hands for the "good of the country." It’s a classic Nairobi script - a show of unity when it suits the powers that be, while the cost of living continues its relentless climb.

The President spouted platitudes about belonging to one nation and succeeding or failing together. It's easy to say that from a podium in Naivasha, with catered lunches and expense accounts. But for the mama mboga struggling to buy maize flour, or the boda boda rider facing fuel price hikes, this "unity" feels more like a convenient handshake to solidify power. The talk of a "track record second to none" rings hollow when you consider the everyday realities of Kenyans, a sentiment perhaps reflected in those who are ditching traditional land feuds for the digital gold rush, as seen in our previous reporting on [Inheritance Wars: Why Kenya’s Gen Z Ditches Family Land Feuds for Crypto Empires](/posts/inheritance-wars-genz-opts-out-land-feuds-crypto).

Ruto proudly declared that the Broad-Based Government is "committed to uniting Kenyans" and "accelerating development." He even took a jab at the opposition, calling them an unsuitable alternative with no agenda. It’s always easier to point fingers at those outside the cozy circle, isn’t it? Meanwhile, the promises of robust measures to address the cost of living hang in the air, as tangible as the mist over Lake Naivasha.

Oburu Oginga chimed in, asserting ODM's long-standing commitment to stability and progress, stating they are now "tied together like the umbilical cord." This isn't about genuine partnership; it's about survival and power sharing. They’re all in this together, ready to claim credit for any perceived success and, presumably, share the blame when things inevitably go south. The nation watches, holding its breath, wondering if this "unity" will translate into anything more than political theatre.

Governor Susan Kihika’s call to prevent competitive politics from reversing unity gains is a noble sentiment, but it’s a bit rich coming from within this very coalition. The real test of this "broad-based" government won't be in Naivasha's gilded halls, but on the streets, in the markets, and in the homes of ordinary Kenyans facing the daily grind.
