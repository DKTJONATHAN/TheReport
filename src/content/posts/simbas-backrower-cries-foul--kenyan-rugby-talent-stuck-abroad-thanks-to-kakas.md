So, Bethuel Anami, a chap who apparently graced French rugby pitches for a bit, is back home to tell us how tough it is for Kenyan players to get those fat overseas contracts. Apparently, we have talent, who knew? But the real problem, according to him, is the lack of someone - a "player agency," he calls it - to hold their hands and escort them to greener pastures. It's not like we don't see this sort of thing everywhere, from dodgy real estate deals to those influencer scams peddled on Instagram. The missing link, surprise surprise, is someone to grease the wheels.

Anami also wails about our obsession with Sevens. Most of these rich overseas clubs, it turns out, are actually looking for players who can hack it in the 15s game. Shocking, I know. We're too busy chasing glory in the shorter, flashier format, while the real money is in the grind. It’s like boasting about your TikTok followers while ignoring the fact that the actual job pays peanuts. This whole thing reminds me of how some of these PR gurus spin things, making a splash about nothing substantial, a bit like [The Karen Circus: Why the Magical Kenya Open is Just High-End Window Dressing](/posts/the-karen-circus--why-the-magical-kenya-open-is-just-high-end-window-dressing).

He’s back now, this Anami fellow, and we’re told he’s waiting for some clearance from the Kenya Rugby Union to even play locally. Talk about bureaucratic nonsense. Still, he managed to snag a win with Strathmore Leos in some pre-season shindig. It’s a nice little full-circle story, from getting into rugby because it offered a scholarship, to now lecturing us about bridging gaps. We’ve heard it all before from these returning heroes.

The core issue remains: talent is one thing, but connections and a system that actually works for the players are another. Anami’s hopes of bridging the gap for future generations sound noble, but let’s be real, until someone starts actually building those bridges with more than just words, our talented players will keep being stuck here, lamenting their missed opportunities while the world moves on. It’s the Kenyan way, isn't it? Lots of potential, very little execution.
