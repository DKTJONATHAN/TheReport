So, the Kenyan youth have finally decided to state the obvious, petitioning Parliament to declare the country's colossal KSh13 trillion public debt a "national emergency." Bless their hearts for thinking a strongly worded letter and a breakfast meeting at the Sarova Stanley will move the mountains of greed that built this mess. They're demanding "tighter controls" and "legal safeguards" for health and education spending. As if the very people who got us into this hole will suddenly sprout consciences and fix it.

The numbers laid out are bleak, but not surprising to anyone who lives here. 65 cents of every shilling earned goes to servicing debt - not building roads, not paying teachers, just paying off IOUs. Meanwhile, 67 per cent of these same youth are jobless, and development expenditure accounts for a paltry eight per cent of the national budget. Dr. Samuel Kinyanjui of AHF Kenya rightly pointed out that "Kenya is haemorrhaging not blood, but revenue." He called it "auctioning the future," and frankly, he's being too polite. It's more like mortgaging the future to fund the lavish present of a select few. It's the same old story where [Our National Security Is Just A Side Hustle For Nyayo House Cartels](/posts/our-national-security-is-just-a-side-hustle-for-nyayo-house-cartels), and now our entire economy is the hustle.

These young people want prior parliamentary approval for all sovereign borrowing and statutory budget floors. Noble ideas, but let's be real. Edward Miano Munene from HERAF correctly stated that Parliament's current role is a "rubber stamp," not oversight. They're excellent at approving whatever is put in front of them, especially if there's a little something for the 'honourable' members. And Jerop Limo's frustration that youth inherit these decisions with "no formal seat at the table" is the bitter truth. Every shilling diverted from a hospital or classroom is indeed "stolen from our future," but who's counting?

Even the MPs who attended the meeting, like Julius Rutto and Ahmed Shakeel Shabbir, offered the usual platitudes about "transparency" and having the "courage to reject" loans. They "stopped a loan before," they claim. One loan? Against a KSh13 trillion mountain? That's like putting a band-aid on a gushing artery. They'll talk a good game about "unsustainable" borrowing, but the gravy train keeps rolling, and the consequences will, as always, be passed on to the generation with nothing but debt certificates.

Globally, AHF is pushing for fancy things like a Borrowers' Forum and an "AI Solidarity Levy." Sounds grand, but for Kenya, the immediate concern is simply breathing. The youth are exercising their constitutional right to petition, demanding budget floors that no austerity programme can override. It's a powerful statement, but in a system so deeply entrenched in self-interest, one has to wonder if anyone in power is truly listening, or if they're just waiting for the next meal to be served.
