So, Kenya Airways, our dear national carrier, is finally pretending to get its act together. They're babbling about restoring full aircraft capacity by 2026. Apparently, the problem wasn't a lack of passengers - shocker! - but rather a shortage of engines and spare parts that kept their fancy Boeing 787s grounded. It’s almost as if running a global airline is more complicated than just showing up for work.

The acting MD, Dr. George Kamal, chirps about tighter cost management and strengthening the balance sheet. Sounds familiar, doesn't it? It’s the same song and dance we’ve heard before. And let's not forget Africa’s reliance on imported jet fuel, making us even more vulnerable when the world decides to throw a tantrum, like with those disruptions in the Middle East. It's a miracle we get anywhere at all, let alone with anything resembling efficiency.

Now, they're talking about a fleet of over 60 planes in three years, with an average age under ten. Ambitious. Especially when the global order backlog is estimated to take over eleven years to clear. It’s like they’re planning a party in 2037 and just realized they need to order the cake now. And this whole government debt assumption? KSh63.1 billion, turned into equity once a "strategic investor" is found. Classic Kenyan playbook: clean up the mess with public funds and then hope someone else will magically fix it. It reminds me of how certain political tours seem more about optics than actual change, much like Ruto’s recent visit to Gusii land.

On top of that, they want to boost cargo revenue from 11% to 20%. Good for them. And their MRO business is apparently expanding. It’s all part of a grand plan to be more "resilient." Resilience is what you say when you’re barely keeping your head above water. While they’re busy diversifying and patching things up, let’s just hope they remember the passengers who are actually paying for these services, not just the accountants and investors.

The airline's turnaround strategy, as they call it, boils down to three things: more planes, less spending, and more money. Revolutionary. With cabin factors above 90% on lucrative routes, the demand is clearly there. It’s the supply that’s been the perennial problem. Let's just hope this isn't another case of a Chicago bus driver running a multi-billion dollar operation - a testament to how things can go spectacularly wrong.

Ultimately, Kenya Airways wants Nairobi to remain a gateway. That’s a nice sentiment, but gateways need reliable transport. Until they can consistently keep their planes in the air without needing government bailouts and hoping for a magic investor, that gateway might just rust shut.
