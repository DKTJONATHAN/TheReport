So, Afrobarometer has dropped another report, "African Insights 2026: Beyond Borders," and guess what? Africans like the *idea* of free movement, of course they do. Who wouldn't want to swan across the continent like it's a weekend trip to Mombasa? But hold your horses, because when it comes to actual foreigners showing up, especially those looking for work or a safe haven, suddenly everyone’s clutching their wallets and muttering about job security. It's the same old song and dance.

They say it's not cultural, that people wouldn't mind having immigrants as neighbours. Sure, until that neighbour starts working at the same company, or their kids are in the same school, or they're just… *there*. The report clearly states the economic anxiety kicks in, dividing folks right down the middle on whether immigrants are a net positive or just another drain on resources. Funny how that works. It's like wanting a bigger cake but then getting grumpy when someone else gets a slice.

The report highlights that while 62% back free trade and 56% support free movement, nearly two-thirds find crossing borders a nightmare. So, there's this huge gap between the lofty ideals and the grim reality on the ground. It's easy to cheer for open doors when you can't even get through them yourself. And then you have the educated and skilled - the very people who should be leading the charge for integration - crying foul because they see new arrivals as direct competition for their hard-won professional slots. Talk about shooting yourself in the foot.

This desperation to leave the continent, especially among the young and educated, is driven by the same old economic woes. Fifty percent are looking for work, twenty-nine percent are running from poverty. It's a vicious cycle, and frankly, a bit depressing. It reminds me of the situation with those [GTA 6 Hackers]( /posts/gta-6-hackers-will-release-breached-data-after-ransom-not-met---predictable-outcome-of-corporate-arrogance) - a predictable outcome when systems fail and people are pushed to the brink.

Amina Oyagbola, the Afrobarometer board chair, nails it by calling migration a "governance story." When institutions fail, people leave. It’s not rocket science. The report is basically a giant flashing sign telling governments: "Shape up or watch your brightest talent walk over the horizon, probably heading elsewhere than their own backyard." It's a barometer of state performance, alright, and most of the readings are probably not flattering. It's a continent eager for global clout but terrified of sharing the lunch table.
