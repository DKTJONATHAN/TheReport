So, Kenya's government is busy "seeking to strengthen partnerships and investment" in the creative economy. Apparently, the grand plan involves roping in some British actor, Idris Elba, and his "Akuna Group." The idea? To "promote Africa’s creative talent" and "expand pathways for young people into the global creative economy." It sounds lovely on paper, doesn't it? Just another government initiative promising the moon while the reality on the ground remains stubbornly earthbound.

Principal Secretary Korir Sing’Oei is all smiles, welcoming Katra Sambili, who used to advise the AUDA-NEPAD on the creative economy and now works for Elba's outfit. Sing’Oei waxes poetic about Kenya's "greatest asset" being its young talent, full of "ideas and ambition." He says they need "stronger partnerships, investment and access to international opportunities." And naturally, the Akuna Group is supposed to be the magic wand to conjure all this up. It’s the same song and dance we’ve heard before, with different faces singing it.

This whole charade is conveniently timed before some "Trust Summit: Nairobi Dialogues on Global Trust." Sambili will be on the "Trust Council," because naturally, discussing "rebuilding trust" and "strengthening international cooperation" is best done with a Hollywood connection in tow. It’s a neat way to tie Kenya’s cultural ambitions to a grander, global narrative, while conveniently sidelining the nitty-gritty of actual job creation and fair compensation for artists. We've seen similar promises in the past, like when certain musicians were touted as the next big export, only to find themselves back in the struggle.

The government keeps saying they want to empower youth and project Kenyan talent globally. But let's be real, most of these partnerships seem to benefit the big players more than the struggling artist in Kayole. They talk about film, music, fashion - but where's the tangible support, the infrastructure, the actual money that filters down to the ground? It’s easy to talk about global markets when you’re sipping chai in some air-conditioned office, but for the young creative trying to make rent, it’s a whole different ballgame. It reminds me of how, sometimes, even getting a simple job reference in Kenya can be a whole saga.

This endless pursuit of foreign validation and investment often feels like a distraction from building a robust local creative ecosystem. We have immense talent here, but the focus is always on the glitz of international recognition, on attracting figures like Idris Elba, rather than on nurturing the roots. It’s a classic case of looking for external saviors when the solutions might actually lie within our own systems, if only they were given the proper attention and resources.

And let's not forget the usual political platitudes. After all the talk of creative economies and global trust, there’s always a nod to voters and promises of continued "Democratic control." It’s a well-worn script. The real question is, when will these grand partnerships translate into concrete opportunities for the average Kenyan creative, beyond the photo ops and the feel-good headlines? I'm not holding my breath.
