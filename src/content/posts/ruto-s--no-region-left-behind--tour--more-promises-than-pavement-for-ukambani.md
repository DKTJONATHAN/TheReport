Ruto Promises "No Region Left Behind" - Yet Nairobi Still Drains Its Own.

President Ruto visits Ukambani, promising fair development distribution, but the usual political song and dance is already in full swing. Expect grand announcements, but don't hold your breath for tangible change that doesn't benefit the chosen few.

"Development for all," they chirped. "No region will be left behind," they beamed. Sounds lovely, doesn't it? Like a fairy tale where everyone gets a pony and the roads are always smooth. President Ruto, fresh off his tour of Ukambani, is singing that same old tune. He says political affiliations won't dictate who gets what, and that the "Usawa ni sawa" slogan is his guiding star. But let's be real, in this country, "sawa" usually means "sawa for those with the right connections."

Ruto waxed lyrical about fairness, even throwing in a jab at "old politics of tribalism." He's promising dams, roads, and infrastructure funded by this magical Ksh300 billion National Infrastructure Fund. Twelve dams, two for Ukambani. Ten roads in Machakos. Roads and water projects in Makueni. And title deeds for good measure. It's a buffet of development promises, enough to make you forget the daily struggle, at least until the cameras leave.

Of course, the "Mwala Cluster Water Supply Project" sounds impressive, 6.6 million litres daily. But will it reach the dusty corners where people still queue for water, or just the well-connected estates? And Ksh21 billion for Machakos roads? Sounds like a lot of tarmac, but we’ve seen these projects before - good for a photo op, then left to crumble like a poorly managed investment in Arsenal, only without the initial excitement.

This whole spiel about development for every region is a classic political move. They dangle carrots like the Ksh1.47 billion water project or the Ksh21 billion in roads, knowing full well that the real beneficiaries are often those who can grease the right palms. We’ll see how much of this actually trickles down, or if it just ends up lining pockets, much like how some Nairobi’s annual 'Swamp Edition' seems to perpetually ignore the real infrastructure needs of its citizens while others stay dry in their fancy neighbourhoods.

The emphasis on "no region left behind" is a nice sentiment, but in Kenya, development is rarely a level playing field. It's a scramble for resources, and those who shout the loudest, or have the deepest pockets, tend to get served first. We've heard these promises before, and while some projects do get completed, the cynical Nairobi realist in me knows that "development for all" often translates to "development for the few who matter."







President Ruto’s grand pronouncements about leaving no region behind as he tours Ukambani are, frankly, as predictable as the traffic on Waiyaki Way during rush hour. He’s trotted out the usual rhetoric about fair distribution of government projects, dismissing tribalism and championing the "Usawa ni sawa" slogan. While the KSh300 billion National Infrastructure Fund and plans for dams and roads sound promising on paper, we’ve seen this movie before.

The commitment to build twelve dams, with two earmarked for Ukambani, and the promised ten roads in Machakos, alongside road projects and title deeds in Makueni, paints a picture of progress. But let’s not get ahead of ourselves. The Mwala Cluster Water Supply Project, lauded for its capacity, is a welcome development, but the question remains: will this truly alleviate the perennial water shortages across the region, or will it be another project that benefits a select few?

Ruto’s dismissal of past political affiliations dictating development is a nice soundbite, a nod to a fairer Kenya. However, the reality on the ground often tells a different story. We’ve witnessed how political goodwill can pave the way for projects, while regions perceived as less supportive might find themselves on the back burner. It’s a complex dance of power and patronage, and these grand tours are often about solidifying that.

The sheer scale of investment, KSh21 billion for Machakos roads and KSh16 billion for Makueni roads, is substantial. But remember, the devil is in the details. Will these roads truly open up economic activity for the common mwananchi, or will they primarily serve as conduits for resources to reach the pockets of those already well-connected? It's a question that lingers, much like the unresolved land issues that plague many Kenyans.

This narrative of "no region left behind" is a powerful one, designed to inspire hope and build political capital. But for those of us who navigate the gritty reality of Nairobi, where development often feels like an exclusive club, the cynicism is hard to shake. We’ve seen promises made and broken, budgets allocated and siphoned, leaving many communities still waiting for their share of the national pie. We’ll be watching, sceptically, to see if Ukambani truly gets the development it deserves, or if this is just another political pit stop on the road to re-election.

The government's promise to use the National Infrastructure Fund to address water shortages is particularly pertinent. While the construction of dams and supply systems is a critical step, the success of such initiatives often hinges on effective management and equitable distribution. We’ve seen how even well-intentioned projects can fall short if they don't reach the communities that need them most, a situation not unlike the recurring issues faced by residents during Nairobi’s annual 'Swamp Edition', where infrastructure failures leave many in dire straits.
