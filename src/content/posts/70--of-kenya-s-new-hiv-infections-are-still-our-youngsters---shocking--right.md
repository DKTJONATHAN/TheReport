So, the big wigs gathered in Nairobi, called it the "National Round Table Forum on Ending the Triple Threat." Fancy name, same old story. State and non-state actors, bless their hearts, are "raising concern" over a "worsening crisis." Translation: things are bad, and they're getting worse. They warn it could "spiral out of control." Did we really need a fancy forum to tell us that? Some might say this entire situation is as predictable as a certain PwC employee once touted as one of finance's 'finest boys' losing his grip.

The numbers, as usual, are the real sting. Seventy percent of new HIV infections? Our kids, aged 15 to 34. Dr. Douglas Bosire of the National Syndemic Diseases Control Council spells it out: "young people are engaging in unsafe sex." No kidding, Doc. Meanwhile, 640 children are walking into health facilities with pregnancies *every single day*. That's 236,000 in a year, all between 10 and 19. It's a maternity ward in broad daylight across Kenya, filled with actual children having babies.

And the "triple threat" isn't just about getting pregnant too young or catching HIV. Adolescent pregnancy, sexual and gender-based violence - it’s all tied together. Poverty and dodgy adults exploiting vulnerable kids are the silent partners in this disaster. The sad part? Most abuse goes unreported, and the perpetrators? They just keep on going, probably laughing all the way. The forum even touched on the defilement cases, or rather, the lack thereof in court, which Senator Catherine Mumma rightly pointed out means someone isn't doing their job.

Now, they'll tell you Kenya's made "significant progress" in the HIV fight. Sure, 1.3 million on treatment, new infections down. But that's the broad stroke. The specifics, the ones affecting our youth, remain a stark reminder that progress isn't universal. The Principal Secretary for Public Health, Mary Muthoni, says these numbers "are not our destiny." Let's hope she's right, because right now, they look pretty grim.

The economic cost is also a punch in the gut. They reckon Kenya could lose 1.1 percent of its GDP if we don't "invest adequately" in sexual and reproductive health. Invest more in prevention, they say. Easier said than done when the system is already creaking. They're engaging communities in Kilifi and Homa Bay, expanding "adolescent-friendly health services." All good intentions, but will it truly reach the ones who need it most, or just become another line item in a bloated budget?

The First Lady, Rachel Ruto, chirped that "young people must be at the heart of finding solutions." Bless her optimism. It's a nice sentiment, but when the same young people are the ones facing the "triple threat" head-on, and adults are the ones largely causing the problem, it feels a bit like asking the fox to guard the henhouse. Let's see if any of this actually translates to less heartbreak and fewer numbers in the statistics by next year. I won't hold my breath.
