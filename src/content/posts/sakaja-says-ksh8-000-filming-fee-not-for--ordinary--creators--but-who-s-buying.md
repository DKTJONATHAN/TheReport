So, Governor Sakaja is out here telling us these new charges in the 2026 Finance Act, the ones that suddenly have everyone from YouTubers to TikTokers clutching their wallets, aren't meant for "ordinary content creators." Apparently, the Ksh8,000 for a commercial filming session, or the Ksh10,000 for a music video, is for the "professional and commercial film productions" that actually *use* public spaces. Right. Because Nairobi's roads aren't already a chaotic mess, and we definitely need more ways to tax the very people trying to make a living, even if it's just through their phone cameras.

He goes on to distinguish between a "content creator shooting a video for social media" and a "large film production arriving with a crew, equipment, vehicles and requiring the temporary closure or controlled use of a road." It’s a nice theory, mheshimiwa. But let’s be real. When a "influencer" holds a "monetised event" and gets slapped with Ksh10,000 per event, that doesn't sound like someone just vlogging their lunch. And what about the Ksh40,000 annual fee for content creation studios? Is he expecting every little garage studio to suddenly cough that up? It feels like they're trying to squeeze blood from a stone, and that stone happens to be the creative talent struggling to survive in this city.

The governor’s administration claims to recognize the creative sector as a livelihood, especially for the youth. Then why hit them with these fees? He's talking about "showcasing and marketing Nairobi," but then slaps hefty annual charges on local streaming platforms (Ksh100,000!), digital content platforms (Ksh80,000!), and even television and radio stations (Ksh200,000 and Ksh150,000 respectively). It sounds more like they're trying to starve the ecosystem that actually puts Nairobi on the map, the one that gives us stories that sometimes feel as wild as what you read about in [TIDBITS OF HOPE: The real cost of Nairobi nightlife - the-star.co.ke].

This whole "distinction" argument is just noise to mask the fact that they're finding new revenue streams, and the creative industry, with its often nebulous structure, is an easy target. Whether it's a massive Hollywood production or a local influencer with a growing following, the lines are blurry. And when the county government starts imposing fees that could easily cripple small operations, it’s the little guys who will inevitably suffer the most.

Sakaja claims they want to support the creative economy while establishing a framework for large-scale productions. But the reality on the ground is that these new charges, regardless of their intended target, create a chilling effect. They add another layer of bureaucracy and financial burden to an already challenging landscape. We’ll see how long this “support” lasts when the invoices start piling up for everyone.

So, while Sakaja reassures us that "ordinary" creators are safe, the Ksh8,000 per commercial filming session and the myriad other fees suggest otherwise. It’s the classic Nairobi playbook: promise support, then find a way to tax it. Let's just hope this doesn't push more talent to seek opportunities outside the city, leaving Nairobi’s creative pulse even weaker.
