So, the university lecturers have finally had enough. They're out on strike, and frankly, who can blame them? This whole Ksh98 billion pay dispute is just another episode in the never-ending Kenyan drama of government promises versus reality. The unions are demanding not just back pay - a tidy Ksh7.9 billion in arrears, mind you, from agreements signed and forgotten - but also binding Treasury guarantees for the next four years. Because, as we all know, a handshake with this government is about as solid as a sandcastle in a tsunami.

The numbers themselves are a joke. The government throws out a measly Ksh9.76 billion, while the unions, who actually know the cost of keeping the lights on and brains functioning, are asking for Ksh98 billion. It’s like offering someone a single grain of rice when they’re starving. This isn't just about salary adjustments; it's about the fundamental question of whether public university employees will be paid through a reliable public funding system or be left to scrape by on student fees and whatever loose change the universities can beg, borrow, or steal. It smells suspiciously like that time Kenya and the US were eyeing tech and arts shenanigans, full of grand pronouncements and little actual follow-through.

Now they’re dragging Parliament into it, hoping Speaker Wetang’ula can somehow shame the Treasury and the Ministry of Education into coughing up actual written assurances. As if a piece of paper will suddenly make the money appear. The Salaries and Remuneration Commission (SRC) even confirmed they've got nothing in writing. It’s the same old song and dance: agreements are signed, return-to-work formulas are drafted, and then, poof, they evaporate like morning mist. The unions say promises made on September 23, 2024, and October 5, 2025, were simply ignored by the Ministry of Education and the Inter-Public Universities Consultative Forum. Shocking, I know.

And let's not forget the vice-chancellors, bless their beleaguered hearts, wailing about a Ksh100 billion shortfall. So, while the lecturers are fighting for their current and past salaries, the entire system is on the verge of collapse. The strike is already disrupting classes, pushing back exams, and threatening graduation dates. Students, who aren't even part of these negotiations, are caught in the crossfire, their academic futures hanging precariously in the balance. It’s a classic Nairobi special: everyone suffers while the suits in government dither.

The parliamentary committee might look into past failures and demand future guarantees, but let’s be real, that’s just another bureaucratic delay tactic. The real issue here isn't just a salary dispute; it's a crisis of faith. Can this government, which struggles to honour its most basic financial commitments, be trusted to fund education? Until they can actually translate promises into payments, this strike will continue to be a stark reminder of their chronic unreliability. It’s a test, alright, but more of a test of our patience and our ability to endure constant disappointment.

This isn't the first time we've seen public funds disappear into thin air, and it certainly won't be the last. Just like banks trying to get tighter evidence in payment disputes, these unions are demanding proof of actual funds, not just hopeful whispers from the Treasury. The whole system is riddled with holes, and it’s always the common man, or in this case, the academic, who ends up paying the price for their fiscal incompetence.
