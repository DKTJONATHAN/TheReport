NAIROBI'S JUSTICE SYSTEM: STILL MORE TALK THAN ACTION FOR GBV SURVIVORS

A story about a young girl, Mary, and her horrific experience shines a spotlight on the long, arduous, and often fruitless journey for justice for gender-based violence survivors in Kenya. While international bodies pat themselves on the back for their "initiatives," the reality on the ground for ordinary Kenyans is a system designed to frustrate, confuse, and ultimately, fail them.

It’s a sad rerun we’ve seen before, hasn't it? A man preys on a vulnerable child, lured by the promise of something as basic as a school uniform. The system, instead of swift justice, offers confusion and delays, just like Mary's grandmother experienced when her case was shuffled around like unwanted luggage. This isn't just a Kenyan problem; UN Women tells us one in three women worldwide face this kind of trauma. But knowing it's global doesn't make the local incompetence any easier to swallow.

Now, we have UN Human Rights Office and UN Women, with Italian money, "implementing a project." They bring together all sorts of institutions, commissions, defenders, and communities to "enhance collaborative efforts." Sounds impressive, doesn't it? But Nelly Warega from UN Human Rights Kenya admits, "stronger collaboration is still needed." Translation: they're still figuring out how to talk to each other, while survivors are left bouncing between offices, their cases gathering dust for years. It reminds me of how even seemingly straightforward government dealings can devolve into a tangled mess, much like [RECEIPTS OR RETRACTIONS? THE MESSY TECH DRAMA IN THE MATLALA CASE](/posts/receipts-or-retractions--the-messy-tech-drama-in-the-matlala-case).

The latest buzzword is "gender desks" and "referral pathways." Police, hospitals, counselling - all meant to be a smooth ride for survivors. Chris Nyamawi from Mtwapa Police Station chirps about how they record statements, send survivors for treatment and counselling, and then gather evidence. But he also admits, "Police alone cannot ensure that a survivor gets justice." Of course, they can't. It requires a coordinated effort, something that seems to be perpetually "strengthening" but rarely fully realized. The program, originally for election violence, has broadened its scope. Training is happening, and for Mary's grandmother, it apparently made a "tangible difference." Let's hope it's more than just a temporary fix.

They talk about "legal and policy reform," "strengthening government systems," and "changing social norms." It's a two-pronged approach, they say, addressing institutional weaknesses and community attitudes. They're empowering women and girls, expanding economic opportunities, and breaking cycles of violence. All noble goals, but when the very foundation of the justice system is shaky, these lofty ambitions often remain just that - ambitions.

Ultimately, the rhetoric of "putting the victim at the centre" and "strengthening pathways" sounds good on paper. But for survivors like Mary, it's the reality of navigating a system that’s often slow, fragmented, and opaque that matters. Until these initiatives translate into genuine, consistent, and transparent justice, rather than just more meetings and reports, they’re just another expensive exercise in futility.







It’s the same old story, isn't it? A child’s innocence shattered, and the system meant to protect them offering more confusion than comfort. Young Mary, a bright girl in Kenya, answered the door to a man who would irrevocably change her life. He promised her the very thing she lacked to attend school - a uniform. Instead, he offered a path to trauma, while her brother’s attempt to intervene was met with brute force. This is not a fairy tale; this is Kenya, and the justice system’s response is often as tangled as a poorly woven basket.

Mary’s grandmother’s search for justice quickly devolved into a bureaucratic nightmare. A court hearing is scheduled, only for her to discover, without notice, that the case had been moved to another room. This isn't an isolated incident. UN Women states nearly one in three women globally experience violence, and Kenya is no exception. The emphasis on prevention is admirable, but what about the actual, tangible justice for those already harmed? Prevention alone, as this story shows, is a laughable notion when the follow-through is so pathetic.

Now, we have the UN Human Rights Office, UN Women, and the Italian Agency for Development Cooperation rolling out a project called "Let It Not Happen Again." They're bringing in justice institutions, commissions, human rights defenders, and communities. Sounds like a party, doesn't it? Nelly Warega from UN Human Rights Kenya admits, "stronger collaboration is still needed." Translation: they're still trying to get the horses to drink from the water trough. Survivors are often tossed around, from one office to another, only to find their cases stalled for years. It's a familiar pattern of incompetence, much like the bureaucratic hurdles faced in other areas of governance, as seen in [RECEIPTS OR RETRACTIONS? THE MESSY TECH DRAMA IN THE MATLALA CASE](/posts/receipts-or-retractions--the-messy-tech-drama-in-the-matlala-case).

The latest buzzwords are "gender desks" and "robust referral pathways." The idea is to simplify the process. Police record statements, survivors get medical attention and counselling, and then the investigating officer builds a case. Charles Kwemoi of UN Human Rights Kenya emphasizes putting the "victim at the centre." But as Officer Commanding Station Chris Nyamawi from Mtwapa Police Station rightly points out, "Police alone cannot ensure that a survivor gets justice." It requires a symphony of agencies - police, hospitals, judiciary, rescue centres, children's departments - all playing in tune. The question is, are they ever truly in sync?

The initiative, initially for election periods, has expanded. Training for police, prosecutors, and judges is underway, and we're told it's "beginning to deliver results." For Mary's grandmother, the improved coordination meant she was reassured her case was being handled and the accused actually showed up in court. It didn’t disappear, as she feared. But this glimmer of hope is often a rare exception in a system notorious for its delays and fragmentation.

The program aims to strengthen national and county government systems and empower women and girls at the community level. They talk about legal reforms, policy changes, and economic empowerment. These are all good things. But the core issue remains: access to justice is undermined by delays, poor coordination, and a lack of transparency. Initiatives like gender desks show promise, but until these "strengthening" efforts translate into consistent, swift, and fair outcomes for all survivors, they're just more paper-pushing in a system that needs a complete overhaul, not just a fresh coat of paint.
