President Ruto's latest photo op involves a Sh390 billion "investment" in electric vehicles, promising a green revolution for Kenya. Sounds fantastic, doesn't it? Another massive deal, another handshake, another round of speeches about escaping fuel imports and creating thousands of jobs. But for those of us living on the ground in Nairobi, these grand pronouncements often feel like just that - pronouncements. We've heard this song before, the one where billions are pledged, and the common mwananchi is left wondering where their cut is.

The government and Endelevu Enterprise Corporation are talking about 50,000 four-wheelers and 100,000 two-wheelers annually, along with 2,000 direct jobs and a whopping 100,000 indirect 'opportunities'. One has to ask, what kind of jobs? Assembly line grunt work or actual high-skill positions that transfer lasting knowledge? And how much of this will be genuine manufacturing versus just bolting together imported parts? It's easy to conjure impressive figures on paper, but the reality on the factory floor, if it ever materializes, tends to be far less shiny. It feels reminiscent of those big promises that turn out to be nothing more than [The Ten Dollar Mirage and the Global Harvest of Despair](/posts/the-ten-dollar-mirage-and-the-global-harvest-of-despair) for ordinary folks.

Then there's the talk of 1,000 solar-powered charging hubs and an entire "green mobility ecosystem." Excellent aspirations, no doubt. But in a country where basic infrastructure often struggles, are we to believe a thousand state-of-the-art charging stations will sprout up seamlessly? And the ambition to manufacture for the entire African market? Let's get the local market sorted first, shall we, before we conquer the continent with EVs that might be too expensive for the average Kenyan.

Ruto himself spoke of "investment and industrial capacity," "jobs for our young people," "contracts for our SMEs," and crucially, "technology and skills to be transferred, not just traded." That last bit is key, isn't it? Because often these foreign partnerships are more about extracting resources and markets than genuinely empowering local industries. And as the President wisely stated, "A signature does not build a factory." A statement that, in the Kenyan context, rings with an almost ironic truth, given how many grand projects remain stuck on paper or become ghost ventures.

So, while the headlines scream "Sh390bn electric vehicle investment," the cynical Nairobi realist in me sees another potential castle in the air. Will this truly change the equation, as Ruto hopes, or will it be another cycle of high hopes, grand announcements, and ultimately, disappointment for the majority? We’ll be watching, but we won't be holding our breath.
