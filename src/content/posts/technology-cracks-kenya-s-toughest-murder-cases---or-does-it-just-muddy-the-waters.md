So, the Big Tech show is in town, eh? Apparently, we're all supposed to be impressed because now the police aren't just relying on "confessions" from people they've beaten senseless, but on CCTV footage. Brilliant. Albert Ojwang, a teacher and blogger, is dead in a police station. And what do we get? Hours of grainy footage showing everyone who was *there*. It’s like watching a meticulously edited home video of a murder, but instead of a party, it's a police cell. The court will decide if it's enough. I'll believe it when I see it.

This whole digital evidence obsession is just a fancy way of saying the system is trying to look more professional. Forget eyewitnesses who might actually have seen something useful, or worse, *known* something. Now we have apps and phones and GPS. They can reconstruct crime scenes, trace movements, all that jazz. It's supposed to be more reliable than a human being, which, let's be honest, isn't exactly setting the bar high in this city. But does it actually bring justice? Or just more complicated ways to point fingers?

Look at this Okoth Obado nonsense. Sharon Otieno is dead, and somehow, the phones and the M-Pesa records are the heroes. They're calling it an "unbroken chain" pointing to guilt. It sounds more like a carefully constructed narrative, built with digital breadcrumbs. And the poor girl's family? They're still left with the fact that their daughter is gone, and all the technology in the world can't bring her back. It's just another way to get a conviction, not necessarily true justice.

Then there's Rex Kanyike Masai, shot during protests. Now they're digging through CCTV footage and phone triangulation. They're even recalling witnesses, because apparently the initial investigation was a bit too clean and tidy. IPOA, the police watchdog, is getting stonewalled. Missing records, misleading information - sounds like business as usual. They're trying to pin it on a specific officer using his phone data and facial recognition. It's a digital witch hunt, and frankly, I'm not holding my breath for them to find actual accountability. This is why we need more than just fancy tech; we need people who are willing to do the hard work, not just chase the digital dust. It's like trying to navigate the creator economy with just a basic phone; you'll get lost.

And the stories just keep coming. Peter Mugure, George Muchai, Monica Kimani, Willie Kimani. All these grim tales where technology plays the starring role. They can track who sent money to whom, who was where, what was said, what was deleted. It's a digital autopsy of a life. They’re painting a picture of meticulously planned murders. But let's not forget, these are still just tools. The real problem isn't the lack of technology; it's the people who use it, and the system that allows them to manipulate it. Until that changes, these "cracked" cases will just be more notches on a belt of a broken system.
