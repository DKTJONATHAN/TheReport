Here we go again. Another week, another grand pronouncement from the big men on the hill, telling us Kenya will be a "First World, high income, industrialised nation" within a generation. President Ruto's inviting us to a "National Conversation" to write a new development charter, because apparently Vision 2030, with its decade-long promises, just wasn't ambitious enough. Let's be real, at our current crawl, that "First World" status is a date our great-great-grandchildren might see - around the year 2200. We're at $2,400 GNI per person, aiming for $14,375, and they think we'll bridge that gap by 2063? That's not a vision, that's a prayer meeting without the miracles.

The numbers don't lie, even if our politicians do. To hit that 2063 target, our income per person needs to grow by seven percent annually for 37 years, demanding a GDP growth of roughly nine percent. Vision 2030 aimed for 10 percent and delivered a pathetic 4.9. Our best year since the 70s was 7.1 percent, and even that crashed the very next year. This isn't just an ambitious plan; it's a desperate gamble, much like [Marvel's decades-long movie plan that now shows a desperate grasp for relevance](/posts/marvel-s-decades-long-movie-plan-shows-desperate-grasp-for-relevance). Only 34 middle-income economies have ever made this leap since 1990, and a third of those cheated with EU accession or oil. Meanwhile, we're still lower-middle income, watching Vietnam slowly climb after 35 years of actual reforms, while we cook up another fantasy.

The inconvenient truth about every economy that's actually made such a leap is investment - massive, relentless investment. The Asian tigers ploughed 25 to 40 percent of their national output back into productive assets. Vietnam and South Korea still run capital formation around 32 percent of GDP. Kenya? We're at a measly 16.8 percent, below the global average and far, far below our own 1978 peak of 29.8 percent. Our savings rates are just as dismal. But why invest when public debt has swollen past Sh13 trillion, gobbling up nearly 91 percent of ordinary revenue just to service itself? It's like planning a lavish wedding while your house is on fire.

To its credit, the report at least talks about sequence - land reform, then agriculture, then manufacturing, then technology. But talk is cheap. Manufacturing has shrunk to 7.2 percent of GDP, far from Vision 2030's 15 percent target, and 83.6 percent of our workforce is stuck in the informal sector. They want to ground this charter in Article 43, making development an "obligation owed to the governed." Sounds noble, but without concrete numbers, without confronting the thorny issue of land redistribution that actually made the Asian sequence work, it's just fancy words on paper, another empty promise in a long line of them.

And the timing? A "national conversation" starting August 12, with the vision launching by the end of 2026. Four months for national consensus on something that's supposed to transform a nation? That's not a conversation, it's a rubber stamp for what's already been decided in boardrooms. The real test isn't fancy phrases, it's numbers: a hard investment rate, a brutal savings target, a manufacturing share of GDP, a debt-service ceiling that actually means something, and a realistic date. Leave those out, and we'll be here in another 20 years, reading about the next grand delusion, wondering why this one also failed.
