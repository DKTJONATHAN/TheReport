Ruto Wants Camels, Goats, Sheep for Milk as Prices Hit Sh85 a Litre. Good Luck With That.

The President, bless his farming heart, thinks a bit of camel juice will solve our milk crisis. As if Nairobians are just going to swap their familiar carton for a goat's udder. With milk now costing more than designer water in some estates, the idea of diversifying sounds like another grand plan that will mostly benefit the fellas in the arid lands. While he's busy dreaming up buffalo milk futures, the rest of us are just trying to afford a decent cup of tea.

The President's big idea to diversify our milk sources to include camels, goats, and sheep, while a novel thought, conveniently ignores the practicalities. It's easy to stand on a podium and talk about "improved camel breeds" when you're not the one queuing at a milk ATM that's run dry or shelling out Sh85 for a litre of the stuff. He’s even talking about buffaloes, for crying out loud. The laughter from the audience probably wasn’t about mirth, but the sheer absurdity of it all. Meanwhile, CS Kagwe is busy playing down the "shortage" with fancy words like "decline in supply," which is just polite talk for "we messed up."

The government’s attempts to reform the dairy sector, including "changes" at KCC, are meant to make farmers happy. Apparently, farmers shouldn't wait "two or three months" for their money. Right. Because who has that kind of patience when bills are due? The President brags about a rise in milk production from 4.5 billion to 5.5 billion litres, making us Africa’s largest producer. So, if we’re producing so much, why is the price of milk going up faster than a matatu on Waiyaki Way? This sounds like the same old story we've heard before, not unlike how the government no longer fears artists, it just uses them.

The summit itself, with its lofty theme of "Agriculture and Food Systems Transformation - Advancing Food Sovereignty, Job Creation, and Shared Prosperity under BETA," sounds like a lot of hot air. Thousands of farmers, officials, and partners gathered to discuss food production. The World Bank is pledging billions, which is great, but we’ve heard promises before. Governor Njuki from Tharaka Nithi pointed out the obvious: increased production is great, but if we don’t have the markets and processing capacity to handle it, it’s just more potential wastage.

While the President is busy cracking jokes about buffaloes and the World Bank talks about investing in irrigation and rural roads, the reality for many Kenyans is a struggle. The idea that camel milk will suddenly become a staple in Nairobi is a stretch, and the talk of improved earnings for farmers rings hollow when the retail price of milk is soaring. It’s a classic case of disconnect between the podium and the pavement.

The President’s latest pronouncements on land for fisheries and a marine centre of excellence at the Coast sound like more government initiatives to be managed, or mismanaged. He’s warning cartels away from public land, which is a good start, but let's see if these threats translate into actual action or just more headlines. The government is committed to working with various partners, but until these "reforms" actually make basic necessities like milk affordable, it’s all just a lot of noise.
