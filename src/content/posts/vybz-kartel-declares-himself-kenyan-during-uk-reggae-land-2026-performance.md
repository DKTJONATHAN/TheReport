VYBZ KARTEL SAYS HE'S KENYAN AT UK GIG, FORGETS HE'S JAMAICAN

WARNING: Another case of a foreign celebrity playing pretend with Kenyan identity for clout. Will we ever learn?

Vybz Kartel, the "World Boss" from Jamaica, apparently decided to ditch his passport for a night at Reggae Land 2026 in the UK. Before 60,000 screaming fans, he declared himself Kenyan. Yes, Kenyan. Because nothing says authentic like a dancehall star claiming heritage he doesn't have on stage, especially after mentioning a list of African countries. It’s all about the cheers, isn't it?

This little stunt, of course, went viral back home. Nairobi, apparently, is one of his top streaming cities. Who knew? Kenyans have apparently been riding with Kartel's "Gaza" crew and adopting his slang and fashion for years, even when he was locked up. It's a cult of personality, a testament to how easily people can be swayed by catchy tunes and a few Swahili phrases thrown in for good measure. It makes you wonder if we’re just looking for validation from anyone who bothers to notice us, even if it’s just for a fleeting moment. It’s not unlike how some parts of Nairobi are just fancy names for trouble; this feels like a similar kind of superficiality.

The "World Boss" even hinted his fiancée is pregnant. So, a new heir to the "Gaza" empire is on the way, born no doubt with a Kenyan flag sewn into its tiny onesie. Meanwhile, the rest of us are here dealing with the actual realities of life, like the perpetual struggle to make ends meet. But hey, at least we have Vybz Kartel, who, for one night, decided we were cool enough to be his people. It’s a flattering gesture, I guess, if you ignore the fact that it’s likely just a calculated move to maintain his massive fan base here.

The show itself sounds like a typical festival spectacle - a crown shaped stage, hits like "Fever," and a duet with Shenseea. Shaggy and Beenie Man were also there, probably exchanging knowing glances. The whole thing is a performance, a carefully crafted illusion. And we, the audience, lap it up. Maybe one day we’ll realize that true Kenyan pride doesn't come from a Jamaican artist declaring allegiance on a stage halfway across the world. But until then, let's keep cheering. It’s what we do.







Vybz Kartel, the self-proclaimed "World Boss" from Jamaica, decided to shake things up at Reggae Land 2026 in the United Kingdom by declaring himself Kenyan during his headline set. Addressing a crowd of over 60,000, he mentioned various African nations before dropping the bombshell: "I was born in Jamaica, but I’m actually Kenyan." This lighthearted declaration was met with cheers and quickly spread like wildfire across social media, particularly in Kenya, where Kartel has a fiercely loyal following.

It’s no secret that Nairobi remains one of Kartel’s top streaming cities, and his influence here is undeniable. Even during his incarceration, Kenyan fans kept his music popular across East Africa. His style has seeped into Kenya’s dancehall scene and even influenced the Gengetone movement, from slang to fashion. Some Nairobi street groups have even adopted the "Gaza" name in tribute to his crew. It’s a testament to how deeply his music and persona have penetrated local culture, making him a figure of admiration, if not always of accurate representation.

During his 90-minute set, which closed the festival’s final day, Kartel made a dramatic entrance under a towering crown-shaped stage. He powered through hits like "Fever" and "Summertime," even performing "Panic" live for the first time with Shenseea. Amidst the musical revelry and alongside the performances of other legends like Shaggy and Beenie Man, Kartel also appeared to announce that his fiancée, Sidem Öztürk, is pregnant with their first child. This adds another layer to his international persona, intertwined with his declared Kenyan connection.

While Kartel’s admiration for Kenya is evident through his repeated self-identification and use of Swahili phrases, the reality is he is Jamaican by birth and nationality. This declaration, while seemingly harmless and intended to please his dedicated Kenyan fans, underscores a broader trend of foreign artists leveraging local identities for engagement. It’s a situation that’s becoming increasingly common, and frankly, a bit tiresome. It raises questions about authenticity and whether such pronouncements hold genuine weight or are merely strategic moves in the grand theatre of celebrity. This constant seeking of external validation feels familiar, not unlike how [Runda is just a fancy name for a crime scene](/posts/runda-is-just-a-fancy-name-for-a-crime-scene) - a veneer of prestige masking something else entirely.
