NAIROBI'S "PROFESSIONAL" SHOOT FEE: ANOTHER WAY TO MILK THE STREETS

WARNING: Nairobi's latest financial move could turn the city's vibrant streets into an expensive obstacle course for budding creatives, with ambiguous rules poised to catch anyone trying to make a living.

This is Nairobi, where dreams come to die, or at least get a hefty bill.

Tom Mboya Street on a Sunday, eh? Used to be a free-for-all, an open-air studio for anyone with a phone camera and enough nerve to ask strangers for a few bob. Sakaja, in his infinite wisdom, decided to remove the photography licensing fee, and suddenly everyone and their cousin was a street photographer, or a Gen Z dance crew filming their next TikTok hit. Good for them, I guess. A bit of hustle, no studio rent, just pure Nairobi grit. But of course, the county government can't let a good hustle go unchecked, can they?

Now, under this new Finance Act, 2026, they want KES 5,000 a day for a "professional" photo shoot. Music videos? Same price. Larger productions? Brace yourselves. The real kicker is the "professional" part. What does that even mean? Does a guy taking portraits of tourists count? Or is it only for the big boys with their fancy lights and crew? The schedule is as clear as a muddy Nairobi river. It could be KES 10,000 a year for individuals, or KES 5,000 a day. Or both. Who knows? It's the classic Nairobi game: introduce a rule, then watch people scramble to figure it out while the money keeps flowing.

And don't even get me started on the content creators. A dance crew filming a video? Suddenly they're "music video production." The photographer snapping them? A "professional photo shoot." It's a free-for-all of fees. Plus, the Kenya Film Classification Board wants its cut too. So, KES 5,000 for a music video from them, then another KES 5,000 from the county? It's enough to make you wonder if they're trying to fund the entire city on the backs of young people trying to make a few shillings. It reminds me of some of the youth empowerment scams we see cropping up everywhere, like that badminton circus in Nanyuki. Look, I get it. Big commercial shoots can be a nuisance. They block pavements, attract crowds, and generally make life difficult. But a solo photographer with one camera is not a Hollywood production. The county needs to be clear. Who are they targeting? Is it the established studios, or the kid on the street trying to earn lunch money? Because right now, it just feels like another tax grab, designed to make it harder for the little guy to survive in this city. If they're not careful, they'll turn our streets from an accessible studio into just another place where the cost of breathing is too damn high.







Tom Mboya Street on a Sunday afternoon, once a free-for-all for budding photographers and Gen Z content creators, is about to get a whole lot more expensive. Governor Sakaja’s previous move to scrap photography licensing fees made the CBD an accessible "open-air studio." Anyone with a camera and ambition could set up shop and earn a few shillings, charging a modest KES 100 per shot. It was a small win for the hustlers, turning the city streets into their unofficial office.

But Nairobi giveth, and Nairobi taketh away, usually with a new set of charges. Enter the Nairobi City County Finance Act, 2026. Suddenly, a "professional" photo shoot could cost KES 5,000 a day. Music videos? Same price. Larger productions? Prepare your pockets. The problem is, the law is as clear as a murky chai. What exactly constitutes a "professional" shoot? Is it the guy taking portraits of tourists, or a full-blown advertising crew? The legislation offers no distinction, leaving everyone guessing.

The confusion doesn't stop there. For a solo photographer or content creator, the annual fee for a "single employee or professional" is KES 10,000. But does that apply here? And if so, does the KES 5,000 daily fee also stack on top? The legal jargon is a labyrinth designed to catch the unwary. A dance crew filming could be slapped with a music video fee, while the photographer documenting them could be hit with a professional shoot charge. It's a bureaucratic nightmare.

To add insult to injury, the Kenya Film Classification Board also has its own set of fees, including KES 5,000 for music videos. It's unclear if these county fees are in addition to those already existing. While regulating large productions that clog up walkways is a legitimate concern, the current ambiguity means small-scale creators are likely to bear the brunt. It’s a familiar pattern, reminiscent of how certain programs are advertised as "youth empowerment" when they're really just another avenue for exploitation. The intention might be to regulate the chaos of large film crews, but the current wording threatens to stifle the very creativity it claims to foster. If the county can't clearly define who pays what, and for whom, the CBD could quickly transition from an accessible creative hub to another financial barrier for young Kenyans trying to make a living. This isn't about progress; it's about finding new ways to tax the hustle.
