STATE BAILS OUT KENYA AIRWAYS AGAIN WITH SH350 MILLION LOAN

The government continues to prop up the national airline with yet another hefty loan, a predictable cycle of taxpayer money thrown at a black hole. Don't expect this to fix anything permanently.

Another day, another bail out for KQ. This time it's a cool $350 million shareholder loan, conveniently timed as the airline sinks deeper into the red. President Ruto's office assures us it's for "urgent financial obligations" and a "turnaround plan." We've heard that song and dance before, haven't we? It’s the same old story, just with a new tune.

This latest injection of cash, disbursed in tranches and overseen by the finance ministry, comes on top of a jaw-dropping Sh122 billion (about $940 million) in existing government loans being converted into equity. So, not only are we lending them more, but we're also turning bad debt into shares. It’s like trying to fix a leaky roof by pouring more water on it. Remember when JKIA was supposed to be the "East Africa Gateway"? This whole situation smells like another self-inflicted wound, just like that failed ambition.

KQ’s supposed revival in 2024, where they miraculously posted a profit, seems to have evaporated faster than a mirage in the Turkana desert. The first half of this year saw them plunge back into losses, thanks in no small part to soaring fuel prices. They're now staring down a pre-tax loss of Sh15.92 billion. This airline is chronically addicted to government handouts, with the state already holding nearly half the company and about 90% of its loans. It’s a classic case of the taxpayer footing the bill for corporate incompetence.

The government's grand plan seems to be keeping KQ afloat in the faint hope of attracting a "strategic investor." Chairman Kiprono Kittony is spouting lines about "interest," but where are the actual deals? Until someone with real money commits, and KQ actually learns to fly without crashing, we’ll be seeing more of these "rescues." It's a never-ending cycle of hope and disappointment, much like expecting anything substantial from those promised salary hikes. Even the consortium of banks that converted their debt into equity in 2017, and KLM's dwindling stake, can't escape the reality: KQ is a bottomless pit. The public might get a temporary bump on the stock market, but the long-term outlook is as murky as the Nairobi River after a downpour. This is not a rescue; it's a prolonged, expensive, and ultimately futile attempt to keep a sinking ship from going under.
