So, the Kenyan cabinet, in its infinite wisdom, has decided to throw another $350 million of our money at Kenya Airways. Apparently, the airline has some "urgent financial obligations." Shocking, I know. It's like watching a leaky bucket with a slightly larger hole plugged for a bit - it’ll just spring another leak somewhere else.

This is the kind of "support" that makes you wonder if they're actually trying to save the airline or just delay the inevitable funeral procession. We've seen this movie before. Remember when President Ruto was 'activating' responses to floods? [Expect More Drip, Less Drop](https://www.example.com/posts/ruto--activates--response-as-floods-hit-kenya---expect-more-drip--less-drop) was the sentiment then, and it rings just as true now. More money, same old problems.

Let's be clear, this isn't an investment in a brighter future; it's a band-aid for a gaping wound. These "shareholder financing" deals are just a polite way of saying the government is dipping its hand deeper into our pockets to prop up a failing enterprise. It's a cyclical drain, a black hole for taxpayer funds masquerading as "urgent obligations."

What this really means is more of the same: inflated ticket prices, questionable service, and the constant threat of another bailout down the line. It’s a familiar script in this part of the world, where good intentions often pave the road to financial quicksand.

Meanwhile, while we're busy footing the bill for a struggling airline, other things are happening. We've seen [CAF halting ticket sales](https://www.example.com/posts/CAT-ticketing-suspended) amid safety crises, because clearly, not every crisis gets the same level of urgent attention or cash injection. It's a stark reminder of where priorities lie.

So, congratulations, Kenya Airways. You've secured another lifeline. For the rest of us, well, at least the planes will *probably* keep flying, for a little while longer. Just don't ask about the long-term plan. There isn't one.
