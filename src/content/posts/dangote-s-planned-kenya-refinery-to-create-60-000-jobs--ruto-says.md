RUTO PROMISES 60,000 JOBS FROM DANGOTE'S KENYA REFINERY - WE'LL SEE.

Another grand pronouncement from State House, another shiny promise. This time it's about oil, refineries, and jobs. As if we haven't heard this song and dance before.

President Ruto, fresh from a tour of Nigeria's massive Dangote refinery, is now touting a similar project for Lamu. Africa's richest man, Aliko Dangote, is planning a $15 to $16 billion oil refinery, supposedly ready by 2030. Ruto claims it will secure fuel, boost industry, and pump out 60,000 jobs, with spillover effects into fertilizers, chemicals, and packaging. Dangote himself chirped about how the refinery is a "gate," and once opened, "shocked" investors will flood in. Right.

Let's talk about this "gate" and the supposed shockwaves. Kenya is drowning in imported fuel, shelling out billions of shillings every year just to keep the lights on and the cars moving. Demand is only going up. This refinery is meant to solve that, serving not just Kenya but a whole host of our less fortunate neighbors. Sounds noble, doesn't it? The reality is, these mega-projects often become sinkholes of cash, with the promised benefits often evaporating like mist on a hot Nairobi day. Remember the endless talk about infrastructure projects that never quite materialize? This feels suspiciously similar. We've seen how promises of prosperity can quickly turn into a different kind of industry, like the one I wrote about before, the one that thrives on [blood, billions, and the big lie: Kenya’s road carnage industry](/posts/blood--billions--and-the-big-lie--kenya-s-road-carnage-industry).

And then there's the money. Dangote is talking about internal cash flow, bonds, and an IPO. East African countries might even chip in with a combined 30% equity stake. Meanwhile, Dangote is busy expanding his Nigerian operation, eyeing a valuation that makes your head spin and seeking billions in an IPO, boasting impressive profits in his prospectus. It’s a lot of zeroes, a lot of ambitious plans. But when the dust settles, who truly benefits? Will the jobs be for Kenyans, or for expatriates? Will the profits stay here, or flow out to some distant shareholder? History suggests we should be skeptical.

The Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority is projecting even higher demand for fuel. Our current infrastructure, bless its heart, is already a regional supply route. This refinery is supposed to be bigger and better than the one in Lagos, even generating significant power from petcoke. But let's not forget the context: Kenya is still heavily reliant on imports, and the import bill is eye-watering. We've been here before, chasing the dragon of self-sufficiency, only to end up with more debt and fewer solutions.

It’s easy to get swept up in the presidential rhetoric and the billionaire's vision. But a realist looks at the numbers, the track record, and the usual outcomes. 60,000 jobs? A stronger fuel security? More downstream businesses? I’ll believe it when I see it. Until then, it’s just another grand vision painted in the colours of hope, laid over the gritty canvas of Kenyan reality.
