So, Kenya wants to be a "globally competitive industrialising economy." That's rich. The whole grand plan, apparently, hinges on "skills." Funny how that's the one thing everyone agrees on, yet nobody seems to deliver. Employers moan about not finding the right people for modern jobs, from farms to tech, while our universities churn out graduates with PhDs in unemployment. It’s the same old song and dance across Africa, and Nairobi is no exception.

The problem isn't a lack of meetings or fancy speeches. Governments, NGOs, training colleges - they all nod along, saying "TVET is important!" But the real issue, the one that keeps us stuck in neutral, is coordination. Swisscontact, bless their well-intentioned hearts, points out the obvious: these partnerships work when they’re built into how things are done, not when they're just another donor-funded project that’ll vanish when the cheque clears. We’ve seen it before, haven't we? Enthusiasm flares up, money flows in for new equipment or allowances, and then… poof. It all fizzles out.

The real kicker is that businesses only commit when they see a direct profit. Surprise, surprise. If training leads to better productivity and less staff turnover, then maybe, just maybe, they'll bother. Swisscontact’s report bragging about a 30 percent return on investment from initiatives like PropelA? That’s the language the bosses understand, not the feel-good stories about empowering youth. Training institutions get a boost when their graduates actually get jobs, and the government likes it when the economy supposedly grows. It’s all about what’s in it for them, not about a genuine national effort.

And let's not even get started on "institutional mechanisms." Visionary leaders might kick things off, but it's the boring, bureaucratic structures like sector committees and advisory boards that are supposed to keep the wheels turning. The problem is, in Kenya, we’re good at creating forums, but terrible at giving them the teeth, resources, and accountability to actually do anything. It's another case of putting lipstick on a pig. We need real labour market information, not just assumptions, driving decisions. Otherwise, we’re just guessing what skills are needed while the world races ahead. Remember that chaos at JKIA? It shows how our systems, even for basic things, are fragile. [JKIA screening chaos exposes Ebola risk gaps in Kenya](/posts/jkia-screening-chaos-exposes-ebola-risk-gaps-in-kenya).

Then there's the money. If these skills partnerships are always begging for donor handouts, they're doomed from the start. True sustainability means everyone chips in - government sets the rules, industry invests through apprenticeships or levies, and colleges put in their own resources. Building trust is also a joke. Businesses get frustrated when they waste time with no influence, and colleges get disillusioned when industry participation is as reliable as a matatu on a Sunday morning. It takes more than a few meetings; it takes consistency and results, not just the promise of future benefits.

Ultimately, the real test isn't about launching more "partnerships." It's about making sure the ones we have actually become part of how things work, creating value long after the projects end. Until we stop treating skills development like a series of temporary gigs and start building enduring systems with clear incentives and accountability, Kenya will just keep spinning its wheels, dreaming of a future that remains stubbornly out of reach.
