So, the chickens have finally come home to roost, or more accurately, the rats have finally started admitting they gnawed the cheese. We always knew it, didn't we? From the streets to the chai joints, everyone whispered about it: Parliament is for sale. And now, the "honourable" members themselves are practically reading from the same script, confirming that the price of a vote, whether for impeachment, a finance bill, or even just staying out of jail, is clearly marked. Junet Mohamed lighting the fuse? Please. This explosion has been brewing for years. The only difference is now, instead of rumour, we have "admissions" from people who are supposed to be running this country.

Langata's own Jalang'o, bless his entertainment-filled heart, practically chortled about MPs getting paid. And Makueni's Dan Maanzo? He paints a picture of state agents dangling millions and threats like a Christmas decoration gone wrong. Four to ten million shillings? For a vote? That's more than most Kenyans earn in a decade. And the best part? They don't even name names. "Fear for my safety," he says. Of course, safety. Because admitting you were offered a bribe, and then not reporting it, is just another day at the office for these "public servants." It’s a theatre of the absurd, where confessions are made under duress, and then conveniently forgotten.

And it's not just about Gachagua, is it? Remember the Finance Bill 2024? The one that left bodies in the streets and buildings burning because the masses felt Parliament wouldn't listen? Juja MP George Koimburi spilled the beans about Ksh.2 million per MP, then quickly retracted it like a bad joke. Even Ruto himself, the man at the top, casually accused parliamentary committees of demanding bribes. He admits the system is a marketplace. A marketplace where laws are bought and sold, where even anti-corruption bills are allegedly greased with Ksh.10 million bribes. We're not talking about minor indiscretions; we're talking about the very pillars of our governance being auctioned off. It reminds me of the old saying, "if you can't beat them, join them," or in this case, "if you can't beat them, just buy them." It’s a shame our leaders aren't as adept at fixing infrastructure as they are at fixing votes.

This isn't about scattered bad apples anymore. This is a systemic rot, a capture of our supposed oversight body by whoever has the fattest wallet. The constitution envisions a Parliament that balances the executive, a check on power. Instead, we have a rubber stamp that bends to the highest bidder, often the Executive itself. When laws are passed not through deliberation but through financial inducement, ordinary Kenyans pay the price. Higher taxes, ridiculous levies, and when all else fails, deadly street confrontations. The Gachagua impeachment, once a solemn constitutional process, is now just another transaction. It’s a betrayal of devolution, a mockery of representative consent, and a clear message to citizens: don't bother participating; the game is rigged. This is why discussions about national development often feel like déjà vu, like the arguments we had during the [Macron and Ruto's Nairobi Duet: Another French Act with African Financing](/posts/macron-and-ruto-s-nairobi-duet--another-french-act-with-african-financing) - grand pronouncements with little tangible change for the average Kenyan.

The irony is that the tools to fix this are staring us in the face: the EACC, the DCI, the courts. Legislators have practically handed them a case on a silver platter. But will they act? Or will we see the usual charade - investigations that lead nowhere, apologies that erase memory, and then… silence. The Powers and Privileges Committee has a history of demanding apologies, not investigations. The courts, bless their hearts, did give Gachagua some damages, but that doesn't un-impeach him. We need robust rules on political financing, not just more committees asking for more apologies. And while Gen Z might have shown us the power of public mobilization, it’s a shame we have to take to the streets to get our elected officials to do their jobs.

Ultimately, it comes down to us, the voters. Can we remember who sold out when election day rolls around in 2027? Or will we be distracted by the next scandal, the next handshake, the next shiny promise, forgetting that the "House of Bribes" has sold its soul? The fact that this isn't coming from outside agitators, but from within the very institution, should make it harder to ignore. But knowing Kenya, I won't hold my breath. The "House for Hire" might just be the accurate name for the circus we call Parliament.
