So, this young chap, a journalist turned political cheerleader, decided to use TikTok dances and selfies to get the youth to register to vote. And what do you know? Two point six million new names on the list. Apparently, it was the "Gen Z way" - "fun, trendy, sexy, fashionable." They call it FOMO, fear of missing out. It's funny, isn't it? The same generation that got tear-gassed and shot at for protesting a tax bill is now being told to get involved in the very system that failed them. They're angry, alright, and they've got the numbers. More than 30% of us who can vote are under 35. But can digital rebellion actually translate into real political power? Or is it just another flash in the pan?

These analysts, like Tom Mboya, call the youth movements a "wild card." They pop up anywhere, require next to nothing, and can sway things. Remember those finance bill protests? Political enough for you. Now, if they decide to throw their weight behind some candidate, that candidate might just have a decent shot. The clever part is that these digitally savvy activists are bypassing the usual party and tribal nonsense that anchors Kenyan politics. This one, Allans, is smart enough not to back a specific politician. He’s talking "leadership," not "politicians," building a force outside the ethnic voting blocs. He claims they're a "swing vote" because nobody knows who they'll pick. We'll see.

President Ruto, bless his heart, is apparently facing "strong head winds" with less than a year to go. Three-quarters of people think the country's going the wrong way, and two-thirds say their pockets are lighter since he took over in 2022. But don't count the man out. He’s a master organiser, with the whole state apparatus at his disposal. Plus, his rivals are a mess, a bunch of squabbling chickens. Meanwhile, Nairobi Senator Edwin Sifuna, the "Linda Mwananchi" chap, is making noise. He’s the one who apparently tried to stop police from shooting protesters at parliament. Now he’s out there, talking about human rights and the constitution, trying to rally the "disaffected youth." He hasn't declared for president, but he's already on the road, telling people they need a "compelling message" for a "new country." Sounds familiar.

The young people we're hearing from are tired of promises. Brandon Muremi, 23, wants a leader who understands their "common problems." Conrad Mutangili, 19, wants someone who "can actually accomplish" and deliver, not just talk. They're tired of promises of "sweets" but feeling no "sugar." Yvonne Kileme, 22, wants "immediate results" and, of course, "employment, employment, employment." Ruto's lot, like UDA youth leader Kariuki Ngunjiri, are busy pushing their "tangible development" narrative, dismissing social media buzz. They claim the president is "listening" and giving jobs, giving them a "head start." They were even handing out 350 Kenyan shillings to attendees at a town hall meeting. It’s a common practice, of course. As political analyst Mboya points out, "money talks in an election here."

All this talk of change and youth power is fine and dandy, but let's be real. For all their digital savvy and rejection of the "old politics," the building blocks of Kenyan elections have always been ethnicity and cold, hard cash. We've seen promising movements fizzle out before, and the system is designed to resist disruption. While the energy of these Gen Z voters is a factor, nobody knows for sure if it will translate into actual votes that change anything significant. It’s still early days, and the same old tactics are likely to be deployed. Remember how Ruto and the opposition are eyeing Western Kenya votes? We've seen that same day blitz before.
