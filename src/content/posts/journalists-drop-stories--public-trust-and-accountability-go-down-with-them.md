So, Okoth Obado finally got his day in court, and guess what? The media actually stuck around to see it through. Shocking, I know. They're calling it "follow-up journalism" - a rare beast these days, like spotting a unicorn in Westlands. It seems some stories, especially the ones involving big names or easily trackable court dates, get the attention. But let's not get too excited, this is more the exception than the rule.

The rest of us are left with a pile of headlines that flash and fade faster than a politician's promise. Murders, calamities, scandals - they hit the front page, then disappear into the Nairobi smog. Why? Because effective follow-up journalism is hard work, and for most outlets, including this one, it’s a luxury they can’t afford. The unwritten contract with us, the audience, is broken. We're told something is important, then left hanging, wondering if that tortured energy agency boss ever faced consequences, or if the abuse of power allegations against Ms. Agnes Muthigani Ndanu were anything more than a passing mention. It leaves you feeling cheated, and frankly, just plain tired.

And it’s not just the dramatic, horror-movie-esque stuff. Remember the former energy agency boss accused of torturing employees? Held incommunicado, beaten, stripped, threatened with death - sounds like a typical Tuesday in some of our corporate offices, but this was a police matter. Now, were those suspects arraigned? Was the money returned? Did the boss ever face any real heat? We’ll probably never know, because the story just… stopped. It’s a pattern. Like the Embakasi gas explosion, the fake fertiliser scandal, or the Hillside Endarasha school fire - these aren't just news items, they're open wounds that fester when the media stops digging.

Then you have the sheer audacity of some public officials. Take those three senior energy officials who "resigned" over a fuel scandal. Resigned? Or were they pushed out the door before they could confess to robbing us blind? We're still waiting for answers on investigations, charges, and whether any public money has been recovered. It’s the same story with the bodies dumped in Kware quarry, or the "shocking escape" of suspect Collins Jumaisi from Gigiri Police Station. Was he found? Were the cops on duty held accountable? It's a cycle of reporting, then forgetting, that benefits no one but those who want to avoid scrutiny.

This constant dropping of stories means investigations get compromised, justice is denied, and promises of help in calamities go unfulfilled. It’s no wonder we have people in positions of responsibility evading accountability. It’s like that report about the Iran war and its impact on fertilizer for Africa; the big players grab what they want, and the rest of us are left with the crumbs, wondering what happened to the promises of a better system. When the media fails to follow up, it’s not just a disservice to the audience, it's a green light for corruption and incompetence to thrive.

The Nation Media Group is asking us to hold them accountable, but it’s hard to hold someone accountable when they don’t even bother to tell you what happened in the first place. This isn't just about reporting events; it's about ensuring that those who mess up, especially public officers, actually face the music. When stories are abandoned, the public loses faith, and that, my friends, is a dangerous state of affairs for any country trying to move forward.
