For decades, the world has operated on the back of the yen carry trade. Investors borrowed cheap money in Japan to fuel speculative bubbles everywhere else. Now that the Bank of Japan is forced to raise rates toward 1 percent, the party is over. The 96 billion dollars spent over two days is a drop in the bucket compared to the trillions in leveraged positions that are now looking for the exit.

The US Treasury is only participating because it has no choice. Japan is the largest foreign holder of US debt. If Tokyo is forced to sell its 1.14 trillion dollars in Treasuries to support the yen, the long end of the yield curve will reprice in a way that Washington cannot afford. As the US government tries to borrow trillions more for AI infrastructure and power projects, it cannot have its biggest creditor turning into its biggest seller.

Bitcoin traders are watching this with justifiable paranoia. While the initial intervention did not trigger an immediate collapse, the writing is on the wall. A stronger yen means the cost of financing risk assets is rising. The momentary recovery of Bitcoin above 64,000 dollars is likely a dead cat bounce in a world where liquidity is being sucked back to the Japanese domestic market.

The use of the Fed’s FIMA Repo Facility is another cynical layer to this story. It allows Japan to borrow dollars against their Treasuries instead of selling them, effectively hiding the market impact of their intervention. It is a shell game designed to keep the illusion of demand alive while the underlying foundation of the global bond market erodes.

Rising Japanese government bond yields are the real signal. They have reached their highest levels since 1995. As domestic returns in Japan become attractive for the first time in a generation, the flow of capital will reverse. No amount of coordinated communication between Scott Bessent and Satsuki Katayama can stop the physics of capital flight. Gravity always wins while the elite fly over our mess.

Ultimately, the world is witnessing the end of the cheap-money era that defined the post-2008 landscape. The coordinated intervention is a temporary bandage on a systemic wound. When the largest creditor in the world decides it is time to go home, the rest of the risk market will be left holding the bag.
