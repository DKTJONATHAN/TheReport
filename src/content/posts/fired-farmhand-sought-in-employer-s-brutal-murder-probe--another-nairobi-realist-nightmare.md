So, this Lydia Wathitha, 60, a farmer in Kirinyaga, thought she could play God by hiring some random farmhand named Victor Kayari. Turns out, Kayari, a mere three months into the job, had a knack for "misbehaving," which in Kenyan terms means pocketing Sh10,200 of his employer's cash. The husband, James Njoka, caught him red-handed on CCTV, the modern-day equivalent of a "wanted" poster. They took him to the police, and what happened next? He promised to pay it back and was let go. This isn't a fairy tale; this is how things work.

Naturally, after being given a clean slate and a swift kick out the door, Kayari decided "revenge" was the best path. Who needs actual justice when you can have blood? He allegedly marched back to the homestead, not for a chat, but to hack Ms. Wathitha to death. Then, because subtlety is clearly not his strong suit, he wrapped her body in a blanket and tossed her into an abandoned well. Because apparently, a shallow grave or a nice funeral would have been too much effort.

Njoka returned home to find a pool of blood, a missing wife, and a locked house - a classic "I went to get milk and never came back" scenario, but with more hacking. The trail of blood, because apparently, criminals are bad at cleaning up after themselves, led them to the well. There, they found the gruesome remains. It’s a scene straight out of a B-grade horror flick, except this is real life, and the budget for sanity is perpetually low.

The family is crying for justice, demanding the suspect be "pursued and punished." Bless their naive hearts. This whole charade highlights the gaping holes in our so called "justice" system. He steals, gets caught, promises to pay, walks free, and then commits murder. It’s a perfectly logical progression in this country, isn't it? You get the sense that even the police are probably thinking, "Well, what did you expect?" It’s a stark reminder that sometimes, the Temple of Justice Has No Ramp, But Runda Has a Padel Court; it’s a system designed to protect the privileged, not necessarily the innocent.

The local MCA is condemning the killing, urging detectives to do their job. The police boss assures everyone a manhunt is underway and asks for patience. Patience? While a murderer is out there? This is Kenya, people. Patience is what you cultivate while waiting for your turn in a broken system, or while watching your hard-earned cash disappear into the pockets of corrupt officials. The only thing certain here is that another life has been needlessly extinguished, and the wheels of justice, if they move at all, will grind slower than a snail on molasses.
