So, the courts have decided the election *should* have been yesterday, not next year. Big deal. Does anyone actually think this changes anything for the mwananchi struggling to put food on the table? We have this national habit of preparing for the *next* political circus long before we’ve even cleaned up the mess from the last one. Politicians are already eyeing the succession, forming alliances tighter than a matatu during rush hour, and treating opinion polls like some divine scripture. Meanwhile, the rest of us are just trying to survive.

The problem isn't that we start thinking about elections too early. It’s that we invest precisely zero energy in the country *between* these predictable power grabs. For years, 2027 has been this mythical destination, with every handshake, every visit, every whispered rumour dissected for its electoral mileage. Politicians calculate, the country watches, and ordinary life - you know, school fees, jobs, medical bills, surviving the market - just keeps chugging along, completely ignored by the political elite. It's like watching a high-stakes football match while your house is on fire; the match might be exciting, but it’s not going to save you.

Kenya has turned succession into a national sport, dissecting alliances like football tactics and reducing communities to mere voting blocs. It’s all about who gets power, not what they’ll actually *do* with it. Behind every percentage point in a poll is a real person with real problems that won't disappear when the pollsters pack up. Behind every shaky coalition are millions who will still need functioning institutions when the political deal inevitably crumbles. We’re obsessed with the trophy, not the game itself. It’s why we end up in these perpetual election modes, where every decision is filtered through the lens of the next campaign. It's a recipe for stagnation, making popular decisions over necessary ones. Just look at the [Mt Kenya Unity Faces Eight Political Battles: Prepare For The usual Mess](/posts/mt-kenya-unity-faces-eight-political-battles--prepare-for-the-usual-mess) - more drama, less substance.

This August 11 ruling should be a wake-up call, not another excuse for political theatre. It exposes our failure to build certainty around the basic rules of our democracy, let alone focus on actual governance. A stable democracy needs more than just elections; it needs institutions that work without fear, predictable timelines, and citizens who demand accountability, not just the right to vote. We need to shift the conversation from "who has the numbers?" to "who has the ideas?" from "which community produces the leader?" to "what policies will they implement?". Because elections change governments, but only good governance changes lives. And right now, we’ve got plenty of the former and a gaping hole where the latter should be.
