So, President Ruto has decided to have a "National Conversation." Apparently, it's about Kenya's future, way beyond this Vision 2030 nonsense that's mostly fizzled out anyway. He's talking about giving the country a "long-term destination" that belongs to "generations yet to come." Sounds nice, doesn't it? Like a politician promising you the moon while picking your pocket. But look closer, past the flowery language, and you see it: the same old tune, just a different singer.

This whole "architecture of the conversation" smells suspiciously like that grand plan Raila Odinga was pushing before he... well, before he couldn't push anything anymore. Both want citizens at the centre, moving beyond Nairobi's elite circles and into the villages. They want young people, experts, counties, academics, the whole choir. And both, supposedly, want to forge a "broad national consensus." It’s a carbon copy, dressed up in slightly different political safari suits.

The similarities are uncanny, especially the grassroots approach. Odinga wanted it to start at the polling station, moving upwards, a "real people's assembly." Ruto's team, with Kisumu Governor Prof Anyang' Nyong'o - who was apparently involved in the original Vision 2030 mess - is now figuring out if they should start at the ward, sub-county, or county level. It’s the same playbook, aimed at making it look like the people are in charge, not just the usual suspects in boardrooms.

Ruto insists this is a "people's project," not a government one. Funny, because that's exactly what Odinga was saying about his "Intergenerational National Conclave," designed to let ordinary Kenyans articulate their problems first. The President's lot are now talking about guarding this vision against political shifts, even suggesting it be anchored in law. But let's be real, the Building Bridges Initiative (BBI) taught us how quickly these "national" dreams can get tangled up in presidential succession games. Remember when Gachagua seemed to be eyeing Kindiki's turf? Politics is always about positioning.

The critics are already sharpening their knives. Why launch this now, just before elections? Where's the accounting for Vision 2030's failures? They're right, of course. The government should be explaining why it didn't hit the targets, not launching another grand promise that will likely end up as dust. Odinga’s plan never got to test its grassroots potential because, well, life happened. Now Ruto's got a similar idea, a new stage for what looks like the same old political theatre.

Ultimately, the question is whether this conversation will be about Kenya, or about extending the current administration's agenda. History, and the ghosts of failed initiatives past, suggest we should be skeptical. This isn't about a new destination; it's about finding a way to keep the current leadership's narrative alive. And knowing Nairobi, that's the real destination they're after.
