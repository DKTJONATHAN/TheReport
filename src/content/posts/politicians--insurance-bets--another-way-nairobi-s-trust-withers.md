Here we go again. Another sector where the average Kenyan just wants a fair shake, and another exposé showing how the big players - and by "big players" I mean the ones with political connections - are making sure that never happens. We're talking insurance, folks. Medical, auto, property - the stuff you actually need when life throws a curveball. And guess what? Turns out, when politicians get involved, your chances of actually getting paid when you need it plummet faster than Ruto's Sh10 diesel "relief".

The article talks about "organisational trust dimensions" like ability, benevolence, and integrity. For us regular Joes, it just means: can they pay? Do they care? Are they honest? When you see a politician's name tied to an insurance firm, ability becomes a question mark. Do they have the real cash to cover you if, God forbid, there's a real catastrophe, or is it all just smoke and mirrors? The regulations are there, sure, but who's really checking when the money men have friends in high places?

And benevolence? That's a good laugh. What we need is transparency. We need to know how many claims they're rejecting, not just the ones that make it to the regulator's desk. In places like the UK, they’ll tell you exactly how many claims they got, how many they paid, and how many they tossed aside. Here? It’s a mystery, and the politically connected ones seem to have a talent for making claims disappear. It’s like trying to get a straight answer from a politician about their campaign promises; you’ll likely end up more confused than when you started.

Then there’s integrity. This is where things get really sour. You pay your premiums faithfully, thinking you're covered. Then, when you have a genuine claim, they refuse. Your only recourse is to complain to the Insurance Regulatory Authority (IRA). But if the insurer is politically connected, who do you think the IRA is going to side with? It’s a rigged game, plain and simple. We've seen how these connections can muddy the waters in other sectors; why would insurance be any different? Remember that story about the DCI arresting suspects after an armed robbery? Imagine trying to get justice when the system itself is compromised.

So, before you sign on the dotted line, do your homework. Don't let the fancy brochures and smiling faces on the advertisements fool you. Look beyond the marketing hype. Find out who really owns the company. Check their claims payout rates, if you can even find them. Because in Nairobi, and in Kenya generally, trust in institutions is already a scarce commodity. When politicians get their hands in the cookie jar, especially in essential services like insurance, that trust doesn't just get a little dented; it gets smashed to pieces. Next week, they’ll be talking about pensions and life insurance. Prepare yourselves.

This exposé is a stark reminder that the financial services sector, much like the political landscape, is rife with opportunities for exploitation. It’s a jungle out there, and only the most informed, and perhaps the most cynical, will survive.
