Ruto: Vision 2030 Served Kenya Well, But We're Nowhere Near The Target - State House Says

Ruto admits Vision 2030 is hitting its sunset, with Kenya still miles away from its ambitious goals, especially becoming an upper-middle-income economy. Now, they're promising a "people-driven" new charter.

President William Ruto, addressing the nation from State House, has finally confessed what most of us already knew: Vision 2030, that grand plan we've all been vaguely aiming for, is pretty much toast. Apparently, it's "approaching its horizon," which is fancy talk for "didn't quite get there." He waxed lyrical about how it "transformed the way we think about national development," which is a nice way of saying it gave us a direction, even if we mostly just stood still.

The President, in his infinite wisdom, acknowledged that many of Vision 2030's "ambitions remain unattained." He even dropped the bombshell that Kenya is "nowhere near" the target of becoming an upper-middle-income country by 2030. Who would have thought? It’s almost as if promising shiny futures doesn't automatically make them appear, a lesson we've learned time and again, much like the recurring theme of infrastructure gaps when we discuss Africa's AI economy.

But fear not, because according to Ruto, this isn't a failure, it's an "opportunity." An opportunity to "reflect with honesty." Apparently, this reflection will lead to a "new national development charter" anchored on the 2010 Constitution. This isn't just another government plan, mind you. No, this is a "people-driven framework," designed to translate "constitutional aspirations into measurable development outcomes." We've heard that song and dance before, haven't we?

The grand unveiling of this new era will apparently commence with a "formal national conversation" on August 12, 2026. Citizens, professionals, faith leaders, businesses, civil society - everyone's invited to the table to "shape the country's next long-term development agenda." Let's just hope this conversation doesn't end up sounding like the same old promises we've been hearing for decades.

The only real achievement here seems to be the admission that the old plan didn't cut it. And the promise of a new one, which, let's be honest, will likely face the same fate as its predecessor. It's the Kenyan way, I suppose: keep setting horizons, keep falling short, and keep planning the next grand vision.
