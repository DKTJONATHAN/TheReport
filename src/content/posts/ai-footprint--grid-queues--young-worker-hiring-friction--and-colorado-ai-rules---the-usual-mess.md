So, the big AI push is hitting a wall, not with dazzling demos, but with good old-fashioned infrastructure problems. The International Energy Agency is basically saying that all the fancy renewable energy projects and massive data centers are stuck waiting for power grids that can handle them. We’re talking 5 to 15 years to build new grid stuff, while these data centers want to plug in within 1 to 3 years. It’s a classic case of not planning for the future, and now everyone’s paying for it. They need to pump up grid investment by 50% by 2030, and the prices for key components have already doubled. It’s not a good look.

And speaking of not looking good, the youth are getting the short end of the AI stick. While there’s no mass job apocalypse, younger workers in jobs where AI can replace tasks are seriously lagging behind. They’re not getting fired en masse, but they’re not being hired at the same rate as their older, more experienced colleagues. It’s entry-level friction, plain and simple. This is the kind of thing that makes you wonder if these fancy AI skills programs are just a rebranded way to deal with youth unemployment, or if they’ll actually make a difference. It’s not like we haven’t seen high-income earners struggle before, remember when [Mt Kenya Workers Urged to Prioritise Savings as Only 18% Achieve Financial Health Despite High Financial Inclusion](/posts/mt-kenya-workers-urged-to-prioritise-savings-as-only-18--achieve-financial-health-despite-high-financial-inclusion)? This is just a new flavor of the same old story.

Meanwhile, Colorado is trying to write the rulebook for AI, which is always a good sign that things are already out of control. They’re dealing with automated decision-making and chatbots, trying to put safeguards in place for things like consumer rights, teens, and even suicide prevention. While Washington is still fiddling, states are stepping up to create the operating manual for this stuff. The real question is whether this will create a baseline or just a confusing patchwork of rules that companies will try to litigate their way around.

Here in Kenya, a primary-care trial with an AI system found that while doctors were better at taking notes, the actual patient outcomes didn't improve. So, the AI can help with the paperwork, but it’s not magically making people healthier within 14 days. This is a crucial point for low-income countries - we need to focus on real patient results, not just documentation scores, before we start rolling out expensive AI tools across the board. It’s the same old song and dance: flashy tech promises, but the actual results are often underwhelming.

And the bookworms are getting nervous. Booksellers are seeing weird, random bulk orders, and they suspect AI companies are buying up physical books to scan them for training data. We’re talking obscure titles mixed with magazines, all bought at full price. It’s a whole new way to reshape cultural markets, and it’s happening far away from any polished AI demos. The big question is what happens to all these books after they're scanned. Are they just going to be pulped? It’s another reminder that this AI boom isn't just about clever code; it's about real-world consequences, even for the publishing industry.
