So, the big tech boys are at it again. Apparently, they've been sneaking around, buying up millions of books - and then, get this, *destroying* them. All in the name of "training" their artificial intelligence. Because what the world really needs is more soulless algorithms, right? Never mind the authors, never mind the stories, never mind the actual paper and ink that someone poured their heart into. It’s just another Tuesday in the digital gold rush, where everything and everyone is a commodity.

This "destructive scanning" is conveniently raising "new questions" about copyright. Shocking, I know. It’s almost as if mass destruction of intellectual property might have some… consequences. And the future of printed books? Please. These giants are looking at our libraries, our little bookstores, our dusty archives, and seeing not culture, but raw data. Raw data that they can gobble up and spit out as something new, something that probably sounds like a broken record player reciting Wikipedia.

And here’s the kicker for us here in Kenya: the article hints that countries like ours could become "hunting grounds for rare literary works." Like we’re some exotic safari destination for tech billionaires looking for a quaint, pre-digital artifact to shred. Imagine, a first edition of Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, not proudly displayed, but being fed into a machine to churn out more generic prose. It’s a slap in the face to our heritage, our creativity, and everything we’ve struggled to build. It's a reminder of how easily our achievements can be overlooked, much like how some seem to have "ghosted the chat" when serious matters arise, as seen in the mess involving diplomats.

They’ll tell you it’s progress. They’ll tell you it’s innovation. But what they’re really doing is cannibalizing the past to feed a future that serves only them. We're already wrestling with our own political dramas and leadership blunders, like when the president was busy with credentials while the country was literally burning. This new threat, however, operates on a global scale, indifferent to our local struggles, ready to plunder our cultural treasures without a second thought. The Eastleigh Voice might be online now, but what happens when the very source material of human expression becomes just another disposable resource for the machines?
