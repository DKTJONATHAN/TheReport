Nairobi Gets More Data Space As iColo Becomes Digital Realty - For Now

This whole song and dance about Digital Realty opening another data centre in Karen, NBO2, and swallowing up iColo? It's the same old story dressed in slightly shinier clothes. They're talking about "resilience" and "high-density capacity" like it's some magic cure for Kenya's tech woes. More megawatts, more cables, more promises. But at the end of the day, it's just another big foreign company setting up shop, buying up local assets, and expecting us to cheer.

The real kicker is the government’s desperate attempts to spin this as a win because of “reforms.” They’re parading the removal of local shareholding requirements like they’ve handed out gold bars. Foreign investors can now own everything outright - no questions asked. This isn't about developing Kenya; it's about making it easier for money to flow out. It's a classic example of how bureaucracy lost the race for the future, leaving genuine local innovation struggling to breathe.

And the networking? Over 100 networks, two internet exchange points, a satellite teleport. Sounds impressive, right? It’s just fancy jargon for saying they’ve built a slightly more complicated way for companies to trade data. For banks and ISPs, it means they can connect to more things without leaving their server room. For the rest of us? It’s just more infrastructure for the digital elite, while the real issues like access and affordability remain untouched.

The government’s touting the "growing demand for AI infrastructure" and Kenya's "skilled workforce" as reasons for this investment. Yeah, right. Let’s see how many of those "skilled Kenyans" actually get a slice of the pie, and how many of these "global networks" are actually built by local hands. It’s all about attracting foreign capital, which is fine, but the talk of benefiting the country feels like an afterthought.

They even had a whole government showcase, with policy reform as a key talking point. This is just the government trying to look good, attributing the investment to reforms that essentially hand over control. It's a calculated move to make Kenya seem more attractive to foreign money, ignoring the long-term implications for local ownership and control.

What happens next is anyone's guess. Will this extra capacity actually translate into tangible benefits for Kenyans, or will it just be more infrastructure for foreign cloud providers and regulated institutions? We’ll see if they actually follow through on their promises of priority locations and genuine development, or if it’s just more hot air and another opportunity for the connected few to benefit.
