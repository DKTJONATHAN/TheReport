Nairobi, Kiambu Top Kenya's Wealth Rankings: Arid Counties Still Scrambling for Crumbs

This report confirms what anyone with eyes in their head already knows: Nairobi is drowning in money while the rest of the country is lucky to have water. The 2026 County Development Index (CDI) report basically says Nairobi is the economic king of Kenya, pulling in a ridiculous 27.5% of the nation's GDP. Meanwhile, places like Wajir and Mandera are at the bottom, probably wondering where their next meal is coming from. Surprise, surprise.

The report blathers on about "diversified economic activities," "infrastructure," and "private investment." Nairobi, naturally, has all of it. Kiambu and Nakuru are riding Nairobi's coattails, thanks to proximity and some manufacturing - which is more than you can say for the arid north. Mombasa, the port, is doing its usual thing. It’s all just a rehashing of the same old story: some places get the cream, others get the skim milk.

These "leading counties" have it all: manufacturing, agriculture, trade, education, tourism, the works. The index even looks at things like youth unemployment and poverty. So, basically, it's measuring how much money some people have versus how little others have. The bottom counties, like Wajir and Turkana, are stuck with livestock and zero investment. It’s like they’re living in a different country, not just a different county. This whole situation is a constant reminder of the same script we've seen before.

The report’s “recommendations” are equally predictable: "economic diversification strategies," "strengthening agricultural value chains," and "attracting private capital." It's the same advice they’ve been giving for years. While they talk about attracting investment, we’re stuck dealing with government projects that seem to be more about lining pockets than actually building anything useful, like that Nairobi-Mombasa Highway Expansion where they ended up forcing a toll road plan.

Economist Samuel Kimote chips in about "infrastructure, human capital, and institutional capacity." Of course, the strong counties have them, and the weak ones don't. It’s a vicious cycle. Weak infrastructure means you can't expand, can't generate revenue, and can't even afford basic services. It's a given. And let's not even start on fiscal management; Narok and Mombasa are apparently good at collecting cash, while Nyamira is struggling with a big wage bill. Just another day in paradise.

The report highlights the stark reality of wealth disparities, proving that while Nairobi might be the "Capital Centre of Kenya," it's also the centre of its economic inequality. The arid counties are left to fend for themselves, waiting for solutions that never seem to arrive. It’s a shame, really, that this is the state of affairs.
