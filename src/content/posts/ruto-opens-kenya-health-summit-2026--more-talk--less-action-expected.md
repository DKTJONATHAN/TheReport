So, President Ruto decided to grace the Kenyatta International Convention Centre (KICC) with his presence, opening some inaugural Kenya Health Summit. Two days of deliberating, assessing, and probably a whole lot of breathing in recycled air. All this under the grand theme “Reforms Delivered, Health as a Right.” Right. Because we haven't heard that one before.

You’ve got the usual suspects: Kindiki, Duale, Mudavadi, Wetang’ula, Muthoni, Oluga - the whole government ensemble, ready to dissect what’s working and what’s not in our healthcare. Duale, bless his heart, even inspected the venue beforehand. Probably checking if the microphones were strategically placed to amplify his pronouncements about the Bottom-Up Economic Transformation Agenda (BETA). Because that’s what Kenya’s health sector needs right now: more acronyms and less actual care.

They’re bragging about KEMSA’s fill rate exceeding 90 percent - a miracle, considering the usual chaos. And 31 million Kenyans "onboarded" onto the Social Health Authority (SHA). Sounds like they're just ticking boxes, hoping nobody notices the reality on the ground. We’ll see if these digital health transformations and expanded services actually reach the mwananchi, or if it’s just another case of HELB scammers giving more effort than our actual health system.

Primary healthcare and Community Health Promoters (CHPs) will be central, they say. Bringing essential services closer to households. Oh, and they’re deploying an additional 5,000 nurses and midwives. Let’s hope they don’t get stuck in the same bureaucratic quagmire as everything else. The Presidential Town Hall is meant to give Kenyans a chance to share their experiences. Translation: a staged performance where carefully selected individuals praise the regime, while the real issues are swept under the very expensive KICC carpets.

Duale’s quote about accounting for reforms and shaping the journey towards Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is pure theatre. "Health is a right, not a privilege," he declared. We’ve been hearing variations of that line for years, while the queues at public hospitals get longer and the cost of basic medication continues its upward climb. It’s a shame our leaders don’t apply the same energy to actual delivery as they do to these grand summits.

This whole affair feels like another round of political theatre, designed to project an image of progress. We'll hear a lot of buzzwords, see a lot of handshakes, and maybe even get a few souvenir pamphlets. But for the average Kenyan struggling to get decent medical attention, this summit is just another Tuesday filled with promises they’ve heard a million times before.
