KENYA'S PAYMENTS REVOLUTION: STILL STUCK ON MOBILE MONEY

WARNING: Kenya's obsession with mobile money has made it complacent. This article argues it's time to look beyond M-Pesa and build new payment infrastructure, or risk being left behind.

Mobile money success is nice, but it's not the finish line.

They've been riding the M-Pesa wave for two decades, haven't they? Kenya practically invented the whole mobile money thing, making us the poster child for financial inclusion. Billions flowing, millions of accounts, agents on every corner. It’s a beautiful picture, a story they love to tell themselves. But this past success is starting to feel like a well-worn pair of shoes - comfortable, familiar, and utterly incapable of running a marathon. We’re so busy congratulating ourselves on M-Pesa that we’ve stopped innovating. Every new app, every new bank service, it’s all just a shiny coat of paint on the same old M-Pesa rails. It’s like building a mansion on a foundation of sand.

Now Kenswitch is throwing a domestic card scheme into the mix, and suddenly everyone’s asking if we should actually *own* the plumbing that keeps our economy flowing. We’ve got millions of cards in circulation, billions in POS transactions, but a lot of that infrastructure? Still reliant on those big international players. It’s fine when you’re travelling to Copenhagen, but what about buying maize flour in Kinoo? Do we really need Visa and Mastercard for that? It feels like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut, or worse, paying exorbitant fees for a service we could manage ourselves.

Look at India. They didn’t just stick with their version of mobile money; they built an entire payments universe with UPI, linking banks, fintechs, QR codes, and even their own RuPay cards. Saudi Arabia did the same with Mada. These countries are building infrastructure that fits their own needs, not just clinging to what’s already there. We did it brilliantly with mobile money, solving a distinctly Kenyan problem. But now? We seem to be stuck in a rut, afraid to apply that same logic anywhere else. It’s like we’ve built a spectacular treehouse and now refuse to build anything else in the garden.

A domestic card scheme could be another set of rails for our banks and fintechs to play on. Think local pricing, virtual cards, tokenisation - things that could actually make life easier and cheaper for Kenyans. It’s not about cutting ourselves off from the world; it’s about having options, about ensuring our own domestic commerce isn't entirely dependent on foreign networks. Payment infrastructure is becoming as critical as electricity or water. Governments in other countries understand this and are investing deliberately. Why are we so reluctant to do the same, especially when we’ve already proven we can do it so well?

The real challenge for Kenswitch, and for Kenya, isn't just launching a card. It's getting anyone to actually use it. Merchants accept cards because customers have them, customers carry them because banks issue them. Breaking that cycle requires more than just a shiny new piece of plastic. It demands scale, incentives, and infrastructure that's more reliable than a Kenyan politician’s promise. We need to be asking what comes *after* mobile money: instant payments that work everywhere, cheap merchant fees, interoperable QR codes, virtual cards that appear in seconds. It’s about building a future, not just polishing the past. We can’t let our past success make us conservative. This Kenswitch thing might just be the nudge we need to start that real conversation about what our payment system should look like, instead of just accepting that mobile money is the end of the road. It’s like reading about women in Mathare struggling after birth and thinking the problem is solved because we have clinics now; the suffering just shifts.
