While Washington plays at diplomacy in the halls of the United Nations, the price of Brent crude is doing the real talking at $106.60 a barrel. The seven-month war with Iran has turned the Strait of Hormuz into a geopolitical choke-point that the U.S. can neither control nor negotiate its way out of. Every failed meeting at the UN translates directly to a spike at the pump, with diesel prices up 73 percent since the first missiles flew.

The international fallout is already visible in the sovereign debt markets of Japan and Germany. When the U.S. 10-year yield sneezes, the rest of the world catches pneumonia. Japan's 10-year bond is at its highest since 1996, proving that no amount of central bank intervention can decouple a nation from the gravity of the dollar. The global bond boat isn't just a metaphor; it is a sinking vessel where the passengers are chained to the floor.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's attempts to "be the house" have been a masterclass in hubris. His interventions to prop up the yen and buy back long-dated Treasurys have failed to intimidate a market that smells blood. Investors are not looking at Bessent's bravado; they are looking at the 4 percent unemployment rate and the inevitable tightening promised by Fed officials like John Williams and Anna Paulson.

The narrative of resilience pushed by central bankers is a thin veil for the coming stagflation. As business activity accelerates, so do input costs, driven by a fuel and transport spike that makes growth a liability rather than an asset. The market is pricing in a reality where the U.S. must choose between a total currency collapse or a prolonged recession, and it is increasingly clear they will choose the latter while pretending it was the plan all along.

In the end, the Bloomberg Terminal bros that Bessent mocks are simply reacting to the math. When the cost of borrowing for a home hits 7.37 percent and the cost of moving goods hits record highs, the AI boom and solid macro data become irrelevant footnotes in a story about the end of American financial hegemony.
