When diesel prices nearly double, the world stops appearing as a collection of sovereign states and reveals itself as a single, fragile supply chain. This is not just an issue for a driver at a Mobil station in California. It affects the grain harvester in the Midwest and the shipping fleets in the Atlantic. Every mile of transport becomes a gamble that the consumer loses. The increase in fuel costs acts as a regressive tax, stripping wealth from those with the least amount of financial cushion while the architects of conflict remain insulated.

The illusion of democratic control is perhaps the darkest aspect of this cycle. By the time a citizen reaches a voting booth to express their displeasure with the economic fallout of a war, the damage is already permanent. The capital has been incinerated, the prices have stabilized at a new, higher floor, and personal savings have been depleted. This is a recurring theme in global instability, much like the situation where 15 Kenyans pulled from Iran-s mess via Turkey, because what else is new?, serves as a reminder that the chaos is widespread and the exit strategies are always reactive rather than proactive.

Accountability in this system is a lag indicator at best. Decisions are made in real-time by a small elite in war rooms, while the consequences are distributed across the global population with surgical precision. The home front is no longer a sanctuary; it is simply another resource to be mined to sustain a conflict. As long as the global economy relies on chokepoints that can be closed with a handful of missiles, the concept of a ballot that can stop the bleeding will remain a comforting fiction for the modern consumer.
