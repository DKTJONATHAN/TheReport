Lagat to Recruits: Keep Up or Be Left Behind by Technology - And Other Empty Promises

Here we go again. Another parade, another pep talk from the big wigs at Kiganjo. Deputy Inspector General Eliud Lagat, probably fresh off a helicopter ride from Nairobi, is telling the fresh-faced police recruits they need to get with the program. "Embrace technology," he says. "Artificial Intelligence, AI, all that jazz." Because apparently, wielding a baton and knowing how to fill out a report isn't enough anymore. You need to be a tech wizard to catch the latest boda-boda thief. It’s a brave new world, they tell us, and if you’re not plugged in, you’re just another fossil.

Lagat, bless his heart, seems to think these young officers, fresh out of the academy, are digital natives who will naturally adapt. He's talking about their "advantage" because they grew up with phones glued to their faces. This is the same generation that probably struggles to find a clean toilet. He’s hoping AI will make policing more "efficient." More efficient at what? More efficient at harassing us for minor offenses? More efficient at filling out paperwork while actual crime runs rampant? It’s the usual song and dance, promising a futuristic police force that will probably still be using the same old methods to get the job done, just with a few more screens involved.

Of course, the obligatory warning about social media. "Don't post sensitive information," he warns. As if these recruits haven't seen their seniors do exactly that, or worse, used it for personal vendettas. They're told to be professional, accountable, and avoid debt. This is the same advice they’ve heard since primary school, delivered with the same sincerity as a politician promising to lower taxes. And then there’s the talk about mental health, seeking professional help instead of drowning their sorrows in cheap liquor. They’re being trained for a job that will chew them up and spit them out, and then told to attend counselling sessions. It’s a twisted kind of logic, really. The system breaks you, then offers you a tissue. It’s a bit like trying to understand the convoluted journey and impact of cheating in relationships; sometimes the advice given doesn't truly address the root cause.

Lagat wrapped it all up by saying the nine months of training have prepared them. Prepared them for what? To be highly trained individuals who will eventually learn that promises of technological advancement are often just shiny distractions from the real, gritty problems of policing in Kenya? They are expected to be "transformational officers." I’ll believe that when I see it. For now, they’re just the next batch of hopefuls, armed with new slogans and a vague understanding of AI, ready to face the reality of Kenyan policing.
