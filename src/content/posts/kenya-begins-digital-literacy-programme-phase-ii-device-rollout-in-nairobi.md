Kenya Starts Tech Push: Laptops and Smartboards for Schools, Because We All Need More Screen Time

The government is at it again, rolling out Phase II of their "Digital Literacy Programme" in Nairobi. Apparently, the plan is to shove laptops and fancy smartboards into 10,382 public Junior Schools across the entire country. Because what our kids really need is to stare at more screens, right? It’s all part of this "Kenya Digital Economy Acceleration Project," which sounds suspiciously like another way to spend taxpayer money with a shiny new name.

At Lenana Primary School, the bigwigs were out in full force - the Principal Secretary for ICT, some MP who chairs a committee (because we have *so* many of those), and the CEO of the ICT Authority. They were all there to launch the "expansion of technology in teaching and learning." You know, because chalk and textbooks were so last century and clearly not responsible for whatever skills gap they're trying to fix now. Nairobi County is already boasting a 92.9 per cent completion rate for their 98 schools. Impressive, if you ignore the fact that it means a few schools are still left in the dark, probably praying for a tablet.

The real kicker? They’re also doing "teacher training" to make sure these educators can actually, you know, *use* the tech. One can only imagine the chaos. While they're busy teaching teachers how to operate a smartboard, the rest of us are dealing with the fallout of economic blunders, like how [Middle East Bloodshed Becomes the New Global Tax](/posts/middle-east-bloodshed-becomes-the-new-global-tax). But sure, let's focus on giving kids more gadgets.

The whole point, they say, is to "enhance digital learning" and prepare students for a "technology-driven economy." This sounds a lot like the promises we heard about AI and the so-called [AI Inflation Trap: Central Banking in a World of Artificial Delusions](/posts/the-ai-inflation-trap--central-banking-in-a-world-of-artificial-delusions). It's a nice theory, but let's see how many of these laptops actually end up in working condition after a few months and how many teachers are left bewildered by the sheer volume of educational YouTube videos they're expected to curate.

So, here's to Kenya's digital future, one shiny new device at a time. Let's just hope these schools have reliable electricity and internet, otherwise, it's just a very expensive way to create really fancy paperweights. And don't forget the training, because apparently, plugging in a laptop is rocket science for some.
