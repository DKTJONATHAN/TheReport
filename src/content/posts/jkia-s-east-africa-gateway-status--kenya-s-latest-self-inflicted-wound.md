So, the planes sat on the tarmac, the luggage stayed grounded, and Kenya's reputation took another beating. This wasn't just some petty squabble between workers and management. This was a glaring, neon-sign-flashing exposé of our pathetic inability to manage anything beyond arguing about who gets the biggest slice of a shrinking cake. JKIA, our supposed golden goose, coughed and sputtered, reminding everyone that geography is nice, but competence is king. And right now, Kenya's competence is looking about as solid as a wet tissue.

Our friends in Ethiopia are practically building a Dubai in Addis Ababa, pouring billions into airports and airlines like it's going out of fashion. Rwanda, bless their ambitious hearts, are doing the same. They’re not waiting for things to happen; they're *making* them happen. Meanwhile, we’re here, bickering over whether to fix the plumbing or repaint the walls, all while our competitors are erecting skyscrapers. It’s like showing up to a Formula 1 race with a donkey cart, expecting to win. This constant infighting and inability to get our act together makes you wonder if we're not just sleepwalking into irrelevance, much like the promise of AI saving us all proved to be a bit of a [Silicon Mirage](/posts/the-silicon-mirage--why-ai-has-yet-to-move-the-macroeconomic-needle).

Sure, the workers have their rights, blah blah blah. But when your demands cripple an entire sector, costing businesses millions, stranding families, and potentially costing lives, you have to ask: are you part of the solution or just another brick in the wall of stagnation? The blame game is old news. Management and government need to accept their part in this mess. Providing dusty equipment and expecting miracles is just asking for trouble, then blaming the workers when the whole system implodes.

The real question isn't just about better pay, it's about whether JKIA can even *survive* in the coming years. We’re so busy debating how to distribute the little we have that we’ve forgotten how to actually *grow* the economy. Competitiveness isn't a birthright; it's earned through consistent, reliable performance. When passengers and airlines start looking elsewhere because they can’t count on us, that’s not just a lost flight; it’s a lost future. We’re putting all our eggs in the JKIA basket, and if that basket breaks, well, the whole country feels the fallout.

This whole fiasco is just another symptom of a deeper malaise. We’re masters of arguing about who gets what, but utterly inept at figuring out how to create more to go around. Investing in infrastructure is a start, but without competent management, efficient systems, and a workforce that’s not just paid but also equipped to succeed, it’s all just a very expensive paperweight. We need a national commitment to building the cake, not just fighting over its crumbs. Otherwise, we’ll be left with nothing but the echoes of what could have been.

Ultimately, this isn't about workers versus the government. It's about whether Kenya has the collective will to stop tripping over its own feet and start acting like a serious player on the regional stage. The promise of connectivity is fragile, and every major disruption chips away at it. If we can't guarantee reliability, the world will simply find a more dependable alternative. And by the time we realize what we've lost, it'll be too late to win it back.
