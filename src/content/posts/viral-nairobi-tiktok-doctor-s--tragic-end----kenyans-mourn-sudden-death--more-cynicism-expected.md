Right, so TUKO is pushing this story about some doctor, Dennis Nganga, who used to make TikTok videos. Apparently, he's kicked the bucket, and now everyone's in a tizzy, flooding his comment section with crocodile tears. He went by @dr_princee, which tells you everything you need to know about the internet these days. His "accessible" health education was apparently just him boogying in a lab coat. How profound.

This particular gem, recorded August 6, 2026, shows him doing his thing, "dancing energetically" - or as I'd call it, looking for likes. The video is now getting "millions of eyes" because, naturally, nothing moves Kenyans like a dead influencer. The on-screen caption, "Rest well, Dr.", now carries a "heartbreaking new meaning". Oh, the drama. It's almost as if people on the internet only realize someone's existence when they're gone.

The comment section, as expected, has turned into a pity party, with "urgent conversations about mental health and hidden struggles" cropping up. Because that's what happens after someone dies - everyone suddenly becomes an expert on their inner turmoil. It's a convenient narrative, isn't it? Makes you wonder if anyone actually cared when he was alive, or if this is just another wave of bandwagon grief. We've seen it all before, especially when politicians are involved, like when [Ruto Pitches Africa 'Positive Light' As G7 Leaders 'Back' Debt Reforms - Again](/posts/ruto-pitches-africa--positive-light--as-g7-leaders--back--debt-reforms---again).

This whole spectacle is just another reminder of how fleeting fame is, especially online. A few catchy dances, a bit of medical jargon thrown in, and suddenly you're a national treasure. Then, *poof*. Gone. And the same people who cheered him on are now dissecting his life like he was some kind of national monument. It’s a sad commentary on our society, this constant need for spectacle, followed by the equally performative outrage and sorrow.
