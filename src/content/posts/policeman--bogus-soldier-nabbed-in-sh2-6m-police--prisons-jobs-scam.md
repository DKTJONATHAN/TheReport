So, the news is out: some bright sparks, including a serving Administration Police officer, are apparently busy lining their pockets by selling fake jobs in the police, prisons, and military. We're talking millions of shillings, disappearing faster than a politician's promises after an election. This Samson Momanyi, supposedly an AP officer, thought he’d play Santa Claus for a hefty Sh1.5 million, promising entry into the Prisons Service. Meanwhile, another joker, Brian Shem Maboni Achieng, impersonating military brass, conned two poor souls out of Sh1.1 million for KDF positions. It’s like a twisted version of "The Imitation Game," but instead of cracking codes, they're cracking open your wallet.

These aren't isolated incidents, mind you. This is just the latest chapter in Nairobi’s favourite soap opera: "The Hustle." Every year, thousands of young Kenyans flock to recruitment drives for these disciplined services, dreaming of stability, a steady salary, and perhaps even a uniform that screams "authority." Naturally, the fraudsters see this desperation like sharks smell blood. They pose as officers, agents, or even "connections" to the powers-that-be, promising guaranteed slots, manipulated medicals, or even fake appointment letters. It's a well-worn playbook, and unfortunately, people still fall for it. It’s almost as predictable as the endless traffic on Waiyaki Way.

The Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI) has been shouting from the rooftops about these scams for years. Remember Derrick Fanuel Oduor back in July 2026? He allegedly fleeced jobseekers of over Sh8.6 million, promising them positions in the KDF, NPS, and Public Service Commission. And let’s not forget the police recruit constable arrested in February 2026 for swindling Sh687,000 from someone desperate for a KDF job. Even military officers like Major Ben Kipkogei Chemjor have been court-martialed and jailed for similar schemes. It's a revolving door of deceit, with the same old stories being rehashed, just with new names and slightly larger sums of money. It makes you wonder about the effectiveness of these "warnings," doesn't it? It’s almost as if these cautionary tales are ignored, much like the advice given in articles about the financial woes of certain entities, like The Blue-Uniformed Billionaires: Police SACCO’s Sh66 Billion Mirage.

The security agencies themselves keep repeating that their recruitment processes are free and fair, and that anyone demanding money is a crook. The Kenya Prisons Service, the Ministry of Defence, and the National Police Service Commission have all issued stern warnings, explicitly stating that bribery and corruption will lead to disqualification and prosecution. They tell applicants to only attend recruitment at designated centres and to check official websites. Yet, here we are, with more people losing their hard-earned cash. It’s a cycle of exploitation that continues because, frankly, the lure of a government job, even with the inherent risks of falling victim to these scams, is still a powerful draw for many in this country.

These cases just prove that no matter how many warnings are issued, or how many arrests are made, the fraudsters will always find a way to prey on the hopeful. The prestige and perceived stability of joining the security forces are too tempting a target. It’s a harsh reality check for those dreaming of a steady career; sometimes, the biggest threat isn't the physical danger of the job, but the con artists operating in the shadows, ready to turn your aspirations into their profit.

Ultimately, it’s a sad reflection of the desperation that permeates the job market here. While the authorities chase down the con artists, the victims are left poorer, disillusioned, and often embarrassed. The only real security is to be street-smart and to remember that if a job offer sounds too good to be true, it almost certainly is.
