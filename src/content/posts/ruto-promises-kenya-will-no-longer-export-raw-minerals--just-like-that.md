Ruto's Big Talk: Kenya Will Stop Exporting Raw Minerals, Apparently.

President Ruto is back, this time from Samburu, with another grand pronouncement about stopping the export of raw materials. Apparently, we've been "losing capital" by letting other countries process our gold, limestone, and soda ash. Now, he says, we'll process everything locally, create jobs, and boost our national wealth. He's even eyeing partnerships, like with Aliko Dangote for a refinery in Lamu. He also defended kicking Tata Chemicals out of Magadi, claiming they weren't making us enough profit.

This whole song and dance about processing minerals locally sounds familiar, doesn't it? It's the same old story we hear every election cycle, a sweet tune designed to get the crowds cheering. They talk about jobs and wealth, but where does it all end up? Usually, it's just another way to sell off chunks of the country, maybe not directly, but through sweetheart deals and grand projects that benefit a select few. Remember how they promised so much during Labour Day while the city drowned and our pockets stayed empty?

The President’s defense of ousting Tata Chemicals from Lake Magadi is particularly rich. He claims they’ve been exporting raw materials and enriching other nations. Yet, Tata Chemicals has been extracting and refining trona for 115 years, turning it into soda ash essential for countless products. They’re one of Kenya’s top export earners, shipping over 350,000 tons annually. Suspending their operations over "compliance and licensing issues" feels less like protecting national interest and more like clearing the way for someone else's turn at the pie. It’s a classic move, similar to how other valuable national assets have been handled in the past, like [Selling the Kitchen to Buy a Meal: The KPC Fire Sale](/posts/selling-the-kitchen-to-buy-a-meal--the-kpc-fire-sale).

Giving "five, six or even 10 companies an opportunity" to exploit these resources sounds like a recipe for more of the same. We’ll likely end up with more concessions, more promises of jobs that never materialize for the average Kenyan, and more capital flowing out of the country, just in a slightly different form. The government’s intention might be to create wealth and reduce poverty, but history shows us that when it comes to raw materials and powerful interests, the outcome is rarely as rosy as the pronouncements suggest.













President Ruto, speaking in Samburu, has declared that Kenya will cease exporting raw minerals. He lamented the lost capital and value incurred from such practices, stating the government's decision to process all minerals-gold, limestone, iron ore, graphite, titanium, and soda ash-locally. This move, he claims, will generate employment opportunities and increase the nation's capital value. He highlighted potential partnerships, including with Aliko Dangote for a mega-billion oil refinery and petrochemical complex in Lamu, to refine gold and other minerals, arguing it's imprudent to export raw materials and create jobs elsewhere while young Kenyans are unemployed.

The President also defended the decision to remove Tata Chemicals Limited from operations in Magadi, Kajiado County. He alleged that the company has been exporting raw materials, benefiting other nations, and then importing processed products back into Kenya. According to Ruto, the government intends to offer opportunities to multiple companies to utilize these resources for job creation, wealth generation, and poverty reduction.

This narrative of local processing and job creation is a well-worn script, often deployed with great fanfare. The promise of newfound wealth and employment for the masses is a potent political tool, but the reality on the ground rarely matches the grand pronouncements. We've seen this play out before, where grand initiatives meant to uplift the nation often end up benefiting a select few, leaving the majority with little more than empty promises. It's a cycle that feels all too familiar, much like other instances where valuable national resources have been managed with questionable outcomes, as seen in discussions around [Selling the Kitchen to Buy a Meal: The KPC Fire Sale](/posts/selling-the-kitchen-to-buy-a-meal--the-kpc-fire-fire-sale).

Tata Chemicals, a company with a 115-year history in Kenya since 1911, has been instrumental in extracting and refining trona from Lake Magadi into soda ash, a vital component for industries like glass and detergents. Their operations consistently rank among Kenya's top export earners, with annual shipments exceeding 350,000 tons to international markets. The suspension of their mining operations starting July 28, 2026, citing "compliance and licensing issues," raises questions about whether this is a genuine effort to improve national benefit or a strategic move to facilitate other arrangements. The promise of empowering numerous new companies feels less like a bold economic strategy and more like a prelude to further resource giveaways.
