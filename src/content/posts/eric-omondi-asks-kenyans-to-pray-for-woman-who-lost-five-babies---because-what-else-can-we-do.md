So, Eric Omondi, the self-proclaimed king of comedy, has decided it's time for us to whip out our rosaries and open our wallets. Apparently, some poor woman named Mercy Kanini has lost five babies. Five. And of course, the comedian's first instinct isn't to, say, connect her with actual resources or mental health professionals, but to orchestrate a prayer rally at All Saints’ Cathedral. Because, as we all know, collective wailing always fixes everything.

He’s urging Kenyans to stand with her, offering prayers and support as she buries her children on Friday in Kuri. Thursday is for the prayer gathering, a grand spectacle of communal grief designed to boost Omondi’s clout, no doubt. It’s a familiar script: tragedy strikes, a celebrity steps in, calls for public sympathy, and everyone feels good about themselves for a hot minute before moving on to the next viral drama. It’s just another episode in the never-ending saga of [Clout, Chaos, and Corpses: Why Kenya’s Social Media Feed Is a Crime Scene](/posts/clout--chaos--and-corpses--why-kenya-s-social-media-feed-is-a-crime-scene).

Let's be real, Eric. Prayers are fine, a nice sentiment, but do they pay the bills for therapy? Do they magically restock a womb? This isn't a football match where cheering brings victory. This is a profound, irreversible loss. And while the intention might be good, the execution feels… performative. It's a sad reminder that in this country, we often default to spiritual platitudes when faced with life's brutal realities.

The whole affair is a classic Nairobi performance. We get worked up, we send our "thoughts and prayers," and then life goes on. Mercy Kanini will have her burial, the cameras will eventually stop rolling, and Omondi will find his next cause célèbre. The real work, the quiet, unglamorous work of healing and rebuilding, will be left to her and a few brave souls. But hey, at least we can all say we "stood with her" on social media.
