KENYA'S DONKEYS ON THE BRINK OF EXTINCTION, THANKS TO GLOBAL SKIN GREED

The humble donkey, the real engine of rural Kenya, is facing extinction thanks to a global skin trade that's bleeding the country dry. Animal welfare groups are screaming bloody murder, but for most of us, it's just another problem for someone else to fix.

Donkey carts now double as produce dryers for farmers like Beth Wambui, whose livelihood was stolen along with her donkey. It's a stark reminder that for many, this isn't just about animal welfare; it's about survival.

The numbers don't lie. Kenya's donkey population has plummeted from 1.8 million in 2009 to barely over a million a decade later. This isn't natural selection; it's a slaughter. Driven by the insatiable demand for ejiao, a fancy Chinese potion, traders are stripping our countryside bare. Four slaughterhouses were processing 1,600 donkeys a day at their peak. You do the math. The government slapped a ban in 2020, calling it "criminality and brutality." Big words, but the black market just laughed. They're still operating, exploiting legal loopholes and weak enforcement like it's just another Tuesday. Fines are pocket change in a billion-dollar industry. It's a business expense, not a deterrent.

Even with the ban, the trade is still deeply entrenched. Just this year, authorities seized over 3,700 hides. That's 3,700 households left with nothing. The government claims meat consumption is still legal in some parts, a convenient excuse for traffickers to move donkeys across borders, under the guise of local demand, while the skins disappear into export channels. It’s a dangerous loophole, and as long as it’s there, the traffickers will keep exploiting it, just like our leaders keep exploiting us. We’ve seen this kind of thing before, like when we discussed the AGOA Band-Aid; we’re bleeding, and they’re buying cotton wool.

The African Union is trying to get a continent-wide ban, but porous borders and corruption are the real gatekeepers. For pastoralists and smallholder farmers, losing a donkey isn't just about a missing animal; it's about losing income, food security, and mobility. Women and children bear the brunt, walking for hours under the punishing sun. Conservationists warn that donkeys reproduce too slowly to keep up with this extraction rate. China, with all its advanced technology, can't even replicate it. This isn't just about saving donkeys; it's about preventing a socioeconomic disaster.

They're calling donkey skins "the new ivory," but unlike elephants and rhinos, donkeys are invisible in policy discussions. They don't get the global headlines. But make no mistake, entire economies are built on these animals. The vulnerability of these working animals is their greatest threat. If policymakers don't start seeing them as critical infrastructure for our most vulnerable communities, we're doomed to lose not just a species, but the very livelihoods of millions. It's a slap in the face to rural justice and economic resilience.
