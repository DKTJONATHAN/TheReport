KENYANS WARNED OF BUILDING COLLAPSE RISK AS RAINS APPROACH - THE-STAR.CO.KE

Oh, joy. Just when you thought Nairobi couldn't get any more chaotic, the National Disaster Operations Centre (NDOC) is suddenly remembering that rain exists and, shocker, it can damage buildings. Apparently, our glorious OND rains are about to hit, and we should all start looking out for our shoddy structures teetering on the brink of destruction. Because, you know, we *weren't* already aware that concrete poured by people who think rebar is optional tends to crumble.

They're calling it an El Niño-influenced season, which, translated from officialese, means "prepare for more than just a drizzle." We're talking flooding, flash floods, and the usual Nairobi symphony of chaos. The NDOC, in its infinite wisdom, has pointed out that buildings not built to "approved construction standards" are, surprise, surprise, vulnerable. It’s almost as if cutting corners on construction leads to… well, collapsing buildings. Who knew?

The NDOC’s advice? "Pay attention." Yes, you heard that right. Just casually observe your building for "large cracks, leaning walls, sinking floors" during a downpour. If you see these, apparently, you're supposed to evacuate and then, *then*, call the authorities. Because clearly, the people who built these death traps will be just sitting around waiting for your call, eager to fix their shoddy work. Let’s not forget the equally brilliant advice about not occupying already unsafe buildings or under-construction sites without permits. Groundbreaking stuff, truly.

This structural safety sermon is just another layer to the government’s pre-rain panic. They’ve even singled out counties like Homa Bay, Kisumu, and Nakuru, where lakes are apparently getting a bit… enthusiastic. Zachary Misiani, a "Disaster Risk Management and Anticipatory Action expert" (a fancy title for someone paid to state the obvious), is warning that El Niño and some other atmospheric mumbo jumbo could mean an early, torrential start to the season. It’s almost as if predicting heavy rain is a foreign concept here, despite it happening every single year. It’s enough to make you wonder if anyone’s ever heard of a sieve, let alone proper urban planning. Maybe they should consult with Cera Imani on how to handle a public relations crisis, because this "warning" feels more like a shrug.







Kenyans are being told to brace themselves, as the National Disaster Operations Centre (NDOC) has suddenly remembered that rain can actually do damage. With the October-November-December (OND) season on the horizon, they're warning us to keep an eye out for buildings deciding to take a nap, courtesy of heavy rainfall. Apparently, our foundations might get a bit weak. Groundbreaking stuff.

This whole hullabaloo is because of an El Niño-influenced short-rains season, which means we can expect more than just a gentle shower. Weather folks are predicting above-average rainfall, translating to the usual Nairobi cocktail of flooding, flash floods, and general mayhem. The NDOC, bless their hearts, have highlighted that buildings that didn't adhere to "approved construction standards" are prime candidates for partial or complete disintegration. It's almost like substandard construction leads to substandard outcomes. Who’d have thought?

So, the NDOC’s brilliant advice is for residents to "pay attention" to their buildings during heavy downpours. Look for "large cracks, leaning walls, sinking floors" - you know, the usual signs that your shanty is about to become a free-fall attraction. If you notice any of these delightful indicators, you're supposed to ditch the structure immediately and then, *maybe*, report it. Because clearly, the people who built these death traps are just waiting for your call to rectify their shoddy workmanship.

The agency is also reminding us not to occupy buildings already declared unsafe, or structures still under construction without the necessary paperwork. As if anyone with a brain cell would willingly inhabit a death trap or a building site that looks like it’s held together by prayer and optimism. This is just another layer to the government’s "anticipatory measures," which apparently include pointing out the obvious to a populace that’s seen it all before. It’s enough to make one appreciate the robust infrastructure of places like Spain, where even their award ceremonies have a bit more substance than this.

Experts like Zachary Misiani are throwing around terms like "positive Indian Ocean Dipole" to explain why the rain might be even worse than usual. They're talking about an early onset and "torrential heavy rainfall." It’s almost as if predicting heavy rain in Kenya during the rainy season is some kind of arcane science. We've seen this movie before, folks. Heavy rainfall, landslides, damaged homes - it's the same old song and dance.
