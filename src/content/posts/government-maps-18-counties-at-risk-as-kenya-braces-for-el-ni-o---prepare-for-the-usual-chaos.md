Government Maps 18 Counties at Risk as Kenya Braces for El Niño - Prepare for the Usual Chaos.

This is not a drill, folks. The weather gurus at the Kenya Meteorological Service Authority are shouting about an 81 percent chance of a "strong El Niño event." And just in case you thought it would be a quick shower and done, they're also saying there's a 97 percent chance it'll drag on into next year. So, expect more than just a bit of dampness; think "above-normal rainfall" turning into the usual Kenyan drama.

Apparently, the government has "identified" 18 counties that are supposedly going to be hit the hardest. They're talking flash floods, landslides, drought, disease outbreaks, and of course, the ever-reliable damage to infrastructure. The National Disaster Operations Centre, bless their hearts, has "mapped out" areas with risks. Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu are apparently "high-risk urban centres." That means our already pathetic drainage systems will be tested, services will be disrupted, and we’ll all be wading through sewage. It’s almost as if they’re surprised this happens every time it rains a bit too much.

The usual suspects are in the firing line: Coast counties like Tana River and Kilifi are bracing for floods and storm surges - good luck with that. The Lake Basin region, including Kisumu, is facing floods and displacement. And for the Rift Valley and North Eastern regions, it's a delightful cocktail of drought, water shortages, and livestock losses. So, if you live in Turkana or Mandera, start hoarding water and praying you don't become another statistic in the endless cycle of Kenyan disaster management. It’s a familiar script, not unlike the predictable political theatrics we've seen before, where warnings are issued, and then… well, you know what happens next.

The government's response? Emergency teams are being "placed on high alert" and "sensitized." This sounds suspiciously like the usual song and dance. They'll hold meetings, issue press releases, and then blame everyone else when things go wrong. We've seen this play out before with everything from election violence to… well, you get the picture. The only new thing here is the fancy jargon about risk assessments and response plans, which usually translates to more confusion and less actual help for the people on the ground.

So, while the Met Authority and the government are busy pointing fingers and mapping out disaster zones, the rest of us can only brace ourselves. Expect the usual Nairobi traffic jams to become impassable rivers, the power to go out more often than not, and the prices of basic commodities to skyrocket. It's El Niño, alright. And it's going to be another spectacular display of how little changes, no matter how many warnings are issued.
