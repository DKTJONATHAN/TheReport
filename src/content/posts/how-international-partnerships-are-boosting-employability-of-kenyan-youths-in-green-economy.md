GERMANY AND NORWAY GIVE KENYAN YOUTH GREEN JOBS - FOR NOW

The so-called "green economy" is being peddled as the next big thing, promising jobs in renewable energy, electric cars, and fancy new infrastructure. They say it's creating opportunities, but what they don't shout from the rooftops is that these jobs need actual skills, not just good intentions. And that's where the WE4D program, funded by the Germans and Norwegians and run by a whole committee of international and local bodies, is supposed to step in.

According to Thomas Jaeschke from GIZ, Germany's grand plan is to make sure this "green transformation" actually puts food on the table, especially for young people and women. They're pumping money into training for solar panels and electric motorbikes, all under the banner of "inclusive economic growth." It sounds nice, doesn't it? They're teaching skills that industry supposedly needs, plus a bit of entrepreneurship and how to not get electrocuted on the job. The goal is to churn out graduates ready for jobs or ready to create their own - a classic promise that rarely pans out for everyone.

Dr. Michael Odhiambo from the Technical University of Mombasa brags about how this partnership is changing the game, linking students directly to employers from day one. They're getting "industry-driven" training, meaning they're not just sitting in classrooms. Apparently, this employer-led approach is the program's "greatest strength," with everyone from GIZ to private companies cooking up curricula that reflect "actual workplace needs." Funny how quickly they forget that market needs shift faster than the Kenyan political winds.

The project aims to train 800 young people and create 480 jobs, while improving conditions for 270. At Rift Valley National Polytechnic, they've trained 241 solar technicians, men and women, thanks to better equipment and trainer capacity. They're even proud of challenging "traditional perceptions" by getting more women into these technical fields. As if women haven't been doing hard, skilled labor for centuries. They say these graduates leave with "experience, confidence and skills that employers can immediately apply." We'll see how long that confidence lasts when the funding dries up, much like that Sh1.5bn that mysteriously vanished from a Kenyan project not too long ago.

Sun King's Clinton Njeru chirps that companies need technicians who can actually do things, not just talk about them. Renewable energy, he claims, is transforming communities, bringing power and new economic activity. And of course, a trained technician can become an entrepreneur, creating more jobs. NITA Nairobi is even developing a national curriculum for solar technicians and making training accessible to people with disabilities, while NITA Athi River is busy teaching folks about electric vehicle batteries. It's all very rosy, but remember, this is foreign aid. Once the donors pack up their bags and count their savings, these "opportunities" might just disappear into thin air, leaving another group of eager young Kenyans with skills for a future that never quite arrived.
