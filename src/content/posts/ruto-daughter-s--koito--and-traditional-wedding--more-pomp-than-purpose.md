So, President Ruto's daughter, Charlene, is now officially off the market. Another traditional ceremony, another round of family portraits and carefully crafted statements of parental pride. This 'koito' business, apparently, is all about handing over the bride. Conveniently, it's happening at the family's sprawling 900-acre farm, a place with its own history of repossession and acquisition. Makes you wonder if the land itself is part of the dowry negotiations, doesn't it?

The President and First Lady waxed lyrical about love, blessings, and new beginnings. Standard parental fare, of course, but when you're in the State House, even a daughter's engagement turns into a PR exercise. They're "proud," they "rejoice," they'll "always stand beside" her. We get it, they're parents. But let's be real, this is also about projecting an image of a strong, united family, even as political winds shift. It’s a performance, just like any other in this circus, not unlike the musical chairs we see in the political arena.

Then comes the historical anthropologist, Mr. Weldon Oriop Kirui, trying to set the record straight about bride price. Six cows and a small token for the father. A tradition meant to make marriage accessible, not a multi-million shilling real estate deal. Yet, the whispers on social media about "millions" persist. It’s classic Kenya: tradition gets a modern facelift, especially when there’s a presidency involved. The community might frown, but when money talks, tradition often gets a quiet handshake and a nod.

Meanwhile, the groom, Isaya Yunge, sounds like a real go-getter. Queen Elizabeth II, Forbes 30 under 30, UNICEF ambassador - the CV is impressive. And Charlene herself isn't exactly a shrinking violet, with degrees from Daystar and a fancy Swiss hospitality school. It’s a union of two accomplished individuals, no doubt. But with such prominent families, one always has to ask: is this about genuine connection, or is it about consolidating influence, a strategic alliance dressed up in colourful traditional attire?

The elders warn against commercializing marriage, calling it "extortion" when parents try to recoup education costs. They emphasize that traditions "unite people and equalize irrespective of the educational or family background." A noble sentiment. But in a country where every transaction seems to have a price tag, and where political power often dictates opportunity, it's hard to believe that such deeply ingrained traditions remain untouched by the realities of wealth and status.

Ultimately, this 'koito' ceremony, like so many other public events involving high-profile families, is a mix of genuine sentiment, cultural practice, and calculated display. The love might be real, the traditions are being observed, but the underlying currents of status, influence, and perhaps even financial gain are always there, swirling beneath the surface like the dust kicked up by a passing presidential motorcade.
