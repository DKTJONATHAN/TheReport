David Rosenberg’s pivot toward renewable energy and critical infrastructure is less an endorsement of a green utopia and more a concession to a fragmented world. The drive for energy security in Europe and Asia, sparked by the volatile shadows of the Iran conflict, proves that the "green transition" is merely a rebranding of geopolitical paranoia. Countries are not fleeing carbon because they love the planet; they are fleeing because they can no longer trust their neighbors. The resilience of clean-energy ETFs is built on the cold reality that energy is the ultimate weapon of the twenty-first century.

The celebration of the cybersecurity trade is equally telling of our current trajectory. The rise of the Global X Cybersecurity ETF is a direct dividend of a deteriorating global security environment. In the so-called new AI age, the threat of cyberattacks has become a permanent tax on existence. We are pouring capital into digital walls because the underlying architecture of our interconnected world is fundamentally broken and prone to state-sponsored sabotage. Investors are essentially betting on the continued success of the world’s hackers and the desperation of their victims.

Even the apparent stability of the Canadian banking sector and the resilience of the "oil patch" reveal a deeper stagnation. Canadian banks outperforming their peers is a byproduct of a protected oligopoly and a regulatory environment that lowers capital buffers at the first sign of a chill. Meanwhile, the return to "low-cost" bitumen and thermal production by giants like Suncor and Cenovus confirms that the global addiction to fossil fuels remains unshaken. The supposed easing of Middle East tensions is a temporary lull in a long-term cycle of violence, providing just enough cover for the extraction industry to squeeze more profit from a warming world.

Ultimately, the market's current darlings-infrastructure for big tech, energy security for a fractured West, and digital fortresses-paints a picture of a world in defensive crouch. We are building faster and spending more just to maintain a status quo that is increasingly expensive and fundamentally fragile. The gains celebrated by analysts are not signs of a healthy global expansion, but rather the frantic billable hours of a civilization trying to outrun its own systemic failures.
