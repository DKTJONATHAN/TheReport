So, the Treasury is suddenly all about "transparency" and "uncovering secret owners." Apparently, this is the magic bullet to get Kenya off this dreaded "grey list" and somehow turn our economy around by 2027. They're making a big song and dance about folks who "ultimately own or control companies." It sounds like a grand plan, but let's be real, this is just the government trying to look like they're doing something about the money laundering and terrorism financing mess. We've been on this list since February 2024, and the FATF is still watching us like a hawk. It's a badge of shame we’ve been wearing for far too long.

They’re demanding names, KRA PINs, ID copies, addresses, occupations, phone numbers - the whole shebang. All this to stop corruption, tax evasion, and all the other fun stuff that keeps our economy humming, right? It’s rich coming from a government that seems to thrive on shady deals. Remember how they talk about economic surges and policy shifts? [/posts/kenya-s-economic-surge--record-trades--policy-shifts--and-a-bold-leap-into-the-future] It's all just noise when the fundamental issues remain unaddressed. This beneficial ownership thing sounds like a good idea on paper, but how many of these "secret owners" will just find another way to hide their cash?

The real estate sector, for starters, is already a hotbed of dodgy dealings, with a huge chunk of agencies flagged as medium to high risk. Law firms aren't far behind. So, you're telling me making a few more people fill out forms will magically clean up these messes? The Trust Administration Act, 2026, is also being thrown into the mix, demanding trusts spill their secrets too. Accessible information to the FRC and banks, they say. Great. More data for them to collect, more loopholes for others to exploit.

They claim Kenya has sorted out 19 out of 21 items on the FATF action plan and they’re optimistic about exiting the grey list by April next year. Optimistic? Or just desperate? The Public Benefit Organisations Regulatory Authority itself admitted that prosecuting money laundering and terrorism financing offences is still a weak point. This whole push is just another attempt to tick boxes for international bodies while the real rot continues underneath. It's a classic Kenyan move: create a lot of noise about a problem, implement a superficial solution, and then claim victory.

The Treasury’s commitment to "safeguarding the integrity of Kenya’s financial system" is a nice sentiment, but it rings hollow. This is about investor confidence and keeping those correspondent banking relationships intact, not about genuinely cleaning up our act for the benefit of the common mwananchi. We’ve seen this movie before. More rules, more declarations, and ultimately, the same old game played with slightly different rules. Let's see how long it takes for this "transparency" to become just another hurdle for ordinary businesses while the big players continue their clandestine operations.

The government also says it's introducing risk-based regulation for non-profit organisations and aligning its sanctions framework. It’s all part of the grand reform narrative. But the truth is, until they actually start prosecuting the big fish and making them pay, all these new laws and disclosures are just window dressing. We'll be watching, but don't hold your breath for a miraculous economic turnaround or a sudden end to illicit financial flows. This is Nairobi, after all.
