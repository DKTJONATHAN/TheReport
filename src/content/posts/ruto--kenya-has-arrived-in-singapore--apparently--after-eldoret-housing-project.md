RUTO: WE'VE ARRIVED IN SINGAPORE ALREADY, APPARENTLY

The President has declared Kenya has reached Singaporean living standards, thanks to a new affordable housing project in Eldoret. Apparently, shiny buildings with swimming pools and gyms mean we've skipped the "low-income nation" phase and landed squarely in "industrialised, high-income economy" territory. Never mind that half the country is still struggling for basic necessities, but hey, at least some people can now afford a bedsitter with a piped gas connection.

This Sh5.37 billion "Pioneer Affordable Housing Project" boasts 2,157 units and 104 shops, a kindergarten, clubhouse, and even a clinic. Ruto claims this is proof his vision for a modern, industrialised Kenya is already a reality. He even threw in a jab at his critics, accusing them of lacking vision and wanting Kenyans to remain in poverty. It's certainly convenient to label anyone questioning the practicality of these grand pronouncements as simply lacking ambition.

He's repeatedly held up Singapore as the gold standard, a nation that transformed from slums to high-rise living through "deliberate development planning." So, naturally, a few blocks of flats with fancy amenities in Eldoret must mean we're on par. It’s a classic move, promising a shiny future while ignoring the present struggles. It reminds me of when Ruto promised Kenya would no longer export raw minerals, just like that, a promise that feels just as tangible as arriving in Singapore overnight.

The critics, however, are pointing out the obvious: Singapore's success was built on decades of consistent planning, a robust manufacturing base, and stable economic policies. Kenya, meanwhile, is still wrestling with high taxes, crippling production costs, and a public debt that would make a loan shark blush. It's like comparing a carefully constructed skyscraper to a hastily assembled kiosk and expecting them to be the same.

Ruto’s "Singapore vision" also extends to transforming Kenya into a manufacturing powerhouse, moving away from a consumption-driven economy. He envisions special economic zones and industrial parks to process raw materials locally. Yet, the harsh reality on the ground often looks more like the chaos of an aviation strike, where essential services grind to a halt, than a smoothly functioning industrial hub.

So, while the President basks in the glow of his Eldoret "Singapore," the rest of us are left to navigate the familiar potholes of Kenyan reality. We're told we've arrived, but for many, the journey feels like it's just beginning, and the destination seems further away than ever.







President William Ruto has declared that Kenya is already making progress towards his vision of a modern, industrialised economy comparable to Singapore, citing the Sh5.37 billion Pioneer Affordable Housing Project in Eldoret as concrete proof. Speaking at the handover ceremony, Ruto proclaimed that this development, featuring amenities like a kindergarten, swimming pool, and gym, demonstrates the world-class living standards his administration aims to provide.

"I thought we were going to Singapore, but I see we have already arrived before we start," he enthused, challenging attendees to spot the difference between the Eldoret project and Singaporean standards, pointing to internet connectivity and piped cooking gas. This leap from a low-income nation to an industrialised, high-income economy, he suggested, is now within reach, echoing his frequent comparisons of Kenya's development trajectory to Singapore's transformation from slum housing to high-rise public apartments.

Beyond housing, Ruto's grand plan involves shifting Kenya from a consumption-driven economy to one that produces and exports more. His administration's promotion of special economic zones and manufacturing hubs aims to localise the processing of raw materials, a concept he's repeatedly linked to Singapore's economic blueprint. He argues that adopting Singapore's approach to production and investment is key to attaining higher living standards.

However, critics remain unconvinced, questioning the Kenya Kwanza administration's policies. They point to Singapore's foundation of consistent long-term planning, a strong manufacturing base, and predictable economic policies, contrasting it with Kenya's persistent challenges of high taxation, costly production, and mounting public debt. The institutional discipline and economic competitiveness that propelled Singapore are, they argue, still elusive for Kenya.

Ruto, unfazed by such skepticism, has dismissed his opponents, accusing them of lacking the vision to transform the country and of perpetuating cycles of poverty. Meanwhile, the reality for many Kenyans remains a daily struggle, with issues like the Northern Kenya's health crisis, marked by lack of water and broken facilities, highlighting the vast gulf between pronouncements and on-the-ground conditions.

The President's assertion that Kenya has "already arrived" in Singapore through developments like the Eldoret housing project serves as a stark reminder of the disconnect that can exist between political rhetoric and the lived experiences of citizens. While the amenities offered are undoubtedly a step up for some, the foundational economic and institutional reforms needed for genuine, widespread transformation remain a work in progress.
