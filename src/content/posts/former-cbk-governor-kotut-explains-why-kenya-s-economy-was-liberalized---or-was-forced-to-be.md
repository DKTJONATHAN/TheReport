So, Eric Kotut, former CBK Governor, finally 'explains' why Kenya threw open its economic doors back in the 80s. Turns out, it wasn't some grand vision, was it? Just a bad drought in '84, shrinking foreign cash, and the good old IMF and World Bank flexing their muscles. 'No choice,' he says. Funny how often that phrase pops up when powerful institutions want their way, especially when they're dangling financial aid.

These 'market-oriented reforms' stripped the government's role, handing it over to the private sector. Supposedly for 'efficiency' - as if efficiency always translates to a better life for ordinary Kenyans. They wanted industries open to 'external competition,' which usually means local businesses getting swallowed whole or struggling to keep up. It's the same old tune, really, played on a different instrument, always with the promise of a brighter future that somehow never quite shines for everyone.

But it wasn't just economics. Remember the multi-party push? Kotut conveniently mentions donors backing those demands by freezing aid, causing a 'major, major problem.' For the government, maybe. For the ordinary mwananchi, it was just another layer of chaos on top of an already struggling economy. We were caught between a rock and a hard place, and the donors knew it, forcing political changes alongside economic ones.

To fix this mess, the CBK cooked up some 'market-based reforms' like foreign currency retention accounts, effectively creating a 'parallel market.' Sounds a bit like finding a loophole to keep the ship from sinking, rather than fixing the leaks, doesn't it? And now, decades later, these 'reforms' are still shaping everything. They laid the foundation, alright. But for whose benefit, really? When you look at [the current state of affairs, sometimes it feels like the same old story impacting your grocery bill](/posts/the-middle-east-meat-grinder-meets-your-grocery-bill), just with a different governor explaining it away.
