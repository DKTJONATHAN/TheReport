Sh1.5bn Ruaraka Land Scam: EACC's Latest Court Drama is Just Another Nairobi Dance

So, the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission (EACC) is now running to the Supreme Court to tell a couple of companies linked to the late Francis Mburu that their appeal is a waste of time. Apparently, these companies, Huelands Limited and Afrison Export Import Limited, are trying to squeeze a constitutional argument out of a simple case of whether they should have been paid Sh1.5 billion for land that schools are sitting on. It’s the same old song and dance in this city, where people try to dress up greed in fancy legal robes.

These companies, bless their greedy little hearts, want the Supreme Court to re-examine facts that the lower courts, including the Court of Appeal, have already decided on. The EACC, in its infinite wisdom, is pointing out that this is not the job of the apex court. They’re arguing that Mburu's companies are just trying to turn a factual dispute - whether they voluntarily surrendered the land in the 80s for public use - into a constitutional crisis. It’s like a child who didn't get a toy trying to claim it's a violation of their human rights.

The core of the issue boils down to this: did Mburu's firms hand over the 13.5 acres for public schools voluntarily when they were getting approvals for their land subdivision back in the day, or is this some elaborate land grab disguised as a legal battle? The companies are yapping about a lack of a "formally registered instrument of surrender," claiming that treating planning approval as a surrender is just a fancy way of saying compulsory acquisition without a penny. They're even throwing around Article 40(3) of the Constitution, crying foul about arbitrary deprivation of property. Honestly, the melodrama. It reminds me of the endless construction dramas and the billions later, like the [Upper Hill-Kenyatta Avenue Viaduct Inches to 60% Completion, Billions Later](/posts/upper-hill-kenyatta-avenue-viaduct-inches-to-60--completion--billions-later) saga.

The EACC, meanwhile, is stating that these constitutional arguments were never the foundation of the case before the lower courts. The real concern was always about the facts: did these companies actually give up the land as a condition for approving their subdivision scheme in 1982? The lower courts, after looking at the paperwork and listening to all the noise, said yes, they did. The Court of Appeal even pointed to correspondence from 1984 showing the land was designated for public use, and the schools popped up soon after, with no one complaining for decades.

Now these companies are trying to have their cake and eat it too, demanding compensation and also trying to stop the EACC from clawing back the Sh1.5 billion already paid. They’re worried that if the EACC gets its way before their Supreme Court appeal is heard, their whole case will become a pointless exercise. It’s a classic Nairobi move: drag everything out, hope for a miracle, and if all else fails, throw a constitutional tantrum. This is just another chapter in the never-ending Ruaraka land saga, a testament to how easily public funds can vanish into thin air.

So, here we are again, watching powerful people try to twist the law to their advantage while the taxpayer is left holding the bag. The EACC wants to recover money paid for land the government already owned, and the companies are fighting tooth and nail to keep it. It’s a predictable script in this city, and unless something drastic changes, we’ll be reading about the next Sh1.5 billion scandal in no time.
