Honestly, these constant uniform changes for the police are just a predictable Nairobi drama. Every regime thinks a fresh lick of paint on the force is some grand reform. Meanwhile, the Constitution is celebrating sixteen years and what do we have? A state organ that’s been bent and twisted to serve whoever's got the loudest voice or the fattest wallet. They want us to believe that after nearly two decades of the National Police Service and its commission, the biggest issue is the colour of their shirts. It's all about the tenders, the kickbacks, lining someone's pockets while the real rot festers.

The Kenyan police are still stuck in that colonial skin, a thick layer of brutality, inhumanity, and corruption that no amount of fancy new gear can hide. Even with IPOA supposedly watching, their record since 2010 is a dark stain on our constitutional promise. From the post-election violence to the documented abuses in informal settlements and the sheer madness of the 2024 Finance Bill protests, the script is always the same: excessive force, disappearing citizens, and lives lost. They treated young Kenyans exercising their rights like common criminals. It's a sad reminder of how little has changed, not unlike the ongoing diplomatic masquerades we sometimes see.

The killing of Albert Ojwang in police custody last year just hammered home that chilling question: who guards the guards? When someone is in their hands, effectively silenced, who is looking out for them? This isn't about a prettier uniform; it's about a complete skin change, starting with the very culture of policing. Officers need to see citizens not as obstacles or enemies, but as the reason they exist. Human rights should be their operating system, not a suggestion they can conveniently ignore.

And then there's accountability. IPOA needs teeth, not just a pretty badge. Commanders must own what happens on their watch. This "blue code of silence," this professional solidarity that shields wrongdoing, is a cancer. When officers refuse to speak up against fellow officers, it cripples justice. We need a system where loyalty to the Constitution trumps loyalty to a colleague, where officers are protected for doing the right thing, not punished. Until that instinct to uphold the Constitution is ingrained, changing the uniform is just putting a band-aid on a gaping wound.
