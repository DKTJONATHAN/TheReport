Ruto: Africa's Big Talk on Stopping Borrowing and Using Own Resources, But Will Anyone Actually Listen?

President Ruto's grand pronouncements about Africa ditching foreign loans and becoming an "equal partner" sound good on paper, especially when delivered on international stages. He's promising a revolution in Kenya, too, where raw materials like Magadi soda will be processed locally, creating glass and chemical factories, and jobs for our youth. It’s the same old song, sung with a new tune.

The President declared at the G7 and now back home in Kajiado that Kenya will no longer export raw materials. "Our raw materials will no longer be exported," he stated firmly. Instead, investors will be expected to "invest" and "add value," not just "extract." It's a noble idea, this shifting from a subservient relationship to one of sovereign equality. But let's be honest, how many times have we heard this before? It’s like a broken record player that keeps skipping to the same optimistic track, even when the vinyl is clearly scratched.

He’s aiming for a complete overhaul of how Africa engages with global partners, moving away from the dependency of borrowing to the empowerment of self-generated wealth. This vision includes processing minerals right here in Kenya, turning what used to be shipped out in its basic form into finished goods. The dream is to build industries, hire our own, and use Kenyan talent. Sounds like a plan, if you ignore the decades of similar promises that have evaporated like mist on a hot Nairobi morning.

The administration's stance is clear: no more "business-as-usual." Kenya, under his leadership, will implement this policy, particularly concerning mineral wealth. The hope is to see glass factories and chemical plants spring up, employing young Kenyans. This is the official position, a push to transform Africa's natural bounty into industrial opportunities. But as history has shown us, especially when it comes to our resources, the devil is always in the details, and often, the details involve more exploitation, not less. Remember when we were promised so much regarding our agricultural sector, only for farmers to be left scrambling? The call for a stronger food value chain often gets drowned out by the noise of bigger, shinier pronouncements.

Ruto wants investors, yes, but their role must transform. From mere extractors, they need to become investors who contribute to value addition within Kenya. This is the administration's policy, a direct move to leverage our own resources for our own industrial growth. It’s a bold declaration, one that many would love to see come to fruition. Yet, the cynic in me whispers about the perennial challenge of translating grand visions into tangible reality on the ground, especially when powerful global interests are involved.







President William Ruto's pronouncements about Africa finally ditching its reliance on foreign loans and raw material exports sound like music to some ears. He’s pushing for a continental shift, a move towards genuine equality in global partnerships, and a focus on leveraging indigenous resources. Back home in Kenya, the plan is to process minerals locally, transforming what was once exported in its raw state into finished products, creating jobs and wealth within the country. It's the same old rhetoric, repackaged with a bit more swagger.

The President made his position clear on the international stage and is now reiterating it locally, stating that Africa will no longer continue with a "business-as-usual mentality." This means an end to the era of dependency, with engagements with global partners shifting from borrowing to mobilising Africa's own resources. The aim is to work as equals, with international partners guaranteeing our progress as we become self-sufficient and eliminate the burden of debt. It’s a lofty goal, one that many have dreamt of, but the path to achieving it is often littered with broken promises and vested interests.

Ruto emphasized that African countries must engage other nations based on sovereign equality, not dependency. This means no more subservient relationships, but a pursuit of genuine equality where every nation is treated as an equal. He also reiterated his call to end the export of raw materials, insisting that Africa's resources will no longer be simply extracted and shipped out. The vision is for value addition to happen within the continent, creating industries and employment for the local population.

Kenya, under his leadership, will implement this policy, especially regarding minerals like those found in Magadi. The President declared, "I am in charge of Kenya. So in Kenya, we are going to do it." The idea is to establish industries such as glass and chemical manufacturing plants locally, thereby hiring Kenyan youth and utilizing Kenyan talent. Investors are still welcome, but their role must pivot from mere resource extraction to investing in and adding value to these resources within Kenya.

This policy represents the official stance of his administration, a broader effort to transform Africa’s natural wealth into tangible industrial opportunities. It’s a declaration that Kenya, under his presidency, will no longer export raw materials but will ensure all resources are added value within the country. While the ambition is commendable, the realist in me has seen too many similar pronouncements fade into obscurity, failing to overcome the entrenched systems that often perpetuate exploitation. Remember the ongoing struggles in the agricultural sector, where farmers and vets constantly call for stronger value chains, yet progress often feels painfully slow? This new push for industrialization faces similar, if not greater, hurdles.

The President wants investors to invest, not just extract. This is the core of his administration's message, a signal of intent to foster industrial growth and economic self-reliance. It’s a commendable goal, one that, if realized, could significantly alter the economic landscape of Kenya and Africa. However, history has a way of repeating itself, and it remains to be seen whether this grand vision will translate into lasting, impactful change or simply become another chapter in the long saga of grand pronouncements that never quite materialize.
