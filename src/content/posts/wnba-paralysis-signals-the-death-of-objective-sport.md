The cynicism is palpable when looking at the league's history. In 2021, the WNBA was more than happy to use its platform to celebrate Andraya Yearwood and Terry Miller, two males who dominated girls' track in Connecticut. Back then, it was easy to signal virtue because it was someone else's daughter losing the medal. Now that the threat has reached the professional level, the "Woman Crush Wednesday" enthusiasm has evaporated, replaced by task forces and bureaucratic stalling.

Internationally, this hesitation paints the WNBA as an ideological outlier. While global governing bodies for swimming and track have begun reinstating sex-based protections to maintain the integrity of female competition, the WNBA is doubling down on "inclusivity" models that effectively erase the very category they were founded to showcase. If the definition of "woman" is plastic enough to include anyone, the "W" in WNBA becomes nothing more than a marketing gimmick.

The league's recent focus on "hate and vitriol" during stakeholder meetings is a classic diversionary tactic. By framing the debate as a matter of online bullying, they can ignore the material reality of the female athletes who have already been displaced. The testimonies of runners like Selina Soule and Chelsea Mitchell - who lost titles and futures to male competitors - are treated as inconveniences rather than warnings.

This is the end result of a league that prioritizes being an activist organization over being a sports product. By partnering with groups like Athlete Ally to "educate" their players on trans inclusivity, they have built an ideological cage they cannot escape without offending their primary donors. The reality is that professional sports depend on the integrity of the category; without it, the WNBA is just a slow-motion car crash of its own design.
