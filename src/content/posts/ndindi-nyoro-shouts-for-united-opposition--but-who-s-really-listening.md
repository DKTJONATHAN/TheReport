So, Ndindi Nyoro, the man from Kiharu, is out in the Rift Valley telling the opposition to finally get their act together and field one presidential candidate for 2027. Apparently, he thinks this will miraculously stop William Ruto from winning. He sounds like he's trying to convince himself as much as anyone else. "We will sit down and agree on one candidate," he declared, as if agreement is just a walk in the park for these guys.

And as if that wasn't enough drama, Nyoro's also got his knickers in a twist about that Sh60 billion Dangote oil refinery in Lamu. He’s demanding transparency, asking Ruto to spill the beans on who owns what. He’s got this paranoid idea that Ruto demands a cut of every business deal. Honestly, the way these politicians carry on, you'd think they invented shady deals. It makes you wonder if any real business can happen here without the constant circus.

Then there's the obligatory attack on the incumbent. Nyoro is telling President Ruto to stop "attacking" former President Uhuru Kenyatta. He’s lecturing Ruto on respecting former heads of state, conveniently forgetting that nobody asked Uhuru to become a statesman when he was deputy. It's all about setting "precedents" now, isn't it? The hypocrisy is thicker than the illicit alcohol Nyoro is also complaining about.

Speaking of illicit alcohol, Nyoro is suddenly the champion of Kenyan youth and families. He’s accusing the government of just making promises while young people get destroyed by cheap booze. He’s pointing fingers at the licensing system, blaming both county and national governments for the mess. His brilliant idea? NACADA should take over. Because clearly, another layer of bureaucracy is exactly what Kenya needs to solve a problem that’s been brewing for years, much like the issues surrounding women’s empowerment in agriculture.

This whole Rift Valley tour by the People's Party of Kenya is just another early bird special, trying to polish their image and get some mileage before the real election circus begins. It’s the same old song and dance: promises, accusations, and a whole lot of noise.

Nyoro's call for unity is a tired refrain, and his concerns about the refinery and alcohol are valid points buried under a mountain of political posturing. Let's see how far this "opposition unity" actually goes when the real jockeying for power begins.
