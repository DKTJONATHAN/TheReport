STATE UNVEILS POLICY TO BOOST ACCESS TO INFORMATION, END GOVT SECRECY - OR SO THEY SAY

The government, in its infinite wisdom and just in time for the next election cycle, has decided to grace us with a new Access to Information Policy. Apparently, the 2016 Act was just collecting dust. Now, they promise to be more transparent, to publish information routinely, and to actually respond when you ask for something. It’s a real page turner, this one.

According to the Gazette notice - because who reads those anyway? - the Ministry of Information and CAJ have discovered that public institutions suffer from “poor records management,” “bureaucratic delays,” and a charming “culture of secrecy.” You don’t say! It’s almost as if asking for documents has always been an uphill battle against a wall of indifference and strategically misplaced files. Now, they’re promising clear procedures, timely decisions, and written reasons for denial. We’ll see about that.

They’re even talking about making information available at no cost, save for the "reasonable charges" for reproduction. Which, in Kenyan terms, usually means "whatever they feel like charging you on the spot." And don’t forget the promises to protect whistleblowers. Remember when that woman was detained after a doctor collapsed in Kitengela? Let’s hope this policy actually means something for people who try to expose wrongdoing, and isn't just another document to be conveniently ignored.

This whole song and dance about digitisation and accessibility sounds suspiciously like the livestock tagging saga. Remember when Kenya digitally tagged livestock, because apparently your cow needs a passport now? Just another expensive initiative that sounds good on paper but leaves the average Kenyan asking, "So, what does this *actually* do for me?" At least county governments are supposed to get their act together with information focal points. Let's just hope these focal points aren't just another desk manned by someone who’s never heard of the policy.

The government claims this will strengthen public participation and make it easier for citizens to obtain information. It’s a noble sentiment, a comforting fairy tale they're selling. But after years of navigating the labyrinthine corridors of bureaucracy, where information is guarded like a state secret and sovereignty is often for sale, one can't help but feel a healthy dose of cynicism. Let's wait and see if this policy actually ends secrecy or just adds another layer to the charade.
