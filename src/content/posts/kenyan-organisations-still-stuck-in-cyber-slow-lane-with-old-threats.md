KENYA'S ORGANISATIONS STILL CHASING GHOSTS ON CYBER THREATS

Forget the fancy AI bogeyman for a moment. Kenyan businesses are getting hacked with the same tired tricks they've been warned about for years, mostly because nobody bothers with the basics. It's the digital equivalent of leaving your door unlocked and then complaining when someone walks in.

Apparently, the clever folks at ESET have been looking at a mountain of data and what they found is less "future shock" and more "déjà vu all over again." Attackers are still raking in the loot with good old-fashioned malicious emails, dodgy links, and software that should have been updated before some of you finished university. They're calling it "familiar techniques," but frankly, it’s just laziness, plain and simple. We're seeing a massive surge in QR code phishing, or "quishing," with over a 145% jump. People are scanning these things faster than they're checking their mobile money balances, even though security controls on their phones are often weaker than a politician's promise.

And the software? Don't even get me started. ESET flagged a vulnerability from *2017* - yes, you read that right, 2017 - that's still being exploited left, right, and centre. This isn't some bleeding-edge AI attack; this is exploiting a hole that's been gaping for nearly a decade. It’s like complaining about the rain while standing in a leaky roof that you’ve been meaning to fix since the last El Niño. Exposed remote desktop services are another classic, with some systems running operating systems so old they probably predate the internet as we know it. It’s an open invitation, a digital welcome mat for anyone with a bit of know-how. We’ve seen similar scandals where basic oversight costs millions, and this cyber nonsense is no different. For instance, the [Billion Shilling Bad Fuel Scandal Gets Another Twist](/posts/billion-shilling-bad-fuel-scandal-gets-another-twist) shows how easily basic checks can be bypassed with devastating financial consequences.

Then there's the malware. They’re seeing a significant rise in stuff like Aotera, which is basically a digital mule carrying other nasty payloads like AgentTesla and Formbook. These aren't new, homegrown Kenyan threats; they’re international pests that have found fertile ground here because our defenses are so porous. It’s a whole interconnected cybercrime ecosystem, and we’re basically just providing the entry point.

The real kicker? Some organisations are panicking about ransomware when it's not even ransomware. They're running around like headless chickens, spending money and disrupting operations based on assumptions. It just goes to show how little understanding there is of the actual threats. The experts are practically begging them to "do the basics" - patch systems, use strong passwords, train employees. It’s not rocket science, but for many Kenyan organisations, it might as well be.

So while the tech world obsesses over AI’s next move, the real danger for Kenya is far more mundane. It's the gaping holes in basic security that have been ignored for years. The challenge isn't about predicting the future of cybercrime; it's about finally fixing the present, which, frankly, is already broken.
