And just like that, gone. For years, the ghostwriting racket was a lifeline for Kenyan graduates, churning out essays for oblivious Western students. Anonymous accounts, shadowy brokers - it wasn't pretty, but it paid the rent. Then ChatGPT waltzed in, the ultimate cheapskate buyer’s dream, and suddenly the whole flimsy structure collapsed faster than a poorly written dissertation.

Don't expect any tears from me. This wasn't some noble profession. It was contract cheating, plain and simple, a stain on academia. But let's be brutally honest: these jobs existed because Kenya has a surplus of English-speaking graduates and a severe shortage of actual, well-paying jobs. The government was pushing online work, and this was the "opportunity" they offered. Now, with AI churning out passable prose in seconds, that meager income - the $900 to $1,200 some were pulling in - is just evaporating. That’s not a dip; that’s people’s lives crumbling.

The numbers don't lie. Loads of online freelancers in Kenya, upwards of 70% last year, were stuck in writing and translation. AI didn't create the "contract cheating" market; it just exploited its inherent weakness. It’s the same story for so many "digital labor" jobs - transcription, basic translation, simple web copy. If the task is just spitting out generic text, AI will always win on price. We're seeing this play out in real time, just like we saw how even the seemingly complex work of writing about a fictional condition, bixonimania, could be easily faked by AI, highlighting the need for verification in fields like health AI.

Sure, there are those like Teresios Bundi who managed to pivot from writing essays to advising on the digital economy. That’s the optimistic spin, the "better version." But that’s a rare escape hatch. The reality for most is that the bottom rung of the digital ladder, the one that was supposed to lift them up, has been kicked away. Kenya’s latest push to attract BPO and IT-enabled services is all well and good, but if it's built on the same foundation of cheap, replaceable tasks, it's doomed to repeat the same mistake. This isn't about managing a distant risk; it's about a force already inside the labor market.

The lesson here is stark: you can't build a durable digital strategy on work that's only valued because it's cheap. The essay writing industry was a miniature tragedy, showing how easily loyalty and human effort can be discarded when a machine can do it faster and cheaper. And it’s not just essays. Think about any task that’s essentially converting generic text into other generic text. AI is coming for it, and the rates will plummet. It's a race to the bottom, and we're already losing. Even something as seemingly straightforward as the PwC employee who was once touted as one of finance's 'finest boys' can highlight the inherent volatility.

The path forward, if there even is one that doesn't involve more empty promises, is narrower and much, much harder. It means focusing on human oversight of AI, specialized data work, quality assurance that requires actual judgment, and training programs that move people into technical and supervisory roles. Kenya’s outsourcing push can still work, but only if it acknowledges that AI is already here and demands a higher value for human contribution. Otherwise, it's just repackaging the same old exploitation with fancier branding.
