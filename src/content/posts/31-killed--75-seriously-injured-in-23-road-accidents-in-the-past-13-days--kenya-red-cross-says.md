31 KILLED, 75 HOSPITALIZED IN 13 DAYS OF SPEEDING MADNESS, RED CROSS SAYS

Another grim reminder that Nairobi roads are a death trap, courtesy of careless drivers and a system that clearly couldn't care less. The Red Cross is wringing its hands, begging people to slow down. As if that's ever worked.

Here we go again. Another "urgent plea" from the Kenya Red Cross, this time about the shocking number of people getting mowed down on our roads. Thirty-one dead, 75 seriously injured in just thirteen days of August. Numbers that should make your stomach churn, but for most of us, it's just another Tuesday. Speeding, overtaking like lunatics, and drivers falling asleep at the wheel - the usual suspects. They're even suggesting 30 km/hr limits around schools and markets. Good luck with that.

The report throws in a few gory details to make sure we don't forget. A Sienta veering off the Embu-Meru Highway, parts scattered like a cheap jigsaw puzzle. Apparently, they were headed to a burial. How ironic. Then there's the Kijabe pile-up, a "mass casualty event." Just another day in paradise. And let's not forget the 16-year-old terror in Parklands, plowing through pedestrians like they were speed bumps. One of them was a 48-year-old man, gone. Just like that. The system that lets teenagers behind the wheel of powerful cars without a second thought is just as culpable. It's like we're living in a real-life episode of "Mandago's Sh1.1 Billion Loan Saga: 'Eat Now, Pay Never' For Taxpayers," but instead of money, it's lives being squandered.

They say they want to protect "vulnerable road users." Who are these mysterious vulnerable beings? The elderly? Children? Or just anyone unlucky enough to be on the road when some idiot decides to reenact a Fast and Furious movie? The truth is, nobody cares enough to enforce anything. These pleas from the Red Cross are just noise in the deafening roar of our traffic. We're too busy trying to make a living, trying to get from A to B, often in vehicles held together by prayer and duct tape, to worry about someone else's delicate sensibilities on the road.

So, the Red Cross is urging motorists to be careful. To reduce their speed. To exercise greater care. It's a nice thought, a noble sentiment. But in Nairobi, "care" is a luxury most people can't afford, and "speed" is often the only way to keep up with the chaos. We'll probably see more of these reports, more tragic statistics, and more impassioned pleas. And then, we'll all just carry on, because that's what we do in this city. We endure.
